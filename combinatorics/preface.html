Preface

This is a continuing set of notes for our complete course in combinatorics and probability. It is not meant to be a collection of unrelated classroom sheets. I shall keep enlarging the completed lectures as our discussions and problem sets develop, while preserving the notation, diagrams, cross-references, exercise sets, and solution style already established here. The aim is that the notes should gradually become a self-contained book that we can return to throughout the year.

The course has three parts. Part I is the Level 1 foundation: here we build the language of careful counting. Part II is the Level 2 continuation: here the same counting problems are approached through recurrence relations, state diagrams, two-parameter arrays, and generating functions. Part III begins probability theory. Probability does not start from nowhere; it asks us to take the sample spaces that combinatorics taught us to construct and then assign numerical weights to events inside them.

For a Class 12 student, probability is often presented as one more chapter of formulas. I want the experience here to be completely different. Probability is the first place in school mathematics where we learn that uncertainty does not mean the absence of structure. We may be unable to predict the next toss, the next random move, or the next data point, and yet be able to predict a long-run proportion, an average, or even a visible geometric pattern with astonishing reliability. This is the mathematical language behind data, statistics, randomized algorithms, scientific experiments, risk, and every situation in which we must make a rational decision before the future is known.

In Part III I shall therefore combine proof, history, counting, simulation, and visual experiments. The Barnsley fern “chaos game” is included not as decoration but as a central motivational experiment: four simple random rules, repeated many times, create a stable fern. It allows us to see with our own eyes that microscopic unpredictability and macroscopic order can coexist.

I do not want you to memorise a list of formulas. I want us to develop a way of thinking. Before applying a formula, I want you to pause and ask:

  • What exactly is the object or outcome being counted?

  • Are we making one choice, or completing several successive choices?

  • Should the problem be divided into complete, non-overlapping cases?

  • Does order matter, and is repetition allowed?

  • Can the object be matched bijectively with something simpler?

  • Can a size-nn object be reduced to one or more smaller objects?

  • In a probability problem, what is one complete elementary outcome, and why are the outcomes being counted equally likely?

A recurring theme of the book is that one mathematical object can be viewed in several ways. A subset may be encoded by a binary word; a path may be encoded by a sequence of steps; a distribution may be encoded by stars and bars or by a generating function; and a probability event is a subset of a sample space. Whenever we use a bijection, a recurrence, or a generating function, I shall explain what information is being preserved and why the construction works in both directions.

The complete course is organised into thirteen lectures. New examples may enlarge a lecture, but they will not create extra lecture numbers or shift the intended finishing point. The planned progression is the following.

Part I: Level 1 Foundations

LecturePrincipal topics
1Factorials; the Addition and Multiplication Principles; sequences; divisor counting; permutations; filling positions; digit restrictions; place-value frequency methods.
2Repetition and identical objects; one-to-one maps; combinations as selection without order; case division; circular arrangements; blocks, gaps, necklaces, garlands, and pairings.

5Distribution of objects; labelled and unlabelled boxes; lower and upper bounds; coefficient extraction; occupancy models; Stirling and Bell numbers; integer partitions; the first generating-function viewpoint.
6The basic and generalised Pigeonhole Principles; geometric pigeonholes; residues and parity; partial sums; equal subset sums; digit constructions; monotone subsequences; extremal and Ramsey-type applications.

Part II: Level 2 Advanced Combinatorics

LecturePrincipal topics
7Counting by recurrence; first-step and last-step decompositions; restricted strings, subsets, compositions, tilings, derangements, Catalan recurrences, auxiliary states, and olympiad counting examples.
8Reading recurrences from state graphs; symmetry and elimination of auxiliary states; permutation cycles; Stirling numbers of both kinds; two-parameter recurrences; Eulerian numbers; Tower of Hanoi; circular colourings; determinant and contest recurrences; basic methods for solving recurrences.
9Ordinary generating functions as a counting language; formal power series; Cauchy products; coefficient extraction; bounded selections and distributions; solving recurrences; Catalan generating functions; integer partitions, Ferrers diagrams, and an introduction to exponential generating functions.

Part III: Probability Theory

LecturePrincipal topics
10Historical origins of probability; random experiments; outcomes and sample spaces; events and event operations; equally likely outcomes; classical probability; odds in favour and against; probability axioms; addition and complement formulas; independence; counting-based probability models; statistical regularity; the Barnsley fern chaos game; and a computational laboratory in Python, R, Shiny, and the web browser.
11Conditional probability; restricted sample spaces; the multiplication rule; independence revisited; the law of total probability; Bayes' theorem; information-revealing games; Bernoulli trials and the binomial distribution; mean, variance, and repeated-trial applications.

The present edition contains all thirteen lectures. The last two lectures bring the counting and probability viewpoints together through recursive random processes, olympiad models, and geometric probability. Their examples and practice collections provide material for both classroom discussion and subsequent independent reading.

I have written the exposition in the order in which I would explain it in class: an idea is motivated first, then stated precisely, and then used in worked examples. Every completed lecture ends with its own practice problems, so that the work assigned after a lecture remains physically attached to that lecture. Open the solution below each practice problem to read the full argument. There I record not only the answer, but the model, the case division, the bijection, the recurrence, or the probability calculation that makes the answer valid.

How to Read These Notes

Do not read the examples passively. Before opening a solution, decide what the objects are, what information a complete outcome must contain, whether order matters, and whether the cases overlap. In a recurrence problem, identify exactly what is removed from the object. In a generating-function problem, explain what every factor records. In a probability problem, justify the sample space before dividing favourable cases by total cases. After reading a proof, close the book and try to reconstruct it in your own words, as though you were explaining it to me at the board.

These notes are meant for classroom learning, revision, and serious problem solving. I encourage you to draw your own diagrams, write every missing step, and test a general statement on small examples. The real aim is not to make counting and probability look formulaic; it is to learn how to see the structure that makes a calculation possible.
