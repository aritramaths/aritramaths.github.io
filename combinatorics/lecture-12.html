Topics Covered in Lecture 12

In this lecture we shall keep probability close to the combinatorics that produced it. We begin with exact and cumulative binomial probabilities, the most likely number of successes, and conditioning on a known total. We then develop one of the most useful problem-solving ideas in elementary probability: look at the first move, or the last move, and write a recurrence.\boxed{\text{look at the first move, or the last move, and write a recurrence}.} This leads naturally to random walks, stopping games, races, forbidden patterns, and state diagrams. The lecture ends by revisiting several AMC and AIME recurrence problems from Lecture 7 and turning their counts into genuine probability calculations.

💡 Motivation

A recurrence relation in probability is not an extra formula placed on top of a random experiment. It records how the experiment can begin. The coefficients are probabilities, the smaller terms describe the remaining states, and the boundary values describe what happens when the game has already ended.

The first-step principle for probability

In a counting recurrence, we split a family of objects into disjoint classes. In a probability recurrence, we split the possible first moves into disjoint cases and then continue from the state reached by that move.

Theorem 12.1

First-step recurrence for a finite probability process

Suppose a random process is currently in a nonterminal state ss. From ss, its next state is one of t1,t2,…,tmt_1,t_2,\ldots,t_m, with probabilities p1,p2,…,pm,p1+⋯+pm=1.p_1,p_2,\ldots,p_m, \qquad p_1+\cdots+p_m=1. Let u(s)u(s) be the probability that a specified target event eventually occurs when the process starts from ss. Then u(s)=p1u(t1)+p2u(t2)+⋯+pmu(tm).\boxed{u(s)=p_1u(t_1)+p_2u(t_2)+\cdots+p_mu(t_m).} For a terminal success state, u=1u=1; for a terminal failure state, u=0u=0.

Proof.

Let EE be the event that the target is eventually reached, and let FjF_j be the event that the first move carries the process from ss to tjt_j. The events F1,…,FmF_1,\ldots,F_m are mutually exclusive and exhaustive. Therefore the law of total probability gives ℙ(E)=∑j=1mℙ(Fj)ℙ(E∣Fj).\mathbb{P}(E)=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\mathbb{P}(F_j)\mathbb{P}(E\mid F_j). Now ℙ(Fj)=pj\mathbb{P}(F_j)=p_j, while after the first move has led to tjt_j, the remaining chance of eventual success is exactly u(tj)u(t_j). Hence u(s)=∑j=1mpju(tj).u(s)=\sum_{j=1}^{m}p_ju(t_j). The boundary values are immediate: success has probability 11 once it has already occurred, and probability 00 once the process has ended in failure.

⚠ Common mistake

Do not write a recurrence until the state has remembered enough information. If the future depends on the last symbol, current score, or current position, then that information must be included in the state. A recurrence with too little memory is usually false.

Example 12.1

Landing exactly on a marked square

A token starts at 00. At every move it advances either one square or two squares, with probability 1/21/2 each, independently of all previous moves. Let unu_n be the probability that the token ever lands exactly on square nn. Find a recurrence and a closed expression for unu_n.

Pause and predict

Before reading the solution, decide what the final move into square nn could have been. There are only two possibilities.

Solution.
Tools to recall.

Theorem 12.1 (first-step recurrence); Theorem 8.4 (distinct characteristic roots)

The token can arrive at nn only from n−1n-1 by a one-step move, or from n−2n-2 by a two-step move. Each of those final moves has probability 1/21/2. Therefore un=12un−1+12un−2(n≥1).\boxed{u_n=\frac12u_{n-1}+\frac12u_{n-2}\qquad(n\ge1).} It is convenient to use the boundary values u0=1,u−1=0.u_0=1, \qquad u_{-1}=0. The first value is clear because the token has already landed on square 00; the second records that the token cannot arrive at 00 from a fictitious square −1-1. In particular, u1=12u0+12u−1=12.u_1=\frac12u_0+\frac12u_{-1}=\frac12.

To solve the recurrence, try un=rnu_n=r^n. We obtain r2=12r+12,r^2=\frac12r+\frac12, or 2r2−r−1=0=(2r+1)(r−1).2r^2-r-1=0=(2r+1)(r-1). Thus the roots are r=1,r=−12.r=1, \qquad r=-\frac12. Hence un=A+B(−12)n.u_n=A+B\left(-\frac12\right)^n. Using u0=1u_0=1 and u1=1/2u_1=1/2, A+B=1,A−12B=12.A+B=1, \qquad A-\frac12B=\frac12. Subtracting the second equation from the first gives 32B=12,B=13,\frac32B=\frac12, \qquad B=\frac13, and therefore A=2/3A=2/3. Consequently, un=23+13(−12)n.\boxed{ u_n=\frac23+\frac13\left(-\frac12\right)^n.}

The first few values are 1,12,34,58,1116,….1,\ \frac12,\ \frac34,\ \frac58,\ \frac{11}{16},\ldots. The alternating correction becomes smaller and smaller, so the probability approaches 2/32/3. This limit is not obvious from any one path; it becomes visible only after we solve the recurrence.

Binomial probabilities: count first, then divide

Let X∼Bin(n,p)X\sim\operatorname{Bin}(n,p), and put q=1−pq=1-p. Lecture 11 proved ℙ(X=r)=(nr)prqn−r.\mathbb{P}(X=r)=\binom nrp^rq^{n-r}. I want us to keep reading the four factors rather than treating the expression as a single memorised block: (nr)⏟choose the successful positionspr⏟probability of those successesqn−r⏟probability of the remaining failures.\underbrace{\binom nr}_{\text{choose the successful positions}} \underbrace{p^r}_{\text{probability of those successes}} \underbrace{q^{n-r}}_{\text{probability of the remaining failures}}.

Example 12.2

At least three heads

Ten fair coins are tossed independently. Find the probability of at least three heads.

Solution.
Tools to recall.

Theorem 10.2 (complement formula); Theorem 11.4 (binomial probabilities)

Directly adding the probabilities of 3,4,…,103,4,\ldots,10 heads would require eight terms. The complement has only three counts: X=0,1,2.X=0,1,2. Therefore ℙ(X≥3)=1−ℙ(X≤2)=1−(100)+(101)+(102)210=1−1+10+451024=1−561024=121128.\begin{aligned} \mathbb{P}(X\ge3) &=1-\mathbb{P}(X\le2)\\ &=1-\frac{\binom{10}{0}+\binom{10}{1}+\binom{10}{2}}{2^{10}}\\ &=1-\frac{1+10+45}{1024}\\ &=1-\frac{56}{1024}\\ &=\boxed{\frac{121}{128}}. \end{aligned} The counts X=0,1,2X=0,1,2 are disjoint, so their probabilities may be added without any overlap correction.

The most likely number of successes

Write br=(nr)prqn−r.b_r=\binom nrp^rq^{n-r}. Instead of evaluating every probability separately, compare neighbouring terms: br+1br=n−rr+1pq.\frac{b_{r+1}}{b_r} = \frac{n-r}{r+1}\frac pq. Thus br+1≥brb_{r+1}\ge b_r exactly when r+1≤(n+1)p.r+1\le(n+1)p. This tells us where the probability table stops increasing and begins decreasing.

Theorem 12.2

Mode of a binomial distribution

Let X∼Bin(n,p)X\sim\operatorname{Bin}(n,p), with 0<p<10<p<1.

  • If (n+1)p(n+1)p is not an integer, the unique most likely value is ⌊(n+1)p⌋.\boxed{\lfloor(n+1)p\rfloor.}

  • If (n+1)p=m(n+1)p=m is an integer, the two most likely values are m−1andm.\boxed{m-1\quad\text{and}\quad m.}

Proof.

The consecutive ratio above is greater than 11 while r+1<(n+1)pr+1<(n+1)p, equal to 11 when r+1=(n+1)pr+1=(n+1)p, and less than 11 afterwards. Hence the sequence of probabilities first increases, may have one tie, and then decreases. The stated location of the peak follows immediately.

Example 12.3

Two neighbouring peaks

Nine independent trials have success probability 2/52/5. Find all most likely success counts.

Solution.

Here (n+1)p=10⋅25=4.(n+1)p=10\cdot\frac25=4. Because this is an integer, the two modes are 4−1=3and4.4-1=3 \qquad\text{and}\qquad 4. Therefore X=3 and X=4 are equally most likely.\boxed{X=3\text{ and }X=4\text{ are equally most likely}.} Indeed, ℙ(X=4)ℙ(X=3)=9−33+12/53/5=64⋅23=1.\frac{\mathbb{P}(X=4)}{\mathbb{P}(X=3)} = \frac{9-3}{3+1}\frac{2/5}{3/5} = \frac64\cdot\frac23 =1.

Conditioning on the total number of successes

Theorem 12.3

Successful positions given their total

In NN independent Bernoulli trials with a common success probability 0<p<10<p<1, condition on exactly rr successes. Then every rr-element set of successful positions is equally likely. If YY counts the successes among the first mm trials, then ℙ(Y=j∣X=r)=(mj)(N−mr−j)(Nr).\boxed{ \mathbb{P}(Y=j\mid X=r) = \frac{\binom mj\binom{N-m}{r-j}}{\binom Nr}. }

Proof.

Every specific success–failure string with exactly rr successes has probability pr(1−p)N−rp^r(1-p)^{N-r}. Hence, after conditioning on X=rX=r, all (Nr)\binom Nr possible sets of successful positions have equal conditional probability.

To have exactly jj successes among the first mm trials, choose those jj positions in (mj)\binom mj ways and choose the remaining r−jr-j successful positions among the last N−mN-m trials in (N−mr−j)\binom{N-m}{r-j} ways. Dividing this favourable count by (Nr)\binom Nr proves the formula.

Example 12.4

A known total in ten tosses

A coin is tossed ten times independently with the same head probability 0<p<10<p<1. Given that exactly four heads occurred, find the probability that exactly two of the first three tosses were heads.

Solution.
Tools to recall.

Theorem 12.3 (successful positions given their total)

Once we know that there were exactly four heads, the four head positions form a uniformly chosen four-element subset of the ten positions.

Choose two of the first three positions for heads: (32).\binom32. Then choose the remaining two head positions among the last seven positions: (72).\binom72. The total number of possible four-element head-position sets is (104)\binom{10}{4}. Therefore ℙ=(32)(72)(104)=3⋅21210=310.\mathbb{P} = \frac{\binom32\binom72}{\binom{10}{4}} = \frac{3\cdot21}{210} = \boxed{\frac3{10}}. Notice that pp disappears. It disappears because all strings with four heads carry the same original probability.

Combining independent blocks and retaining successes

Theorem 12.4

Adding independent binomial counts

If X∼Bin(m,p),Y∼Bin(n,p),X\sim\operatorname{Bin}(m,p), \qquad Y\sim\operatorname{Bin}(n,p), and XX and YY are independent, then X+Y∼Bin(m+n,p).\boxed{X+Y\sim\operatorname{Bin}(m+n,p).}

Proof.

The variable XX counts successes in one independent block of mm trials, while YY counts successes in a second independent block of nn trials. Joining the two blocks produces m+nm+n independent trials with the same success probability pp. Therefore their total success count is binomial with parameters m+n,pm+n,p.

Algebraically, for a fixed rr, ℙ(X+Y=r)=∑jℙ(X=j)ℙ(Y=r−j)=prqm+n−r∑j(mj)(nr−j)=(m+nr)prqm+n−r,\begin{aligned} \mathbb{P}(X+Y=r) &=\sum_j\mathbb{P}(X=j)\mathbb{P}(Y=r-j)\\ &=p^rq^{m+n-r}\sum_j\binom mj\binom n{r-j}\\ &=\binom{m+n}{r}p^rq^{m+n-r}, \end{aligned} where the final step is Vandermonde's identity.

Theorem 12.5

Independent thinning of successes

Perform nn independent trials, each succeeding with probability pp. Retain each success independently with probability aa. Then the number of retained successes has distribution Bin(n,pa).\boxed{\operatorname{Bin}(n,pa).}

Proof.

At one position, a retained success requires two independent events: the original trial succeeds and that success is retained. Hence its probability is papa. The decisions at different positions are independent, so the retained-success count is binomial.

Probability generating functions: unequal success chances

Definition 12.1

Probability generating function

For a nonnegative integer-valued random variable XX, its probability generating function is GX(z)=𝔼[zX]=∑r≥0ℙ(X=r)zr.\boxed{G_X(z)=\mathbb E[z^X]=\sum_{r\ge0}\mathbb{P}(X=r)z^r.} The coefficient of zrz^r is ℙ(X=r)\mathbb{P}(X=r), and GX(1)=1G_X(1)=1.

Theorem 12.6

Products of probability generating functions

If XX and YY are independent nonnegative integer-valued random variables, then GX+Y(z)=GX(z)GY(z).\boxed{G_{X+Y}(z)=G_X(z)G_Y(z).}

Proof.

Independence gives 𝔼[zX+Y]=𝔼[zXzY]=𝔼[zX]𝔼[zY].\mathbb E[z^{X+Y}] = \mathbb E[z^Xz^Y] = \mathbb E[z^X]\mathbb E[z^Y]. Equivalently, the coefficient of zrz^r in the product is ∑jℙ(X=j)ℙ(Y=r−j)=ℙ(X+Y=r).\sum_j\mathbb{P}(X=j)\mathbb{P}(Y=r-j)=\mathbb{P}(X+Y=r).

For one Bernoulli trial with success probability pip_i, the factor is (1−pi)+piz.(1-p_i)+p_i z. Therefore, for independent trials with possibly different success probabilities, GX(z)=∏i=1n((1−pi)+piz).\boxed{G_X(z)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\bigl((1-p_i)+p_i z\bigr).}

Example 12.5

Three coins with three different biases

Three independent coins have head probabilities 1/21/2, 1/31/3, and 1/41/4. Find the probability of exactly two heads.

Solution.

The probability generating function for the number of heads is G(z)=(12+12z)(23+13z)(34+14z).G(z)= \left(\frac12+\frac12z\right) \left(\frac23+\frac13z\right) \left(\frac34+\frac14z\right). We need the coefficient of z2z^2. There are three ways to decide which coin is the single tail: ℙ(HHT)=12⋅13⋅34=18,ℙ(HTH)=12⋅23⋅14=112,ℙ(THH)=12⋅13⋅14=124.\begin{aligned} \mathbb{P}(HHT)&=\frac12\cdot\frac13\cdot\frac34=\frac18,\\ \mathbb{P}(HTH)&=\frac12\cdot\frac23\cdot\frac14=\frac1{12},\\ \mathbb{P}(THH)&=\frac12\cdot\frac13\cdot\frac14=\frac1{24}. \end{aligned} Hence ℙ(exactly two heads)=18+112+124=14.\mathbb{P}(\text{exactly two heads}) = \frac18+\frac1{12}+\frac1{24} = \boxed{\frac14}. The generating product is useful because it keeps the three different biases visible.

Example 12.6

An even number of successes

Let X∼Bin(n,p)X\sim\operatorname{Bin}(n,p). Find a closed expression for the probability that XX is even.

Solution.

We know 1=(p+q)n=∑r=0n(nr)prqn−r.1=(p+q)^n = \sum_{r=0}^{n}\binom nrp^rq^{n-r}. Also, (q−p)n=∑r=0n(−1)r(nr)prqn−r.(q-p)^n = \sum_{r=0}^{n}(-1)^r\binom nrp^rq^{n-r}. When we add these equations, all odd-rr terms cancel and all even-rr terms double. Therefore 2ℙ(X is even)=1+(q−p)n.2\mathbb{P}(X\text{ is even})=1+(q-p)^n. Since q−p=1−2pq-p=1-2p, ℙ(X is even)=rac1+(1−2p)n2.\boxed{ \mathbb{P}(X\text{ is even})= rac{1+(1-2p)^n}{2}. } For a fair coin, p=1/2p=1/2, so the answer is 1/21/2 for every positive nn.

Forbidden patterns: a recurrence with probability weights

Theorem 12.7

No two consecutive successes

Let ana_n be the probability that nn independent Bernoulli trials, each with success probability pp, contain no two consecutive successes. With q=1−pq=1-p, an=qan−1+pqan−2(n≥2),\boxed{a_n=qa_{n-1}+pqa_{n-2}\qquad(n\ge2),} with a0=a1=1.a_0=a_1=1.

Proof.

Separate valid strings by their first symbol.

If the first trial is a failure, which has probability qq, the remaining n−1n-1 trials must form any valid string. This contributes qan−1qa_{n-1}.

If the first trial is a success, the second trial must be a failure. The initial block SFSF has probability pqpq, and the remaining n−2n-2 trials may be any valid string. This contributes pqan−2pqa_{n-2}.

The two cases are disjoint and exhaustive, which proves the recurrence.

Random walks between two boundaries

Suppose a token occupies one of the positions 0,1,2,…,N.0,1,2,\ldots,N. Positions 00 and NN are absorbing: once the token reaches one of them, the game ends. From an interior position, the token moves one step right or one step left.

Theorem 12.8

Fair gambler's ruin

Let hih_i be the probability that a fair nearest-neighbour walk, starting at ii, reaches NN before 00. Then hi=iN(0≤i≤N).\boxed{h_i=\frac{i}{N}\qquad(0\le i\le N).}

Proof.

The boundary values are h0=0,hN=1.h_0=0, \qquad h_N=1. For 1≤i≤N−11\le i\le N-1, the first step goes to i+1i+1 or i−1i-1, each with probability 1/21/2. By the first-step principle, hi=12hi+1+12hi−1.h_i=\frac12h_{i+1}+\frac12h_{i-1}. Rearranging, hi+1−hi=hi−hi−1.h_{i+1}-h_i=h_i-h_{i-1}. Thus all consecutive differences are equal, so hih_i is an arithmetic progression in ii. The only arithmetic progression satisfying h0=0h_0=0 and hN=1h_N=1 is hi=iN.h_i=\frac{i}{N}.

Example 12.8

A fair walk starting at three

A token moves on 0,1,…,100,1,\ldots,10. From every interior point it moves left or right with equal probability. Starting from 33, what is the probability of reaching 1010 before 00?

Solution.

By the fair gambler's-ruin theorem, ℙ(reach 10 before 0)=h3=310.\mathbb{P}(\text{reach }10\text{ before }0)=h_3=\frac3{10}. Therefore 310.\boxed{\frac3{10}}. The answer is proportional to the starting distance from the losing boundary.

Theorem 12.9

Biased gambler's ruin

Suppose the walk moves one step right with probability pp and one step left with probability q=1−pq=1-p, where p≠qp\ne q. If hih_i is the probability of reaching NN before 00, then hi=1−(q/p)i1−(q/p)N.\boxed{ h_i= \frac{1-(q/p)^i}{1-(q/p)^N}. }

Proof.

The first-step equation is hi=phi+1+qhi−1,h0=0,hN=1.h_i=ph_{i+1}+qh_{i-1}, \qquad h_0=0, \quad h_N=1. Try hi=rih_i=r^i. The characteristic equation is pr2−r+q=0=(r−1)(pr−q).pr^2-r+q=0=(r-1)(pr-q). Thus the two roots are 1,qp.1, \qquad \frac qp. Hence hi=A+B(qp)i.h_i=A+B\left(\frac qp\right)^i. The condition h0=0h_0=0 gives A+B=0A+B=0, so hi=A(1−(qp)i).h_i=A\left(1-\left(\frac qp\right)^i\right). Using hN=1h_N=1, A=11−(q/p)N,A=\frac1{1-(q/p)^N}, which proves the formula.

Here qp=1/32/3=12.\frac qp=\frac{1/3}{2/3}=\frac12. Two parameters vary: races and match probabilities

This is the probability analogue of the two-parameter recurrences in Lecture 8. The state must record how many further wins each player needs.

Theorem 12.10

Recurrence for a race to two targets

Suppose player AA wins each independent round with probability pp, and player BB wins with probability q=1−pq=1-p. Let W(r,s)W(r,s) be the probability that AA wins the match when AA still needs rr round-wins and BB still needs ss. Then W(r,s)=pW(r−1,s)+qW(r,s−1),\boxed{W(r,s)=pW(r-1,s)+qW(r,s-1),} with boundary values W(0,s)=1,W(r,0)=0.\boxed{W(0,s)=1, \qquad W(r,0)=0.} Moreover, W(r,s)=∑j=0s−1(r+j−1j)prqj.\boxed{ W(r,s)=\sum_{j=0}^{s-1}\binom{r+j-1}{j}p^rq^j. }

Proof.

For the recurrence, condition on the next round. If AA wins it, the state becomes (r−1,s)(r-1,s); if BB wins it, the state becomes (r,s−1)(r,s-1). The boundary values say that a player who needs no more wins has already completed the match.

For the closed sum, suppose BB has won exactly jj rounds when AA obtains the final required win. The last round must be won by AA. Before it, there are r−1r-1 wins by AA and jj wins by BB, arranged in (r+j−1j)\binom{r+j-1}{j} orders. The probability of each full sequence is prqjp^rq^j. Since BB must not yet have reached ss wins, j=0,1,…,s−1j=0,1,\ldots,s-1. Summing gives the formula.

Two players play independent games until one has won three. Player AA wins each game with probability 2/32/3. Find the probability that AA wins the match.

Solution.

Player AA needs r=3r=3 wins, and player BB needs s=3s=3 wins. Put p=23,q=13.p=\frac23, \qquad q=\frac13. The number of wins by BB before AA's final win may be 0,10,1, or 22. Hence W(3,3)=(20)p3+(31)p3q+(42)p3q2=(23)3+3(23)3(13)+6(23)3(13)2=827+827+1681=6481.\begin{aligned} W(3,3) &=\binom20p^3+\binom31p^3q+\binom42p^3q^2\\ &=\left(\frac23\right)^3 +3\left(\frac23\right)^3\left(\frac13\right) +6\left(\frac23\right)^3\left(\frac13\right)^2\\ &=\frac8{27}+\frac8{27}+\frac{16}{81}\\ &=\boxed{\frac{64}{81}}. \end{aligned} The three terms correspond respectively to winning in 3,43,4, or 55 games.

Pattern races: the state must remember a suffix

When we wait for one word to occur before another, the current number of tosses is not enough information. We must remember the longest recent suffix that could become the beginning of one of the target words.

Example 12.11

Will HHHH or TTTT appear first?

A fair coin is tossed until either HHHH or TTTT first appears. Given that the first toss is HH, find the probability that HHHH appears before TTTT.

Solution.

Olympiad bridges: recurrence counts become probabilities

The recurrence problems in Lecture 7 were originally counting problems. Once a random model is specified, the same counts become numerators of probabilities. This is a useful bridge between combinatorics and probability: the recurrence does the difficult counting, and the probability model supplies the denominator and the weights.

⚠ Common mistake

A recurrence count is not automatically a probability. Before dividing, check whether all counted objects are equally likely. If different objects have different lengths or use different transition probabilities, weight them separately.

A first-reader checklist for recursive probability

Before you calculate

When a probability problem looks recursive, ask these questions in order.

  1. What exactly is the state?

  2. What are all possible first moves from that state?

  3. Are those first-move cases mutually exclusive and exhaustive?

  4. What probability multiplies each smaller state?

  5. Which states are terminal successes, and which are terminal failures?

  6. Do I need one sequence, several states, or two varying indices?

  7. After solving, is the answer between 00 and 11?

  8. Can I verify a small case by listing outcomes or drawing a state graph?

💡 The central habit

Do not begin by searching for a formula. Begin by asking what happens next. Once the state and the first move are correct, the recurrence usually writes itself.

Theorem 12.1 (first-step recurrence)

Let bnb_n be the probability that no occurrence of HHHHHH appears in the first nn tosses. Classify a valid string according to its initial block before the first tail. Exactly one of the following happens: T,HT,HHT.T, \qquad HT, \qquad HHT. The forbidden block HHHHHH prevents a longer initial run of heads. These three initial blocks have probabilities 1/2,1/4,1/81/2,1/4,1/8, and leave valid suffixes of lengths n−1,n−2,n−3n-1,n-2,n-3, respectively.
