What we shall learn in this lecture

In our first probability lecture, we learnt to describe the possible outcomes of an experiment and assign probabilities to events. Today I want to change one part of that story: suppose the experiment has already taken place, and someone gives us some information about its outcome. We have not necessarily changed the experiment. We have changed what we know.

For example, before a fair die is rolled, the probability of a six is 1/61/6. If I now tell you that the result is even, only 2,4,62,4,6 remain possible, and the probability of a six becomes 1/31/3. If I tell you that the result is greater than four, it becomes 1/21/2. The die has not changed its mind; our description has been updated.

Our route through the lecture is new information→conditional probability→multiplication along a path,different possible routes→total probability→Bayes’ theorem,repeated independent trials→the binomial distribution.\begin{gathered} \text{new information}\ \longrightarrow\ \text{conditional probability} \ \longrightarrow\ \text{multiplication along a path},\\[2pt] \text{different possible routes}\ \longrightarrow\ \text{total probability} \ \longrightarrow\ \text{Bayes' theorem},\\[2pt] \text{repeated independent trials}\ \longrightarrow\ \text{the binomial distribution}. \end{gathered} We shall work with dice, cards, urns, information-revealing games, quality-control models, and repeated trials. When a model describes a factory or a detector, its numerical data are hypothetical: they let us understand the mathematics without pretending to report measured data.

How I want you to read this lecture

Before using a formula, complete the sentence: “The information I am conditioning on is…” Then identify the event whose probability is requested. Keep these two roles separate. In a tree, multiply along a complete route and add only across disjoint routes. At the end, use the binomial formula only after checking its assumptions.

New information changes the reference group

Let us begin with people rather than symbols. Suppose a class contains 8080 students. There are 3030 students in the football club, 2020 in the music club, and 1212 in both. If I choose a student uniformly from the class, the chance of choosing a music-club member is 20/8020/80. If I choose uniformly from the football-club members, the relevant fraction is 12/3012/30. We have changed the group relative to which the fraction is calculated.

The definition of conditional probability

Let AA and BB be events in a probability space. We assume ℙ(B)>0\mathbb{P}(B)>0. Once BB is known to have occurred, outcomes outside BB are discarded. Inside BB, the event we want is A∩BA\cap B. Its original probability mass must be compared with the original mass of all of BB.

Definition 11.1

Conditional probability

For ℙ(B)>0\mathbb{P}(B)>0, the probability of AA given BB is ℙ(A∣B)=ℙ(A∩B)ℙ(B).\boxed{\mathbb{P}(A\mid B)=\frac{\mathbb{P}(A\cap B)}{\mathbb{P}(B)}.} The vertical bar means “given that”, not division by an event.

The denominator rescales the retained probability mass to one. In particular, ℙ(B∣B)=1\mathbb{P}(B\mid B)=1, while ℙ(Bc∣B)=0\mathbb{P}(B^{\mathrm c}\mid B)=0. In a finite uniform sample space, the definition simplifies to ℙ(A∣B)=|A∩B||B|.\mathbb{P}(A\mid B)=\frac{|A\cap B|}{|B|}. We cancel the common denominator |Ω||\Omega|. Without uniformity, we must add the probability masses of the retained outcomes; merely counting them is not enough.

Why this is itself a probability law

Fix BB with positive probability and put Q(A)=ℙ(A∣B)Q(A)=\mathbb{P}(A\mid B). Then Q(A)≥0Q(A)\ge0, Q(Ω)=1Q(\Omega)=1, and, for disjoint events A1,A2,…A_1,A_2,\ldots, Q(⋃iAi)=∑iℙ(Ai∩B)ℙ(B)=∑iQ(Ai).Q\left(\bigcup_i A_i\right) =\frac{\sum_i\mathbb{P}(A_i\cap B)}{\mathbb{P}(B)} =\sum_i Q(A_i). The intersections Ai∩BA_i\cap B are disjoint because the original AiA_i's are disjoint. Consequently all the usual probability identities still hold within the same conditioning event. For example, ℙ(Ac∣B)=1−ℙ(A∣B)\mathbb{P}(A^{\mathrm c}\mid B)=1-\mathbb{P}(A\mid B) and ℙ(A∪C∣B)=ℙ(A∣B)+ℙ(C∣B)−ℙ(A∩C∣B).\mathbb{P}(A\cup C\mid B)=\mathbb{P}(A\mid B)+\mathbb{P}(C\mid B)-\mathbb{P}(A\cap C\mid B). Notice that the condition is BB in every term.

⚠ Common mistake

The elementary ratio is undefined when ℙ(B)=0\mathbb{P}(B)=0. We shall not divide by zero or assign a conditional probability arbitrarily in that situation. Conditioning on exact values of continuous random variables requires a different development later.

Example 11.2

An even result on a die

A fair die is rolled. Given that the result is even, find the probability that it is greater than 33.

Solution.

Let B={2,4,6}B=\{2,4,6\} be the information, and let A={4,5,6}A=\{4,5,6\} be the target event. Then A∩B={4,6}A\cap B=\{4,6\}, so ℙ(A∣B)=23.\mathbb{P}(A\mid B)=\frac{2}{3}. The unconditional answer would be 3/6=1/23/6=1/2. The face 55 is favourable to AA, but it cannot contribute after we are told that the outcome is even.

Example 11.3

At least one six is not the same as a specified six

Two fair dice are rolled independently. Find the probability that both show six, (a) given that at least one shows six, and (b) given that the first shows six.

Solution.

We temporarily label the dice first and second. There are 3636 equally likely ordered pairs. Under information (a), the retained pairs are the six in the last row and the six in the last column, with (6,6)(6,6) counted in both lists. Therefore there are 6+6−1=116+6-1=11 retained outcomes, exactly one of which has two sixes. The answer is 1/111/11. Under information (b), the retained pairs are (6,1),(6,2),…,(6,6)(6,1),(6,2),\ldots,(6,6). There are six, and the answer is 1/61/6.

What changed was the information, not the fairness of either die.

Solution.

Example 11.5

A hand known to contain an ace

Two cards are selected uniformly from a standard 5252-card deck, without replacement. Given that at least one is an ace, find the probability that both are aces.

Solution.

We use unordered two-card hands throughout. There are (522)−(482)=1326−1128=198\binom{52}{2}-\binom{48}{2}=1326-1128=198 hands containing at least one ace: subtract the hands with no ace. Among them, (42)=6\binom42=6 contain two aces. Thus the required probability is 6198=133.\boxed{\frac{6}{198}=\frac1{33}}. The statement did not identify a particular ace or reveal a particular card. Saying “one card is an ace” informally can hide this distinction; the precise statement is “at least one of the two cards is an ace”.

The multiplication rule: build a complete history

Rearranging (11.1) gives

Theorem 11.1

Multiplication rule

If ℙ(A)>0\mathbb{P}(A)>0, then ℙ(A∩B)=ℙ(A)ℙ(B∣A).\mathbb{P}(A\cap B)=\mathbb{P}(A)\mathbb{P}(B\mid A). If ℙ(B)>0\mathbb{P}(B)>0, the same joint probability is ℙ(A∩B)=ℙ(B)ℙ(A∣B).\mathbb{P}(A\cap B)=\mathbb{P}(B)\mathbb{P}(A\mid B).

The rule does not assert independence. It says that, after the first event, the probability of the next event must be calculated using the information already obtained. For three events, provided the conditioning events have positive probability, ℙ(A∩B∩C)=ℙ(A)ℙ(B∣A)ℙ(C∣A∩B).\mathbb{P}(A\cap B\cap C) =\mathbb{P}(A)\mathbb{P}(B\mid A)\mathbb{P}(C\mid A\cap B). The denominators cancel when we substitute the definitions. More generally, ℙ(⋂i=1mAi)=ℙ(A1)∏j=2mℙ(Aj|⋂i=1j−1Ai).\mathbb{P}\left(\bigcap_{i=1}^{m}A_i\right) =\mathbb{P}(A_1)\prod_{j=2}^{m} \mathbb{P}\left(A_j\,\middle|\,\bigcap_{i=1}^{j-1}A_i\right). If a prefix event has probability zero, the complete history has probability zero. We stop there instead of writing later undefined conditional probabilities.

One order versus all orders

A bag contains 55 red and 33 blue balls. Three are drawn successively without replacement. Find the probability of the order red–blue–red, and then the probability of exactly two red balls.

Solution.

After the first red is removed, 77 balls remain; after a blue is removed, 66 remain, of which 44 are red. Therefore ℙ(RBR)=583746=528.\mathbb{P}(RBR)=\frac58\frac37\frac46=\boxed{\frac5{28}}. A probability tree is a record of conditional choices

At each branching point, the outgoing probabilities add to one. An edge label answers a question of the form “given the history that led us here, what happens next?” A leaf represents a complete history. The product along its path is the probability of that history. Different leaves are mutually exclusive, so their probabilities may be added. We shall use exactly these rules when we derive total probability.

Independence: when information makes no change

Recall the definition ℙ(A∩B)=ℙ(A)ℙ(B)\mathbb{P}(A\cap B)=\mathbb{P}(A)\mathbb{P}(B). When ℙ(B)>0\mathbb{P}(B)>0, division by ℙ(B)\mathbb{P}(B) shows that this is equivalent to ℙ(A∣B)=ℙ(A).\mathbb{P}(A\mid B)=\mathbb{P}(A). This is the precise meaning of “learning that BB occurred does not change the probability of AA”. If both probabilities are positive, we may reverse the roles as well. Independence is symmetric, even though the two conditional probabilities generally differ.

Example 11.8

Independent events need not come from separate experiments

A fair die is rolled. Let A={2,4,6}A=\{2,4,6\} and B={1,2}B=\{1,2\}. Are the events independent?

Solution.
Tools to recall.

Definition 10.10 (independence)

We have ℙ(A)=1/2\mathbb{P}(A)=1/2, ℙ(B)=1/3\mathbb{P}(B)=1/3, and ℙ(A∩B)=ℙ({2})=1/6\mathbb{P}(A\cap B)=\mathbb{P}(\{2\})=1/6. The last number equals (1/2)(1/3)(1/2)(1/3), so the events are independent. Within the two outcomes of BB, exactly one is even. ⚠ Common mistake

Mutually exclusive events with positive probabilities are not independent: ℙ(A∩B)=0\mathbb{P}(A\cap B)=0, but ℙ(A)ℙ(B)>0\mathbb{P}(A)\mathbb{P}(B)>0. “They cannot occur together” and “one does not affect the probability of the other” are very different statements.

Pairwise independence is not enough for many trials

For a family A1,…,AnA_1,\ldots,A_n, mutual independence requires the product rule for every subcollection: ℙ(⋂i∈IAi)=∏i∈Iℙ(Ai)for every nonempty I⊆{1,…,n}.\mathbb{P}\left(\bigcap_{i\in I}A_i\right)=\prod_{i\in I}\mathbb{P}(A_i) \quad\text{for every nonempty }I\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}. Checking only pairs is a weaker condition.

We may replace any of these independent events by its complement and retain independence. For example, for a subcollection not containing index jj, set H=⋂i∈IAiH=\bigcap_{i\in I}A_i. Then ℙ(H∩Ajc)=ℙ(H)−ℙ(H∩Aj)=(∏i∈Iℙ(Ai))(1−ℙ(Aj)).\mathbb{P}(H\cap A_j^{\mathrm c}) =\mathbb{P}(H)-\mathbb{P}(H\cap A_j) =\left(\prod_{i\in I}\mathbb{P}(A_i)\right)(1-\mathbb{P}(A_j)). Applying the same argument successively handles several complements. This explains why, for independent trials, a specified string of successes and failures has probability equal to the product of all its individual factors.

The law of total probability: add all possible routes

Sometimes we cannot calculate the target probability in one step, but we can calculate it after being told which of several cases occurred. Then we condition on those cases and average, using their correct weights.

Definition 11.2

A partition of the sample space

Events B1,…,BmB_1,\ldots,B_m form a partition if they are pairwise disjoint and B1∪⋯∪Bm=ΩB_1\cup\cdots\cup B_m=\Omega. Exactly one case occurs. For conditional formulas below we assume ℙ(Bi)>0\mathbb{P}(B_i)>0; zero-probability cases contribute zero joint mass and can be omitted.

Theorem 11.2

Law of total probability

For such a partition and any event AA, ℙ(A)=∑i=1mℙ(Bi)ℙ(A∣Bi).\boxed{\mathbb{P}(A)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathbb{P}(B_i)\mathbb{P}(A\mid B_i).}

Proof.

We have the disjoint decomposition A=(A∩B1)∪̇⋯∪̇(A∩Bm)A=(A\cap B_1)\mathbin{\dot\cup}\cdots\mathbin{\dot\cup}(A\cap B_m). By additivity and the multiplication rule, ℙ(A)=∑i=1mℙ(A∩Bi)=∑i=1mℙ(Bi)ℙ(A∣Bi).\mathbb{P}(A)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathbb{P}(A\cap B_i) =\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathbb{P}(B_i)\mathbb{P}(A\mid B_i). No independence assumption has been used.

Because the weights ℙ(Bi)\mathbb{P}(B_i) add to one, ℙ(A)\mathbb{P}(A) lies between the smallest and largest of the conditional probabilities. This is a useful quick check. Taking an unweighted average is justified only when the cases have equal probabilities.

Example 11.12

A transferred ball changes the second urn

Urn I contains 33 white and 22 black balls. Urn II contains 22 white and 33 black balls. A uniformly selected ball from Urn I is transferred to Urn II; then a ball is drawn uniformly from Urn II. Find the probability that this final ball is white.

Solution.

We do not know the final composition of Urn II until we know the transferred colour. There are two exhaustive cases. If a white ball was transferred, probability 3/53/5, Urn II has 33 white out of 66. If a black ball was transferred, probability 2/52/5, it has 22 white out of 66. Therefore ℙ(final white)=3536+2526=1330.\boxed{\mathbb{P}(\text{final white})=\frac35\frac36+\frac25\frac26=\frac{13}{30}}. Each branch uses a definite composition. We did not replace the unknown transferred colour by a fractional ball; we averaged the probabilities of two actual cases.

Example 11.14

A first-step recurrence for a game

Players AA and BB take turns until someone succeeds, with AA first. On each attempt AA succeeds with probability 1/31/3, and BB with probability 1/21/2. All attempts are independent. Find the probability that AA wins.

Bayes' theorem: reverse the direction of the question

Total probability starts with possible sources and predicts observations. Bayes' theorem starts with an observation and updates the probabilities of those sources. The two calculations use the same joint probabilities.

Theorem 11.3

Bayes' theorem for a partition

Let B1,…,BmB_1,\ldots,B_m be a partition with positive probabilities, and let ℙ(A)>0\mathbb{P}(A)>0. Then ℙ(Bj∣A)=ℙ(Bj)ℙ(A∣Bj)∑i=1mℙ(Bi)ℙ(A∣Bi).\boxed{\mathbb{P}(B_j\mid A)= \frac{\mathbb{P}(B_j)\mathbb{P}(A\mid B_j)}{\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathbb{P}(B_i)\mathbb{P}(A\mid B_i)}.}

Proof.

The numerator is ℙ(Bj∩A)\mathbb{P}(B_j\cap A), by the multiplication rule. The denominator is ℙ(A)\mathbb{P}(A), by total probability. Their ratio is exactly the definition of ℙ(Bj∣A)\mathbb{P}(B_j\mid A).

The prior is ℙ(Bj)\mathbb{P}(B_j), the probability before the new observation. The likelihood ℙ(A∣Bj)\mathbb{P}(A\mid B_j) asks how compatible the observation is with source BjB_j. The posterior ℙ(Bj∣A)\mathbb{P}(B_j\mid A) is the updated probability. We first multiply prior by likelihood for each source, then divide by the total of those products. The resulting posterior probabilities sum to one.

Example 11.16

A flagged item is not automatically defective

In a hypothetical production model, 1%1\% of items are defective. A detector flags 99%99\% of defective items, and also flags 5%5\% of good items. For a randomly selected item that is flagged, find the probability that it is defective.

Solution.

The final connection: a count becomes a stable proportion

Bayes' theorem reverses a question about evidence. The binomial distribution then lets us describe an entire count of repeated outcomes, including the fluctuations around its average.

☠ Problem 6 · A suit and a rank

Two cards are drawn successively without replacement from a standard deck. Given that the second card is an ace, find the probability that the first card is a heart.

Show solutionHide solution
Solution.
Tools to recall.

Definition 11.1 (conditional probability)

Model the experiment by ordered pairs of distinct cards. Among pairs whose second card is an ace, there are 4⋅51=2044\cdot51=204 equally likely possibilities. If the second card is the ace of hearts, the first has 1212 possible heart choices. If the second is any of the other three aces, the first has 1313 heart choices. Sampling without replacement does not imply that every pair of events is dependent; independence must be checked for the particular events.

Solution.

☠ Problem 16 · Four doors

A prize is placed uniformly behind one of four doors. You choose one door. A host who knows the prize location always opens two other doors with no prize, leaving your door and one other door closed. You switch to that other door. Find your success probability.

Show solutionHide solution
Solution.
Tools to recall.

Theorem 11.2 (total probability)

Your initial choice is correct with probability 1/41/4 and wrong with probability 3/43/4. If it was wrong, the prize is behind one of the three unchosen doors. ☠ Problem 20 · Which counts are binomial?

Decide whether each success count is binomial, explaining the assumptions.

  1. Draw five balls without replacement from a bag with five red and five blue balls; count red.

  2. Draw five times with replacement and independent uniform selection from that bag; count red.

  3. Make five independent trials with success probabilities 1/2,1/3,1/4,1/5,1/61/2,1/3,1/4,1/5,1/6.

Show solutionHide solution
Solution.
Tools to recall.

Theorem 11.4 (binomial probability formula); Definition 11.4 (binomial-model assumptions)

In (a), draws are dependent and the conditional red probability changes. The count is hypergeometric, not binomial. Its zero-red probability is 1/(105)=1/2521/\binom{10}{5}=1/252, unlike the 1/321/32 that Bin(5,1/2)\operatorname{Bin}(5,1/2) would give.

In (b), replacement restores the same composition, and independent selection is explicitly assumed. Each red probability is 1/21/2, so the count is Bin(5,1/2)\boxed{\operatorname{Bin}(5,1/2)}.

In (c), the trials are independent, but their probabilities differ. The generating polynomial is ∏i=15(1−pi+piz)\prod_{i=1}^{5}(1-p_i+p_i z), not a fifth power of one common factor. This counts independent trials with unequal success probabilities, rather than trials with one common parameter.

In (d), the count is binomial conditional on which coin was chosen, but the unconditional law is a mixture. The trials share one hidden coin and are dependent in that mixed model. Thus it is not Bin(5,1/2)\operatorname{Bin}(5,1/2).
