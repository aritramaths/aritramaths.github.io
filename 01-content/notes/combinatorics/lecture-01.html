Why Do We Need Counting?

💡 Motivation

Combinatorics is the art of counting carefully.

At first, counting may look easy. But in many problems, direct counting becomes confusing. We may miss some cases. We may also count the same case more than once.

For example, we may ask:

  • How many ways can some boys and girls be arranged in a row?

  • How many numbers can be formed using some given digits?

  • How many routes are possible from one city to another?

  • How many possible passwords can be made under certain rules?

In this lecture, I want us to learn how to count in an organised way.

There are two basic principles in this lecture:

  • the Addition Principle;

  • the Multiplication Principle.

The Addition Principle is used when we divide the problem into cases.

The Multiplication Principle is used when a task is completed step by step.

Factorials

Definition 1.1

Factorial

For a positive integer nn, the product of the first nn natural numbers is called nn factorial and is denoted by n!n!.

Thus, n!=n(n−1)(n−2)⋯3⋅2⋅1.n! = n(n-1)(n-2)\cdots 3\cdot 2\cdot 1.

By convention, 0!=1.0! = 1.

For example, 4!=4⋅3⋅2⋅1=24,4! = 4\cdot 3\cdot 2\cdot 1 = 24, and 5!=5⋅4⋅3⋅2⋅1=120.5! = 5\cdot 4\cdot 3\cdot 2\cdot 1 = 120.

Remark

In this chapter, factorials are used only for whole numbers.

So expressions such as (−3)!or(12)!(-3)! \quad \text{or} \quad \left(\frac12\right)! are not meaningful for us here.

Theorem 1.1

Basic Properties of Factorials

For non-negative integers, factorials satisfy the following properties:

  1. 0!=10!=1.

  2. If n≥1n\geq 1, then n!=n(n−1)!.n! = n(n-1)!.

  3. If x!=y!x! = y!, then either x=yx=y, or {x,y}={0,1}.\left\{x,y\right\}=\left\{0,1\right\}.

  4. For every n≥5n\geq 5, the number n!n! ends in 00.

Remark

The reason n!n! ends in 00 for every n≥5n\geq 5 is that n!n! contains both 22 and 55 as factors.

Since 2⋅5=10,2\cdot 5=10, there is at least one factor of 1010 inside n!n!.

The Addition Principle

💡 Motivation

The Addition Principle is used when a task can happen in one of several separate ways.

The important word is or.

For example, if a student may choose either a cricket bat or a football, then the choices are added. The two choices are alternatives.

Theorem 1.2

Addition Principle

Suppose an event can occur in one of kk mutually exclusive ways: E1,E2,…,Ek.E_1,E_2,\ldots,E_k.

If E1E_1 can occur in n1n_1 ways, E2E_2 can occur in n2n_2 ways, and so on, then the total number of ways in which at least one of these events can occur is n1+n2+⋯+nk.n_1+n_2+\cdots+n_k.

Remark

The phrase mutually exclusive means that the cases do not overlap.

In counting language, this means that the same object is not counted in two different cases.

⚠ Common mistake

Before adding the answers from different cases, always check that the cases are disjoint.

If the cases overlap, then adding will count some objects more than once.

Theorem 1.3

Addition Principle in Set Form

Let A1,A2,…,AkA_1,A_2,\ldots,A_k be finite sets.

If they are pairwise disjoint, that is, Ai∩Aj=⌀whenever i≠j,A_i\cap A_j=\varnothing \quad \text{whenever } i\neq j, then |A1∪A2∪⋯∪Ak|=|A1|+|A2|+⋯+|Ak|.\left|A_1\cup A_2\cup\cdots\cup A_k\right| = \left|A_1\right|+\left|A_2\right|+\cdots+\left|A_k\right|.

Equivalently, |⋃i=1kAi|=∑i=1k|Ai|.\left|\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}A_i\right| = \sum_{i=1}^{k}\left|A_i\right|.

Theorem 1.3 (Addition Principle in Set Form) is just the set-theoretic form of Theorem 1.2 (Addition Principle).

Example 1.3

Routes from One City to Another

A person wants to travel from city PP to city QQ.

There are 22 ways by sea, 33 ways by air, and 22 ways by road.

How many total ways are there to travel from PP to QQ?

Solution.

The person can travel by sea, by air, or by road.

These three types of routes are separate cases.

By Theorem 1.2 (Addition Principle), the total number of ways is 2+3+2=7.2+3+2=7.

Therefore, there are 7\boxed{7} ways to travel from PP to QQ.

Example 1.4

Entering a City

There are 1515 gates to enter a city from the north and 1010 gates to enter the city from the east.

In how many ways can a person enter the city?

Solution.

The person may enter from the north or from the east.

There are 1515 ways from the north and 1010 ways from the east.

Since these are separate choices, by Theorem 1.2 (Addition Principle), 15+10=25.15+10=25.

Thus, the person can enter the city in 25\boxed{25} ways.

Example 1.5

Choosing a Monitor

A class has 1010 boys and 55 girls.

The teacher selects either a boy or a girl for the post of monitor.

In how many ways can the teacher make the selection?

Solution.

A boy can be selected in 1010 ways.

A girl can be selected in 55 ways.

The teacher selects either a boy or a girl, not both. So the two cases are disjoint.

By Theorem 1.2 (Addition Principle), 10+5=15.10+5=15.

Hence, the monitor can be selected in 15\boxed{15} ways.

Example 1.6

Two-Digit Numbers Divisible by 55

Find the number of two-digit numbers with different digits which are divisible by 55.

Solution.

A number is divisible by 55 if its last digit is 00 or 55.

We split the problem into two disjoint cases.

Case 1: The unit digit is 55.

The tens digit can be any digit from 11 to 99, except 55.

So there are 88 possibilities.

Case 2: The unit digit is 00.

The tens digit can be any digit from 11 to 99.

So there are 99 possibilities.

By Theorem 1.2 (Addition Principle), the total number is 8+9=17.8+9=17.

Hence, the answer is 17.\boxed{17}.

The Multiplication Principle

💡 Motivation

The Multiplication Principle is used when a task is completed in steps.

The important word is and.

For example, if a student chooses one shirt and one pair of trousers, then the choices are multiplied because both choices are made.

Theorem 1.4

Multiplication Principle

Suppose an event can be completed in rr ordered steps: E1,E2,…,Er.E_1,E_2,\ldots,E_r.

If E1E_1 can be done in n1n_1 ways, E2E_2 can be done in n2n_2 ways, and so on, then the total number of ways is n1n2⋯nr.n_1n_2\cdots n_r.

Equivalently, ∏i=1rni.\prod_{i=1}^{r} n_i.

Remark

Use Theorem 1.4 (Multiplication Principle) when the choices are made one after another.

In class, I use the following test: if you can say “first choose this, then choose that”, you are probably using the Multiplication Principle.

Theorem 1.5

Multiplication Principle in Set Form

Let A1,A2,…,ArA_1,A_2,\ldots,A_r be finite sets.

Then A1×A2×⋯×Ar={(a1,a2,…,ar)∣ai∈Ai}.A_1\times A_2\times\cdots\times A_r = \left\{(a_1,a_2,\ldots,a_r)\mid a_i\in A_i\right\}.

Moreover, |A1×A2×⋯×Ar|=|A1||A2|⋯|Ar|.\left|A_1\times A_2\times\cdots\times A_r\right| = \left|A_1\right|\left|A_2\right|\cdots\left|A_r\right|.

Equivalently, |∏i=1rAi|=∏i=1r|Ai|.\left|\prod_{i=1}^{r}A_i\right| = \prod_{i=1}^{r}\left|A_i\right|.

Example 1.8

Travelling Through Several Cities

To reach city DD from city AA, a traveller must pass through city BB and then city CC.

There are 22 ways from AA to BB, 55 ways from BB to CC, and 33 ways from CC to DD.

Find the number of ways to travel from AA to DD.

Solution.

The journey has three steps: A→B,B→C,C→D.A\to B, \qquad B\to C, \qquad C\to D.

There are 22 choices for the first step, 55 choices for the second step, and 33 choices for the third step.

By Theorem 1.4 (Multiplication Principle), the total number of routes is 2⋅5⋅3=30.2\cdot 5\cdot 3=30.

Therefore, the number of possible routes is 30.\boxed{30}.

Example 1.9

Entering and Leaving Through Different Gates

A hall has 33 gates.

In how many ways can a person enter through one gate and come out through a different gate?

Solution.

There are 33 choices for the entrance gate.

After entering, the person must leave through a different gate. So there are 22 choices for the exit gate.

By Theorem 1.4 (Multiplication Principle), the total number of ways is 3⋅2=6.3\cdot 2=6.

Hence, the answer is 6.\boxed{6}.

Example 1.10

City with 1212 Gates

A city has 1212 gates.

In how many ways can a person enter through one gate and come out through a different gate?

Solution.

The entrance gate can be chosen in 1212 ways.

After choosing the entrance gate, the exit gate must be different. So the exit gate can be chosen in 1111 ways.

By Theorem 1.4 (Multiplication Principle), the number of ways is 12⋅11=132.12\cdot 11=132.

Therefore, the answer is 132.\boxed{132}.

Sequences

Definition 1.2

kk-ary Sequence

A sequence a1a2⋯ana_1a_2\cdots a_n is called a kk-ary sequence of length nn if each term is chosen from {0,1,2,…,k−1}.\left\{0,1,2,\ldots,k-1\right\}.

When k=2k=2, it is called a binary sequence.

When k=3k=3, it is called a ternary sequence.

When k=4k=4, it is called a quaternary sequence.

For example, the binary sequences of length 33 are 000,001,010,011,100,101,110,111.000, 001, 010, 011, 100, 101, 110, 111.

There are 88 of them.

Theorem 1.6

Number of kk-ary Sequences

The number of kk-ary sequences of length nn is kn.k^n.

Proof.

To form a kk-ary sequence a1a2⋯an,a_1a_2\cdots a_n, we choose each term from {0,1,2,…,k−1}.\left\{0,1,2,\ldots,k-1\right\}.

There are kk choices for a1a_1, kk choices for a2a_2, and so on.

By Theorem 1.4 (Multiplication Principle), the total number of sequences is k⋅k⋅⋯⋅k⏟n times=kn.\underbrace{k\cdot k\cdot\cdots\cdot k}_{n\text{ times}} = k^n.

Connection. There is a one-to-one matching between the following two collections: Set A: k-ary sequences of length n,Set B: fillings of n labelled places, each using one of k symbols.\begin{array}{l}\text{Set A: }k\text{-ary sequences of length }n,\\\text{Set B: fillings of }n\text{ labelled places, each using one of }k\text{ symbols.}\end{array} These two sets are matched by reading the entries of the sequence as the entries placed in the labelled boxes. A sequence tells us exactly how to fill the labelled places, and a filling of the labelled places gives exactly one sequence. In Lecture 2, we shall call such a one-to-one matching a bijection.

Binary Sequences

Find the number of binary sequences having nn terms.

A binary sequence uses only 00 and 11.

By Theorem 1.6 (Number of kk-ary Sequences), the number of binary sequences of length nn is 2n.\boxed{2^n}.

Using Addition and Multiplication Together

💡 Motivation

In many counting problems, we first divide the problem into cases. Then we count each case step by step.

So a difficult problem may use both the Addition Principle and the Multiplication Principle.

Permutations

💡 Motivation

A permutation is an arrangement.

Whenever order matters, permutations appear.

For example, arranging the letters A,B,CA,B,C as ABCABC is different from arranging them as BACBAC.

The same objects are used, but the order is different.

Definition 1.3

rr-Permutation

Let A={a1,a2,…,an}A=\left\{a_1,a_2,\ldots,a_n\right\} be a set of nn distinct objects.

An rr-permutation of AA is an arrangement of any rr objects chosen from AA in a row.

The number of rr-permutations of nn distinct objects is denoted by nPr.{}^{n}P_{r}.

Example 1.14

Three-Permutations of Four Letters

Let A={a,b,c,d}.A=\left\{a,b,c,d\right\}.

Some 33-permutations of AA are abc,acb,bac,bca,cab,cba.abc, acb, bac, bca, cab, cba.

There are altogether 2424 such arrangements.

Theorem 1.8

Formula for rr-Permutations

The number of rr-permutations of nn distinct objects is nPr=n(n−1)(n−2)⋯(n−r+1).{}^{n}P_{r} = n(n-1)(n-2)\cdots(n-r+1).

Using factorial notation from Definition 1.1, this becomes nPr=n!(n−r)!.{}^{n}P_{r} = \frac{n!}{(n-r)!}.

Proof.

To form an rr-permutation, we fill rr positions.

The first position can be filled in nn ways.

The second position can be filled in n−1n-1 ways.

The third position can be filled in n−2n-2 ways.

Continuing in this way, the rr-th position can be filled in n−r+1n-r+1 ways.

By Theorem 1.4 (Multiplication Principle), nPr=n(n−1)(n−2)⋯(n−r+1).{}^{n}P_{r} = n(n-1)(n-2)\cdots(n-r+1).

Also, n!=n(n−1)(n−2)⋯(n−r+1)(n−r)!.n! = n(n-1)(n-2)\cdots(n-r+1)(n-r)!.

Dividing by (n−r)!(n-r)!, we get nPr=n!(n−r)!.{}^{n}P_{r} = \frac{n!}{(n-r)!}.

Remark

By convention, 0!=1.0!=1.

Also, nP0=1{}^{n}P_{0}=1 because there is exactly one way to arrange no object.

Finally, nPn=n!.{}^{n}P_{n}=n!.

Counting by Filling Places

💡 Motivation

Many counting problems can be solved by drawing empty boxes.

Each box represents a place to be filled.

For example, a five-letter word has five positions: aaaaa.\boxed{\phantom{a}}\quad \boxed{\phantom{a}}\quad \boxed{\phantom{a}}\quad \boxed{\phantom{a}}\quad \boxed{\phantom{a}}.

Then we count how many choices are possible for each position.

Let EE be the set of the 2626 English letters.

By Theorem 1.4 (Multiplication Principle), the total number of words is 5P2⋅21P3=(5⋅4)(21⋅20⋅19).{}^{5}P_{2}\cdot {}^{21}P_{3} = (5\cdot 4)(21\cdot 20\cdot 19).

Hence, 20⋅7980=159600.20\cdot 7980=159600.

Therefore, the answer is 159600.\boxed{159600}.

Sums of Numbers Formed from Digits

💡 Motivation

Sometimes we are asked not only to count how many numbers are formed, but also to find their sum.

The key idea I want you to remember is: Count how many times each digit appears in each place.\text{Count how many times each digit appears in each place.}

This is called the place-value frequency method.

Theorem 1.9

Place-Value Frequency Method

Suppose numbers are formed from a fixed set of digits in such a way that each digit appears equally often in a given place.

Then the total contribution of that place is number of appearances of each digit×sum of the digits×place value.\text{number of appearances of each digit} \times \text{sum of the digits} \times \text{place value}.

☠ Problem 30

Find the number of possible outcomes in a throw of nn distinct dice in which at least one die shows an odd number.

Solution.
💡 How we should think.

Count the whole sample space and subtract the complementary outcomes, using Theorem 2.10 (Principle of Complementation); the individual choices are then counted by Theorem 1.4 (Multiplication Principle).

Since the dice are distinct, outcomes are ordered. For example, for two dice, (1,2)and(2,1)(1,2)\quad \text{and}\quad (2,1) are different outcomes.

Each die has 66 possible outcomes. Therefore, by Theorem 1.4 (Multiplication Principle), the total number of outcomes when nn distinct dice are thrown is 6⋅6⋅⋯⋅6⏟n times=6n.\underbrace{6\cdot 6\cdot \cdots \cdot 6}_{n\text{ times}}=6^n.

We need at least one die to show an odd number. The opposite case is that no die shows an odd number. That means every die shows an even number.

The even numbers on a die are 2,4,6.2,4,6. So each die has 33 choices in the opposite case. Therefore, the number of outcomes in which all dice show even numbers is 3⋅3⋅⋯⋅3⏟n times=3n.\underbrace{3\cdot 3\cdot \cdots \cdot 3}_{n\text{ times}}=3^n. Hence the number of outcomes in which at least one die shows an odd number is 6n−3n.6^n-3^n. Therefore the answer is 6n−3n.\boxed{6^n-3^n}.

Remark

The method used in Problems 12, 13, 28, and 30 is the complement method. Whenever the phrase “at least one” appears, it is often easier to count the opposite case, namely “none”, and subtract from the total.

Why we count the complement rather than mark an odd die. For two dice the answer is 36−9=2736-9=27. There are 1818 outcomes with exactly one odd entry and 99 with two odd entries. An approach that first chooses “the odd die” would count each of the latter outcomes twice. The complement has no such ambiguity: each excluded outcome simply has both entries even. The same reasoning works for any positive number of dice.
