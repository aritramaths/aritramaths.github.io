Topics Covered in Lecture 9

In class we introduced generating functions from the point of view of counting. The main ideas of this lecture are:

  1. attaching a formal power series to a sequence;

  2. equality, addition, scalar multiplication, and multiplication of generating functions;

  3. the Cauchy product and its combinatorial meaning;

  4. Newton's generalized binomial expansion and the standard series (1−x)−m(1-x)^{-m};

  5. reading shifts, differences, partial sums, and weighted sequences from algebraic operations;

  6. modelling selections from a set and from a multiset;

  7. modelling distributions of identical objects into labelled boxes;

  8. handling lower and upper bounds by coefficient extraction;

  9. solving recurrence relations by generating functions;

  10. deriving the Catalan generating function;

  11. a bridge to integer partitions and exponential generating functions, developed further in Appendices C and D.

The central idea

I do not want you to think of a generating function as a mysterious function that must always be evaluated at a numerical value of xx. In most of this chapter, xx is only a position marker. The coefficient of xnx^n stores the answer for objects of size nn.

Why Encode a Sequence as a Power Series?

💡 Motivation

Suppose a0,a1,a2,…a_0,a_1,a_2,\ldots is a counting sequence. Instead of carrying infinitely many numbers separately, we package them into one formal expression: A(x)=a0+a1x+a2x2+a3x3+⋯.A(x)=a_0+a_1x+a_2x^2+a_3x^3+\cdots.

This one expression can do two jobs: it records a counting problem coefficient by coefficient,and it can transform a recurrence into an algebraic equation.\boxed{ \begin{array}{c} \text{it records a counting problem coefficient by coefficient},\\[1mm] \text{and it can transform a recurrence into an algebraic equation.} \end{array}}

The idea has a long history: power-series encodings were used by de Moivre and Euler, and the name “generating function” became standard after Laplace. For us, the important matter is the dictionary between coefficient operations and combinatorial constructions.

Definition 9.1

Ordinary Generating Function

Let (an)n≥0=(a0,a1,a2,…)(a_n)_{n\ge0}=(a_0,a_1,a_2,\ldots) be a sequence. Its ordinary generating function, abbreviated OGF, is the formal power series A(x)=∑n≥0anxn=a0+a1x+a2x2+⋯.\boxed{ A(x)=\sum_{n\ge0}a_nx^n =a_0+a_1x+a_2x^2+\cdots. }

Definition 9.2

Equality of Formal Power Series

Two formal power series A(x)=∑n≥0anxn,B(x)=∑n≥0bnxnA(x)=\sum_{n\ge0}a_nx^n, \qquad B(x)=\sum_{n\ge0}b_nx^n are equal if and only if an=bnfor every n≥0.a_n=b_n \qquad\text{for every }n\ge0.

Formal does not mean careless

The series 1+2x+3x2+4x3+⋯1+2x+3x^2+4x^3+\cdots may be manipulated as a formal series without substituting x=1x=1. The divergent numerical expression 1+2+3+4+⋯1+2+3+4+\cdots is a different question. For every fixed power of xx, formal power-series arithmetic involves only finitely many coefficient calculations.

Recall.

In Section 5.13 (Generating Functions for Distribution Problems), we already encoded bounded choices by factors such as 1+x+x2+⋯+xm.1+x+x^2+\cdots+x^m. Lecture 9 does not replace that viewpoint; it systematises it and connects it with recurrence relations.

Coefficient Extraction

Definition 9.3

Coefficient Notation

If A(x)=∑n≥0anxn,A(x)=\sum_{n\ge0}a_nx^n, then [xn]A(x)[x^n]A(x) denotes the coefficient of xnx^n in A(x)A(x). Thus [xn]A(x)=an.\boxed{[x^n]A(x)=a_n.}

For example, [x5]11−x=1,[x5]1(1−x)2=6.[x^5]\frac1{1-x}=1, \qquad [x^5]\frac1{(1-x)^2}=6.

Whenever we construct a generating function for a counting problem, the final answer usually takes the form [xn](product of simple factors).\boxed{[x^n](\text{product of simple factors}).}

Algebra of Generating Functions

Let A(x)=∑n≥0anxn,B(x)=∑n≥0bnxn.A(x)=\sum_{n\ge0}a_nx^n, \qquad B(x)=\sum_{n\ge0}b_nx^n.

Addition and scalar multiplication are coefficientwise: αA(x)+βB(x)=∑n≥0(αan+βbn)xn.\alpha A(x)+\beta B(x) = \sum_{n\ge0}(\alpha a_n+\beta b_n)x^n.

Multiplication is more interesting. Writing the first few terms, A(x)B(x)=a0b0+(a0b1+a1b0)x+(a0b2+a1b1+a2b0)x2+⋯.\begin{aligned} A(x)B(x) ={}&a_0b_0+(a_0b_1+a_1b_0)x\\ &+(a_0b_2+a_1b_1+a_2b_0)x^2+\cdots. \end{aligned}

Theorem 9.1

Cauchy Product

If A(x)=∑n≥0anxn,B(x)=∑n≥0bnxn,A(x)=\sum_{n\ge0}a_nx^n, \qquad B(x)=\sum_{n\ge0}b_nx^n, then [xn](A(x)B(x))=∑k=0nakbn−k.\boxed{ [x^n](A(x)B(x)) = \sum_{k=0}^{n}a_kb_{n-k}. } Equivalently, A(x)B(x)=∑n≥0(∑k=0nakbn−k)xn.A(x)B(x) = \sum_{n\ge0} \left(\sum_{k=0}^{n}a_kb_{n-k}\right)x^n.

Proof.

To obtain xnx^n in the product, we must multiply akxka_kx^k from the first series by bn−kxn−kb_{n-k}x^{n-k} from the second. The possible values are k=0,1,…,nk=0,1,\ldots,n. Adding these contributions gives the stated coefficient.

The combinatorial meaning of convolution

Suppose a size-nn object is made from an AA-object of size kk and a BB-object of size n−kn-k. For fixed kk there are akbn−ka_kb_{n-k} pairs. Summing over kk produces the Cauchy coefficient. Multiplication is therefore the algebraic form of joining independent components whose sizes add.

Newton's Generalized Binomial Expansion

For a parameter α\alpha, define (αr)=α(α−1)⋯(α−r+1)r!,(α0)=1.\binom{\alpha}{r} = \frac{\alpha(\alpha-1)\cdots(\alpha-r+1)}{r!}, \qquad \binom{\alpha}{0}=1.

Theorem 9.2

Generalized Binomial Theorem

Formally, and analytically whenever the series converges, (1+x)α=∑r≥0(αr)xr.\boxed{(1+x)^\alpha=\sum_{r\ge0}\binom{\alpha}{r}x^r.} In particular, for a positive integer mm, 1(1−x)m=∑r≥0(m+r−1r)xr.\boxed{ \frac1{(1-x)^m} = \sum_{r\ge0}\binom{m+r-1}{r}x^r. }

The most useful special cases are 11−x=1+x+x2+x3+⋯,\frac1{1-x}=1+x+x^2+x^3+\cdots, 1(1−x)2=1+2x+3x2+4x3+⋯,\frac1{(1-x)^2}=1+2x+3x^2+4x^3+\cdots, and 1(1−x)3=1+3x+6x2+10x3+⋯.\frac1{(1-x)^3}=1+3x+6x^2+10x^3+\cdots. More generally, [xr]1(1−x)m=(m+r−1r).[x^r]\frac1{(1-x)^m}=\binom{m+r-1}{r}.

Example 9.1

A Coefficient from an Infinite Tail

Find the coefficient of x48x^{48} in (x3+x4+x5+⋯)6.(x^3+x^4+x^5+\cdots)^6.

Solution.

Factor out the compulsory power x3x^3 from each bracket: (x3+x4+x5+⋯)6=x18(1+x+x2+⋯)6=x18(1−x)6.(x^3+x^4+x^5+\cdots)^6 =x^{18}(1+x+x^2+\cdots)^6 =\frac{x^{18}}{(1-x)^6}. Therefore we need the coefficient of x30x^{30} in (1−x)−6(1-x)^{-6}: [x30](1−x)−6=(355).[x^{30}](1-x)^{-6} = \binom{35}{5}. Hence the answer is (355).\boxed{\binom{35}{5}}.

Example 9.2

Linear and Quadratic Sequences

Find closed forms for the generating functions of an=3n+5andbn=n2.a_n=3n+5 \qquad\text{and}\qquad b_n=n^2.

Solution.

We know ∑n≥0xn=11−x,∑n≥0nxn=x(1−x)2.\sum_{n\ge0}x^n=\frac1{1-x}, \qquad \sum_{n\ge0}nx^n=\frac{x}{(1-x)^2}. Therefore ∑n≥0(3n+5)xn=3x(1−x)2+51−x=5−2x(1−x)2.\sum_{n\ge0}(3n+5)x^n = \frac{3x}{(1-x)^2}+\frac5{1-x} = \boxed{\frac{5-2x}{(1-x)^2}}.

For n2n^2, differentiate and multiply by xx: ∑n≥0n2xn=xddx(x(1−x)2)=x(1+x)(1−x)3.\sum_{n\ge0}n^2x^n =x\frac{d}{dx}\left(\frac{x}{(1-x)^2}\right) = \boxed{\frac{x(1+x)}{(1-x)^3}}.

Modelling Selections from a Set

Let S={a,b,c}S=\{a,b,c\}. For the object aa, either we do not select it, contributing 11, or we select it, contributing axax. Thus its factor is 1+ax1+ax. Similarly, (1+ax)(1+bx)(1+cx)(1+ax)(1+bx)(1+cx) contains every subset of SS: the power of xx records how many objects were selected, while the product of letters records which objects were selected.

Indeed, (1+ax)(1+bx)(1+cx)=1+(a+b+c)x+(ab+bc+ca)x2+abcx3.\begin{aligned} (1+ax)(1+bx)(1+cx) ={}&1+(a+b+c)x\\ &+(ab+bc+ca)x^2+abcx^3. \end{aligned} If only the number of selections matters, set a=b=c=1a=b=c=1: (1+x)3=1+3x+3x2+x3.(1+x)^3=1+3x+3x^2+x^3.

Theorem 9.3

Selection Polynomial for Distinct Objects

For nn distinct objects, the generating function in which the coefficient of xrx^r counts selections of exactly rr objects is (1+x)n.\boxed{(1+x)^n.}

Modelling Selections from a Multiset

If an object type is available with multiplicity mm, then we may choose 0,1,…,m0,1,\ldots,m copies. Its factor is 1+x+x2+⋯+xm.1+x+x^2+\cdots+x^m.

Theorem 9.4

One Factor per Object Type

Suppose a multiset contains mim_i copies of object type ii, for i=1,2,…,ki=1,2,\ldots,k. Then the number of ways to select exactly rr objects is [xr]∏i=1k(1+x+x2+⋯+xmi).\boxed{[x^r]\prod_{i=1}^{k}(1+x+x^2+\cdots+x^{m_i}).} If type ii is available without an upper bound, replace its factor by 1+x+x2+⋯=11−x.1+x+x^2+\cdots=\frac1{1-x}.

Solution.

Identical Objects in Labelled Boxes

Suppose rr identical objects are distributed among nn labelled boxes, and empty boxes are allowed. If box ii receives xix_i objects, then x1+x2+⋯+xn=r,xi≥0.x_1+x_2+\cdots+x_n=r, \qquad x_i\ge0. For each box the factor is 1+x+x2+⋯=(1−x)−11+x+x^2+\cdots=(1-x)^{-1}. Thus the generating function is (1−x)−n(1-x)^{-n}, and [xr]1(1−x)n=(r+n−1r).[x^r]\frac1{(1-x)^n}=\binom{r+n-1}{r}. This recovers Stars and Bars from Section 3.6 (Stars and Bars).

Same number, different languages

The number (r+n−1r)\binom{r+n-1}{r} counts nonnegative integer solutions, distributions of identical objects into labelled boxes, multisets of size rr from nn types, and the coefficient of xrx^r in (1−x)−n(1-x)^{-n}.

Three Dice with Total Fourteen

Three ordinary dice are rolled. In how many ordered outcomes is the total 1414?

Solving Recurrences by Generating Functions

The method is systematic:

  1. define A(x)=∑n≥0anxnA(x)=\sum_{n\ge0}a_nx^n;

  2. multiply the recurrence by xnx^n;

  3. sum over the range where the recurrence is valid;

  4. separate the initial terms carefully;

  5. solve the algebraic equation for A(x)A(x);

  6. extract coefficients by a standard expansion or partial fractions.

Example 9.6

The Fibonacci Generating Function

Let F0=0,F1=1,Fn=Fn−1+Fn−2.F_0=0,\qquad F_1=1,\qquad F_n=F_{n-1}+F_{n-2}. Find F(x)=∑n≥0FnxnF(x)=\sum_{n\ge0}F_nx^n.

Solution.
Tools to recall.

Definition 9.1 (ordinary generating functions)

For n≥2n\ge2, multiply the recurrence by xnx^n and sum. The three sums are F(x)−x,xF(x),x2F(x).F(x)-x, \qquad xF(x), \qquad x^2F(x). Hence F(x)−x−xF(x)−x2F(x)=0,F(x)-x-xF(x)-x^2F(x)=0, so F(x)=x1−x−x2.\boxed{F(x)=\frac{x}{1-x-x^2}}.

Example 9.7

A First-Order Non-Homogeneous Recurrence

Solve an=2an−1+1,a0=0,a_n=2a_{n-1}+1, \qquad a_0=0, using generating functions.

Solution.

Let A(x)=∑n≥0anxnA(x)=\sum_{n\ge0}a_nx^n. Summing an−2an−1=1a_n-2a_{n-1}=1 over n≥1n\ge1 gives A(x)−2xA(x)=x1−x.A(x)-2xA(x)=\frac{x}{1-x}. Thus A(x)=x(1−x)(1−2x)=−11−x+11−2x.A(x)=\frac{x}{(1-x)(1-2x)} =-\frac1{1-x}+\frac1{1-2x}. Therefore an=2n−1.\boxed{a_n=2^n-1}.

The Catalan Generating Function

Recall from Appendix A that C0=1,Cn=∑k=0n−1CkCn−1−k.C_0=1, \qquad C_n=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}C_kC_{n-1-k}. Let C(x)=∑n≥0CnxnC(x)=\sum_{n\ge0}C_nx^n. The convolution becomes C(x)−1=xC(x)2.C(x)-1=xC(x)^2. Thus xC(x)2−C(x)+1=0,xC(x)^2-C(x)+1=0, and the branch with constant term 11 is C(x)=1−1−4x2x.\boxed{C(x)=\frac{1-\sqrt{1-4x}}{2x}}. Using the generalized binomial expansion gives Cn=1n+1(2nn).\boxed{C_n=\frac1{n+1}\binom{2n}{n}}.

Remark

This is the central example showing why generating functions are more than convenient notation: a nonlinear recurrence becomes a quadratic equation.

Ordinary versus Exponential Generating Functions

Ordinary generating functions are naturally suited to size contributions and integer solution problems. For labelled objects, factorials often appear in the bookkeeping, which motivates the exponential generating function ∑n≥0anxnn!.\sum_{n\ge0}a_n\frac{x^n}{n!}. The basic dictionary, Stirling and Bell-number examples, and the oscillating-permutation enrichment are placed in Appendix D.

Integer partitions, Ferrers diagrams, Euler's distinct-parts theorem, and conjugation are developed in Appendix C.

Key Lessons from Lecture 9

Remark

  1. A generating function is a sequence written as a formal power series.

  2. Equality means equality coefficient by coefficient.

  3. Products encode convolution and independent components whose sizes add.

  4. The expansion (1−x)−m(1-x)^{-m} is the generating-function form of Stars and Bars.

  5. For selections and distributions, use one factor for each type, box, or variable.

  6. The exponent records total size; the coefficient records the number of ways.

  7. Lower bounds contribute compulsory powers of xx; upper bounds truncate a factor.

  8. Recurrences become algebraic equations after multiplying by xnx^n and summing.
