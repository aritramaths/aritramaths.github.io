Concept of Infinitesimals

Read this lecture online, then continue to the next chapter. Figures and exercises are being adapted separately for the web edition.

We use the standard limits of Lecture 11 and the ideas about one-sided limits and squeezing from Lecture 12.

What is an infinitesimal?

Motivation 13.1

Imagine two machines. When you give both machines the input \(x\), the first returns \(x\) and the second returns \(x^2\). Try \(x=0.1\): the answers are \(0.1\) and \(0.01\). Try \(x=0.001\): the answers are \(0.001\) and \(0.000001\). Both outputs are heading towards zero. But each time you make the input ten times smaller, the second output becomes a hundred times smaller. The two limits are the same; the ways of reaching them are different.

The word infinitesimal in this lecture describes a function at a specified approach. There is no smallest positive real number: given any \(c>0\), the number \(c/2\) is positive and smaller. The constant function \(f(x)=0.000001\) is small in everyday language, but it does not tend to zero. On the other hand, \(x-2\) is infinitesimal as \(x\to2\), even though it takes large values far from \(2\).

Definition 13.1

Infinitesimal function

A real-valued function \(\alpha\) is infinitesimal at a specified approach if its limit there is \(0\). For example, as \(x\to a\) this means \[\forall\varepsilon>0\ \exists\delta>0:\quad 0<|x-a|<\delta\ \Longrightarrow\ |\alpha(x)|<\varepsilon .\] As \(x\to+\infty\) it means \[\forall\varepsilon>0\ \exists M>0:\quad x>M\ \Longrightarrow\ |\alpha(x)|<\varepsilon .\] The implications are tested at inputs in the domain of \(\alpha\). One-sided approaches and \(x\to-\infty\) use the corresponding input conditions.

At a finite point, \(a\) must be a limit point of the domain so that there are inputs to test arbitrarily close to it. For \(x\to+\infty\) the domain must contain arbitrarily large inputs. In either case, the definition asks us to get the output into the band \((-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)\) and keep it there for all sufficiently close inputs, or for all sufficiently large inputs, respectively. The output can approach zero from above, from below, or by changing sign; it may even be exactly zero at some inputs. The value at \(a\), if there is one, plays no part in the limit. For instance, \(1/x\) is infinitesimal as \(x\to+\infty\) because \(x>1/\varepsilon\) implies \(|1/x|<\varepsilon\).

Historical note 13.1

In his Cours d’analyse (1821), Chapter II, Augustin-Louis Cauchy described an infinitesimal as a variable quantity tending to zero. G. H. Hardy later asked readers to pay attention to how fast quantities change. His Orders of Infinity (1910) built on the work of Paul du Bois-Reymond and supplied detailed proofs. Here is the link with our two machines: as , \(t^2\) outgrows \(t\), since \(t^2/t=t\to+\infty\). Turn both outputs upside down: \((1/t^2)/(1/t)=1/t\to0\). A comparison of growth has become a comparison of infinitesimals.

Infinite functions and reciprocals

A function is infinite in magnitude at an approach if \(|f(x)|\to+\infty\). This says that for every height \(H>0\), all inputs sufficiently far along the approach satisfy \(|f(x)|>H\). If \(f(x)>H\) eventually for every \(H\), we write \(f(x)\to+\infty\). If \(f(x)<-H\) eventually for every \(H\), we write \(f(x)\to-\infty\). These last two statements specify a direction on the output axis.

Theorem 13.1

The reciprocal turns large magnitudes into small ones

Suppose \(f\) is nonzero at all inputs sufficiently far along a specified approach. Then \[\left|f(x)\right|\longrightarrow+\infty \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \frac{1}{f(x)}\longrightarrow0 .\]

Show proof

Proof. If \(|f(x)|\to+\infty\), then for any \(\varepsilon>0\) we eventually have \(|f(x)|>1/\varepsilon\). Therefore \(|1/f(x)|<\varepsilon\). Conversely, if \(1/f(x)\to0\), fix any \(H>0\) and apply the limit definition with \(\varepsilon=1/H\). Eventually \(0<|1/f(x)|<1/H\), which gives \(|f(x)|>H\). ◻

Thus the reciprocal of an infinitesimal \(\alpha\) is infinite in magnitude provided \(\alpha\) is eventually nonzero. If \(\alpha\) is eventually positive, \(1/\alpha\to+\infty\); if it is eventually negative, \(1/\alpha\to-\infty\). For \(\alpha(x)=x\) as \(x\to0\), the reciprocal has magnitude tending to infinity, but it tends to \(-\infty\) from the left and \(+\infty\) from the right. There is no two-sided signed infinite limit.

Warning 13.1

For \(x\to0\) the function \(f(x)=\sin(1/x)/x\) is unbounded near \(0\): at \(x_n=1/(2\pi n+\pi/2)\) its magnitude is \(1/x_n\to+\infty\). But at \(y_n=1/(2\pi n)\) it is zero, so \(|f(x)|\) does not tend to infinity. A large value somewhere in every neighbourhood is different from large values everywhere in a sufficiently small deleted neighbourhood.

Arithmetic with infinitesimals

Theorem 13.2

Finite sums, products, and bounded factors

At a common approach on a common domain:

  1. A sum of finitely many infinitesimals is infinitesimal.

  2. A product of finitely many infinitesimals is infinitesimal.

  3. If \(\alpha\) is infinitesimal and \(g\) is bounded near the approach, then \(g\alpha\) is infinitesimal.

Show proof

Proof. The sum and product laws give \(0+0=0\) and \(0\cdot0=0\) for two infinitesimals. Applying the same laws repeatedly gives (i) and (ii) for any finite number of terms.

For (iii), boundedness near the approach means \(|g(x)|\le B\) for some fixed \(B\ge0\) and for all sufficiently close inputs (or all sufficiently large inputs in a limit at \(+\infty\)). Given \(\varepsilon>0\), go far enough along the approach for that bound to hold and for \(|\alpha(x)|<\varepsilon/(B+1)\). Then \[|g(x)\alpha(x)| \le B|\alpha(x)| \le (B+1)|\alpha(x)| <\varepsilon.\] This proof does not require \(g\) to have a limit. ◻

Example 13.1

Two ways to show that a function tends to zero

Show that each function is infinitesimal at the indicated approach: \[\text{(a)}\quad \frac{2x-4}{x^2+5}\quad(x\to2), \qquad \text{(b)}\quad (x-1)^2\sin^3\!\left(\frac1{x-1}\right) \quad(x\to1).\]

Explanation 13.1

(a) The numerator tends to \(0\), while the denominator tends to \(9\ne0\). The quotient law gives limit \(0\). We can also see the definition directly: \[\left|\frac{2x-4}{x^2+5}\right| =\frac{2|x-2|}{x^2+5}\le\frac25|x-2|.\] So \(0<|x-2|<5\varepsilon/2\) makes the output magnitude less than \(\varepsilon\).

(b) The factor \((x-1)^2\) tends to zero. The other factor may oscillate, but its magnitude is at most \(1\) whenever \(x\ne1\). Thus \[\left|(x-1)^2\sin^3\!\left(\frac1{x-1}\right)\right| \le |x-1|^2.\] The choice \(\delta=\sqrt{\varepsilon}\) proves the limit directly. This is the bounded-factor argument with bound \(1\).

Example 13.2

An oscillation squeezed into a narrowing band

Find \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to0}x\sin(1/x)\).

Explanation 13.2

The sine factor keeps changing as \(1/x\) grows in magnitude. Its values always stay between \(-1\) and \(1\). Hence \[-|x|\le x\sin(1/x)\le |x|\qquad(x\ne0).\] Both outer functions tend to \(0\), so the squeeze theorem gives limit \(0\). Equivalently, \(0<|x|<\varepsilon\) forces the magnitude of the middle expression below \(\varepsilon\). Even a rapidly changing sine factor cannot escape the shrinking band.

Warning 13.2

If both \(\alpha\to0\) and \(g\to+\infty\), the product need not be infinitesimal. For \(x\to+\infty\), take \(\alpha(x)=1/x\) and \(g(x)=\sqrt{x}\), \(x\), or \(x^2\). Their products tend to \(0\), equal \(1\), or tend to \(+\infty\), respectively.

Comparing the rates of approach to zero

Motivation 13.2

Suppose two functions approach zero. At each input where the denominator is nonzero, their ratio asks: How big is the first output, measured in units of the second? That is the question which distinguishes \(x^2\) from \(x\) near zero, even though both limits are zero.

Definition 13.2

Higher order, same order, and lower order

Let \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) be infinitesimal at the same approach, with \(\beta\) eventually nonzero on the common domain.

  1. If \(\alpha/\beta\to0\), \(\alpha\) is of higher order than \(\beta\). We write \(\alpha=o(\beta)\) (read: little \(o\) of \(\beta\)).

  2. If \(\alpha/\beta\to c\) for a finite \(c\ne0\), they are of the same order. If \(c=1\), they are also equivalent, written \(\alpha\sim\beta\).

  3. If \(|\alpha/\beta|\to+\infty\), \(\alpha\) is of lower order than \(\beta\). In this case \(\alpha\) is eventually nonzero and \(\beta=o(\alpha)\).

In the first case, \(\alpha\) eventually fits within any prescribed positive fraction of \(\beta\) in magnitude. In the third case the roles are reversed. Thus a higher-order infinitesimal is the one which becomes smaller in this comparison. In the middle case the outputs stay at a definite relative scale. For instance, \(-2x\) and \(x\) have the same order near zero because their ratio is \(-2\), but they are not equivalent.

Warning 13.3

Here eventually nonzero means nonzero at every sufficiently close input in the deleted domain, or at every sufficiently large input for a limit at \(+\infty\). If a denominator has zeros arbitrarily far along the approach, a quotient on a smaller domain is a different limit question. The zero function is an infinitesimal; it cannot serve as a denominator in these comparisons. Also, a ratio can tend to infinity in magnitude while taking opposite signs on the two sides of the approach point. Equivalence always requires the signed ratio to tend to \(1\).

Measuring an order by a power

If \(\beta\) is an infinitesimal, is eventually nonzero, and \(m\) is a positive integer, we say \(\alpha\) has order \(m\) relative to \(\beta\) when \[\frac{\alpha(x)}{\beta(x)^m}\longrightarrow c \quad\text{for some finite }c\ne0.\] This gives both a power and a leading factor: \(\alpha\sim c\beta^m\). With an eventually positive reference \(\beta\), the same definition works with a real power \(p>0\).

For a two-sided approach \(x\to0\), the always-positive reference on the deleted domain is \(|x|\). In particular we say that \(\alpha\) has magnitude order \(p\) relative to \(x\) if \[\frac{|\alpha(x)|}{|x|^p}\longrightarrow C, \qquad 0<C<+\infty .\] This tells us about size and allows fractional orders without trying to raise negative numbers to arbitrary real powers. It does not by itself give a signed equivalence. The example \(\alpha(x)=|x|\) has magnitude order \(1\) relative to \(x\); its signed ratio to \(x\) takes the values \(-1\) and \(1\).

Three comparisons with the reference \(x\)

We will use \(\sin u/u\to1\) and \(\cos u\to1\) as \(u\to0\), with angles in radians. In particular \(\tan u/u=(\sin u/u)/\cos u\to1\).

Example 13.3

Faster, slower, and the same order

Compare each infinitesimal with \(x\) as \(x\to0\): \[\text{(a)}\quad\tan(x^3),\qquad \text{(b)}\quad\sqrt[3]{\sin^2 x},\qquad \text{(c)}\quad\sqrt{9+x}-3.\]

Explanation 13.3

(a) For nonzero \(x\) close to zero, \[\frac{\tan(x^3)}{x}= \frac{\tan(x^3)}{x^3}\,x^2\longrightarrow1\cdot0=0.\] It is of higher order than \(x\). The first factor also gives the more precise statement \(\tan(x^3)\sim x^3\).

(b) Because the cube root of \(\sin^2 x\) is \(|\sin x|^{2/3}\), we have \[\frac{\sqrt[3]{\sin^2 x}}{|x|^{2/3}} =\left|\frac{\sin x}{x}\right|^{2/3}\longrightarrow1, \qquad \left|\frac{\sqrt[3]{\sin^2 x}}{x}\right| =\left|\frac{\sin x}{x}\right|^{2/3}\frac1{|x|^{1/3}} \longrightarrow+\infty.\] This gives magnitude order \(2/3\) and lower order than \(x\). The signed quotient by \(x\) tends to \(-\infty\) on the left and \(+\infty\) on the right. The absolute value in the comparison matters.

(c) Rationalize first so that the factor \(x\) appears: \[\frac{\sqrt{9+x}-3}{x} =\frac{(9+x)-9}{x(\sqrt{9+x}+3)} =\frac1{\sqrt{9+x}+3}\longrightarrow\frac16.\] The two infinitesimals have the same order, and the more precise equivalence is \(\sqrt{9+x}-3\sim x/6\).

Example 13.4

When the first visible terms cancel

Find the order of smallness relative to \(\alpha\) as \(\alpha\to0\): \[\text{(a)}\quad\beta=\cos\alpha-\cos(2\alpha),\qquad \text{(b)}\quad\beta=\tan\alpha-\sin\alpha .\]

Explanation 13.4

(a) By the difference-of-cosines identity, \[\cos\alpha-\cos(2\alpha)=2\sin(3\alpha/2)\sin(\alpha/2).\] Now each sine contributes one power of \(\alpha\): \[\frac{\beta}{\alpha^2} =\frac32\, \frac{\sin(3\alpha/2)}{3\alpha/2} \frac{\sin(\alpha/2)}{\alpha/2} \longrightarrow\frac32.\] The order is \(2\), and \(\beta\sim(3/2)\alpha^2\).

(b) First factor out \(\sin\alpha\). The expression left over contains \(1-\cos\alpha=2\sin^2(\alpha/2)\): \[\frac{\tan\alpha-\sin\alpha}{\alpha^3} =\frac{\sin\alpha}{\alpha} \frac{1-\cos\alpha}{\alpha^2}\frac1{\cos\alpha} =\frac12\, \frac{\sin\alpha}{\alpha} \left(\frac{\sin(\alpha/2)}{\alpha/2}\right)^2 \frac1{\cos\alpha} \longrightarrow\frac12.\] The order is \(3\), and \(\beta\sim\alpha^3/2\). In both parts the identities expose the powers hidden by the subtraction.

Example 13.5

When a ratio has no stable scale

Compare \(\alpha(x)=x\) and \(\beta(x)=x\cos(1/x)\) as \(x\to0\).

Explanation 13.5

Both are infinitesimal, since \(|\beta(x)|\le |x|\). Nevertheless \(\beta(x)/\alpha(x)=\cos(1/x)\) keeps oscillating. Along \(x_n=1/(2\pi n)\) the ratio is \(1\), and along \(y_n=1/(2\pi n+\pi/2)\) it is \(0\). These sequences both tend to zero, so the signed ratio has no limit, and neither does its magnitude. There is no higher, same, or lower order comparison in the sense of Definition [defn:inf13-orders]. The inequality \(|\beta(x)|\le|x|\) still gives useful information: it is a bound rather than a ratio limit. Notice that \(\beta(y_n)=0\); reversing the quotient does not give a denominator that is eventually nonzero.

Three orders in one cube

Example 13.6

A cube whose edge shrinks

Let \(s>0\) be a cube’s side length. As \(s\to0^+\), compare the orders of its space diagonal \(d\), total surface area \(S\), and volume \(V\) relative to \(s\).

Explanation 13.6

The diagonal of a square face has length \(\sqrt{2}\,s\). Applying Pythagoras again with the perpendicular edge gives \(d=\sqrt{(\sqrt2\,s)^2+s^2}=\sqrt3\,s\). The six square faces have total area \(S=6s^2\), and the volume is \(V=s^3\).

Comparing functions that grow without bound

The ratio test is useful for large magnitudes too. For functions whose magnitudes both tend to infinity, \(|f/g|\to0\) means that \(f\) has lower growth order than \(g\), while \(|f/g|\to+\infty\) means that \(f\) has higher growth order. A finite nonzero signed ratio limit gives the same growth order, and ratio limit \(1\) gives equivalence. For example, \(x^2\) is a higher-order infinitesimal than \(x\) near zero, but it has higher growth order than \(x\) at \(+\infty\). The ratio is \(x\) in both cases; the approach decides what it does.

Example 13.7

Three comparisons at infinity

For \(x\to+\infty\) compare \(f\) with \(g\) in each pair: \[\begin{array}{c|c|c} & f(x)&g(x)\\ \hline \text{(a)}&3x^2+2x+5 &2x^3+2x-1\\ \text{(b)}&2x^2+3x &(x+2)^2\\ \text{(c)}&\sqrt[3]{x+a} &\sqrt[3]{x}\quad (a\text{ fixed}) \end{array}\]

Explanation 13.7

In all three pairs the functions are eventually positive and tend to \(+\infty\). For the polynomials this follows by factoring out the highest power, leaving a factor tending to a positive constant. Both cube roots grow without bound because their arguments do.

(a) Divide numerator and denominator of \(f/g\) by \(x^3\): \[\frac{3x^2+2x+5}{2x^3+2x-1} =\frac{3/x+2/x^2+5/x^3}{2+2/x^2-1/x^3}\longrightarrow0 .\] So \(f\) has lower growth order than \(g\).

(b) Divide by \(x^2\): \[\frac{2x^2+3x}{(x+2)^2} =\frac{2+3/x}{(1+2/x)^2}\longrightarrow2 .\] The growth order is the same, and \(f\sim 2g\).

(c) The ratio is \[\frac{\sqrt[3]{x+a}}{\sqrt[3]{x}} =\sqrt[3]{1+a/x}\longrightarrow1 .\] These functions are equivalent at \(+\infty\): adding a fixed \(a\) inside the cube root changes the output by a vanishing relative amount.

Equivalent Infinitesimals

Motivation 13.3

Suppose someone tells you that \(x+x^2\) is almost \(x\) when \(x\) is near zero. The difference is \(x^2\), which tends to zero. But \(2x\) also differs from \(x\) by a quantity that tends to zero, and \(2x\) is always twice \(x\). The useful question is: how large is the error compared with \(x\) itself?

Definition 13.3

Equivalent Infinitesimals

Let \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) be infinitesimals for the same approach, with \(\beta\) eventually nonzero. We write \[\alpha(x)\sim\beta(x) \qquad\text{if}\qquad \lim\frac{\alpha(x)}{\beta(x)}=1.\] The quotient is signed. It lies between \(1/2\) and \(3/2\) eventually, so \(\alpha\) is also eventually nonzero and has the same sign as \(\beta\) there.

The limit is taken on the common domain with the indicated approach. Here eventually means sufficiently close to the point of approach, excluding that point itself, or sufficiently far out for a limit at infinity. For example, as \(x\to0\), \[\frac{x+x^2}{x}=1+x\longrightarrow1, \qquad \frac{2x}{x}=2, \qquad \frac{x^2}{x}=x\longrightarrow0.\] All three numerators tend to zero. Only the first is equivalent to \(x\).

Theorem 13.3

Equivalent Means Negligible Relative Error

For eventually nonzero infinitesimals \(\alpha,\beta\), \[\alpha\sim\beta \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \alpha-\beta=o(\beta) \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \alpha-\beta=o(\alpha).\]

Show proof

Proof. Since \((\alpha-\beta)/\beta=\alpha/\beta-1\), the first ratio on the right tends to zero exactly when \(\alpha/\beta\to1\). Likewise, \((\alpha-\beta)/\alpha=1-\beta/\alpha\) tends to zero exactly when \(\beta/\alpha\to1\). By the reciprocal law, this is equivalent to \(\alpha/\beta\to1\). ◻

The same reciprocal law shows that equivalence is symmetric. It is reflexive for eventually nonzero infinitesimals because \(\alpha/\alpha=1\). It is transitive: if \(\alpha\sim\beta\) and \(\beta\sim\gamma\), then \[\frac{\alpha}{\gamma} =\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\frac{\beta}{\gamma} \longrightarrow1\cdot1=1.\] So two infinitesimals equivalent to the same simpler expression are equivalent to each other.

When We Can Replace an Infinitesimal

Theorem 13.4

Replacement in Products and Quotients

Suppose \(\alpha\sim\gamma\) and \(\beta\sim\delta\). Then \(\alpha\beta\sim\gamma\delta\). Moreover, if \(\gamma/\delta\) has a finite limit or tends to \(+\infty\) or \(-\infty\), then \(\alpha/\beta\) has the same limit.

Show proof

Proof. The ratio of the products is \((\alpha/\gamma)(\beta/\delta)\to1\). For the quotient, write \[\frac{\alpha}{\beta} =\frac{\gamma}{\delta} \underbrace{\frac{\alpha}{\gamma}\frac{\delta}{\beta}}_{r(x)}.\] Here \(r(x)\to1\). If \(\gamma/\delta\) has a finite limit \(L\), the product law gives \(L\cdot1=L\). If \(\gamma/\delta\to+\infty\), then for any \(M>0\) we eventually have \(\gamma/\delta>2M\) and \(r(x)>1/2\), so the product exceeds \(M\). If \(\gamma/\delta\to-\infty\), the inequalities \(\gamma/\delta<-2M\) and \(r(x)>1/2\) give a product less than \(-M\). All the divisions are valid eventually by the definition of equivalence. ◻

Another useful case is a factor \(f(x)\) with a finite nonzero limit \(k\). If \(\alpha\) is an eventually nonzero infinitesimal, then \(f\alpha\) is an infinitesimal by the product law, and \[\frac{f(x)\alpha(x)}{k\alpha(x)}=\frac{f(x)}k\longrightarrow1. \qquad\text{Thus } f(x)\alpha(x)\sim k\alpha(x).\]

Theorem 13.5

Keeping the Dominant Term in a Sum

If \(\alpha\) is an eventually nonzero infinitesimal and \(\beta=o(\alpha)\), then \(\alpha+\beta\sim\alpha\). The same statement holds when \(\beta\) is a finite sum of terms that are each \(o(\alpha)\).

Show proof

Proof. Since \(\beta/\alpha\to0\), the product \(\beta=\alpha(\beta/\alpha)\) tends to zero. Now \[\frac{\alpha+\beta}{\alpha}=1+\frac{\beta}{\alpha} \longrightarrow1.\] For a finite sum, divide every term by \(\alpha\) and use the sum law: each ratio tends to zero, so their sum does too. ◻

Warning 13.4

Since \(x+x^2\sim x\), it is safe to use \(x\) in place of \(x+x^2\) as a factor in a product or quotient limit. But in \((x+x^2)-x\), the two copies of \(x\) cancel, and the entire answer is \(x^2\). For example, \(((x+x^2)-x)/x^2=1\); replacing the first term by \(x\) would give \(0\). If terms of the same size can cancel, simplify the whole difference before choosing an equivalent. Writing \(\alpha\sim0\) is never a way out: the ratio to zero is undefined.

A Short List of Equivalents

The standard limits from Lecture 11 and the limit laws give the following comparisons, with angles in radians.

Formula 13.1

Equivalents as \(u\to0\)

\[\begin{array}{@{}r@{\;\sim\;}l@{\qquad}r@{\;\sim\;}l@{}} \sin u & u & \tan u & u\\[2pt] 1-\cos u & \tfrac12u^2 & \arcsin u & u\\[2pt] \arctan u & u & \ln(1+u) & u\\[2pt] e^u-1 & u & a^u-1 & u\ln a\\[2pt] (1+u)^p-1 & pu & \sqrt[n]{1+u}-1 & u/n \end{array}\] Here \(a>0\), \(a\ne1\), \(p\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}\) and \(n\) is a positive integer. Take \(u\ne0\) near zero, on the natural real domain of the expression. In particular \(1+u>0\) for the logarithm and the real power.

For \(\sin u\), \(\ln(1+u)\), and \(e^u-1\), dividing by \(u\) gives exactly the standard limits. For the tangent and cosine rows, write \[\frac{\tan u}{u}=\frac{\sin u}{u}\frac1{\cos u} \longrightarrow1, \qquad \frac{1-\cos u}{u^2/2} =\left(\frac{\sin(u/2)}{u/2}\right)^2 \longrightarrow1.\] In the first limit \(\cos u\to1\), so its reciprocal is defined near zero.

For the inverse sine, let \(t=\arcsin u\) on the usual branch \([-\pi/2,\pi/2]\). For \(0<\eta<\pi/2\), the condition \(|u|<\sin\eta\) implies \(-\eta<t<\eta\) by strict increase of sine on that branch. Thus \(t\to0\) as \(u\to0\). Since \(u=\sin t\), for \(u\ne0\) we have \[\frac{\arcsin u}{u}=\frac{t}{\sin t}\longrightarrow1.\] For \(t=\arctan u\), use the increasing branch of tangent on \((-\pi/2,\pi/2)\). If \(|u|<\tan\eta\), then \(|t|<\eta\), so again \(t\to0\), and \((\arctan u)/u=t/\tan t\to1\).

For \(a>0\), \(a\ne1\), use \(a^u=e^{u\ln a}\). The ratio \((a^u-1)/(u\ln a)\) is the exponential standard ratio at input \(u\ln a\), which is nonzero for \(u\ne0\) and tends to zero. For a real \(p\ne0\), put \(v=p\ln(1+u)\). Then \(v\to0\), \(v\ne0\) for \(u\ne0\), and \[\frac{(1+u)^p-1}{pu} =\frac{e^v-1}{v}\frac{\ln(1+u)}{u} \longrightarrow1\cdot1=1.\] Taking \(p=1/n\) gives the root formula. If \(a=1\) or \(p=0\), the corresponding difference is identically zero; there is no equivalence to a zero comparison function.

In the examples below we use the list with inputs such as \(u=5x\) or \(u=\cos x-1\). In each use, the input approaches zero, stays in the required domain, and is nonzero sufficiently near the point of approach.

Worked Comparisons and Limit Calculations

Example 13.8

Proving equivalence by simplifying the ratio

Show that \[1-\frac1{\sqrt{1+x}}\sim\frac x2,\qquad 1-\frac1{1+x}\sim x \qquad (x\to0),\] and that \[\sin\!\sqrt{x\sqrt{x}}\sim\sqrt{x^2+\sqrt{x^3}} \qquad (x\to0^+).\]

Explanation 13.8

For the first pair, combine the terms and rationalise: \[1-\frac1{\sqrt{1+x}} =\frac{\sqrt{1+x}-1}{\sqrt{1+x}} =\frac{x}{\sqrt{1+x}\,(\sqrt{1+x}+1)}.\] For \(x\ne0\), the ratio to \(x/2\) tends to \(2/(1\cdot2)=1\). For the second pair, \(1-1/(1+x)=x/(1+x)\), so the ratio to \(x\) tends to \(1\).

For the last pair, the square roots restrict us to \(x>0\) in the approach. The argument of the sine is \(\sqrt{x\sqrt{x}}=x^{3/4}\), hence \(\sin\sqrt{x\sqrt{x}}\sim x^{3/4}\). In the other expression, factor \(x^{3/2}\) inside the outer root: \[\sqrt{x^2+\sqrt{x^3}}=x^{3/4}\sqrt{1+\sqrt{x}} \sim x^{3/4}.\] Since both are equivalent to \(x^{3/4}\), they are equivalent to each other.

Example 13.9

Finding the dominant term

Find a simpler equivalent for each expression as \(t\to0\): \[3\sin t-5t^3, \qquad (1-\cos t)^2+16t^3+5t^4+6t^5.\]

Explanation 13.9

Divide the first expression by \(3t\): \[\frac{3\sin t-5t^3}{3t} =\frac{\sin t}{t}-\frac53t^2\longrightarrow1.\] So the first expression is equivalent to \(3t\).

In the second expression, \((1-\cos t)^2\sim t^4/4\). The first term written is therefore smaller in size than the cubic term \(16t^3\). Indeed, \[\frac{(1-\cos t)^2}{16t^3} =\frac{t}{64}\left(\frac{1-\cos t}{t^2/2}\right)^2 \longrightarrow0.\] The other two ratios, \(5t^4/(16t^3)=5t/16\) and \(6t^5/(16t^3)=3t^2/8\), also tend to zero. Thus the second expression is equivalent to \(16t^3\).

Example 13.10

A sine divided by a logarithm

Find \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to0}\frac{\sin(5x)}{\ln(1+4x)}\).

Explanation 13.10

Near zero the logarithm is defined, and it is zero only at \(x=0\). We have \(\sin(5x)\sim5x\) and \(\ln(1+4x)\sim4x\). Both are separate factors of the quotient. The replacement theorem now gives \[\lim_{x\to0}\frac{\sin(5x)}{\ln(1+4x)} =\lim_{x\to0}\frac{5x}{4x}=\frac54.\]

Example 13.11

Nested small inputs

Evaluate \[\lim_{x\to0}\frac{\ln(\cos x)}{\sqrt[4]{1+x^2}-1}, \qquad \lim_{x\to0}\frac{\sqrt{1+x+x^2}-1}{\sin(4x)}.\]

Explanation 13.11

For the first limit, \(\cos x>0\) near zero. Use the logarithm with input \(u=\cos x-1\), then the cosine equivalent: \[\ln(\cos x)=\ln(1+(\cos x-1))\sim\cos x-1\sim-\frac{x^2}{2}.\] The fourth-root formula, with input \(x^2\), gives \(\sqrt[4]{1+x^2}-1\sim x^2/4\). Hence the quotient tends to \((-1/2)/(1/4)=-2\). The sign matters: for small nonzero \(x\), the logarithm is negative and the denominator positive.

For the second limit, the square-root input is \(u=x+x^2\). Since \(u\sim x\), we get \[\sqrt{1+x+x^2}-1\sim\frac{x+x^2}{2}\sim\frac{x}{2}.\] Also \(\sin(4x)\sim4x\). The quotient tends to \((1/2)/4=1/8\).

Example 13.12

A sum in the numerator

Evaluate \[\lim_{x\to0}\frac{\sin(2x)+(\arcsin x)^2-(\arctan x)^2}{3x}.\]

Explanation 13.12

The denominator is a constant times \(x\), so compare each squared term to \(x\). For example, \[\frac{(\arcsin x)^2}{x} =x\left(\frac{\arcsin x}{x}\right)^2\longrightarrow0.\] The same computation with \(\arctan x\) gives zero. We do not need an equivalent for the difference of the squares: after division by \(x\), both terms tend to zero separately. The sine term satisfies \(\sin(2x)/(3x)=(2/3)\sin(2x)/(2x)\to2/3\). By the sum law the required limit is \(2/3\).

Example 13.13

A quotient of several factors

Evaluate \[\lim_{x\to0^+} \frac{\sin(\sqrt[3]{x})\,\ln(1+3x)} {(\arctan\sqrt{x})^2\bigl(e^{5\sqrt[3]{x}}-1\bigr)}.\]

Explanation 13.13

The \(\sqrt{x}\) in the denominator makes this a right-hand limit at zero on the real domain. Compare the factors: \[\sin(\sqrt[3]{x})\sim\sqrt[3]{x},\quad \ln(1+3x)\sim3x,\quad (\arctan\sqrt{x})^2\sim x,\quad e^{5\sqrt[3]{x}}-1\sim5\sqrt[3]{x}.\] The square comparison follows by multiplying \(\arctan\sqrt{x}\sim\sqrt{x}\) by itself. Thus the numerator is equivalent to \(3x\sqrt[3]{x}\) and the denominator to \(5x\sqrt[3]{x}\). Both comparisons are nonzero for \(x>0\). The limit is \(3/5\).

Approximations with an Error Bound

Motivation 13.4

The equivalence \(\sqrt{1+h}-1\sim h/2\) suggests an approximation you can do in your head: \(\sqrt{1+h}\approx 1+h/2\). A limit tells us why it works as \(h\to0\). To use it at a particular number, we can go one step further and calculate how far off it is.

Example 13.14

Estimating two square roots

Approximate \(\sqrt{1.02}\) and \(\sqrt{0.994}\) using \(\sqrt{1+h}\approx1+h/2\), and bound the absolute errors.

Explanation 13.14

Use \(h=0.02\) and \(h=-0.006\) to get \(1.01\) and \(0.997\), respectively. To get a bound, first note that \(\sqrt{1+h}-1=h/(\sqrt{1+h}+1)\) for \(h>-1\). It follows that \[\begin{aligned} 1+\frac h2-\sqrt{1+h} &=\frac h2-\frac{h}{\sqrt{1+h}+1}\\ &=\frac{h(\sqrt{1+h}-1)}{2(\sqrt{1+h}+1)} =\frac{h^2}{2(\sqrt{1+h}+1)^2}. \end{aligned}\] This quantity is nonnegative even for \(h<0\). Thus the estimate lies above the true root, and the displayed quantity is its absolute error.

For \(h=0.02\), the denominator is greater than \(8\), so \[0<1.01-\sqrt{1.02}<\frac{(0.02)^2}{8}=0.00005.\] In particular \(1.00995<\sqrt{1.02}<1.01\).

For \(h=-0.006\), the denominator is less than \(8\). However, \((0.996)^2=0.992016<0.994\), so \(\sqrt{0.994}>0.996\). This gives the upper bound \[0<0.997-\sqrt{0.994} <\frac{(0.006)^2}{2(1.996)^2} <0.00000452.\] The last inequality follows by multiplying by \(2(1.996)^2\): \(0.00000452\cdot2(1.996)^2>0.000036=(0.006)^2\). In particular \(0.99699548<\sqrt{0.994}<0.997\).

The actual errors are about \(4.9506\cdot10^{-5}\) and \(4.5136\cdot10^{-6}\), respectively. As \(h\to0\) from either side, the exact error divided by \(h^2/8\) tends to \(1\). The value \(h^2/8\) gives the leading size of the error; it is an upper bound for \(h\ge0\).

Method 13.1

For a product or quotient, look for an equivalent for each factor. In a sum, compare the terms to a candidate dominant term: if their ratios to it tend to zero, they can be dropped for the purpose of equivalence. If the leading terms cancel, use an identity, a factorisation, or a rationalisation to expose the smaller quantity. When a numerical estimate needs a guaranteed accuracy, bound the error at the specific input.
