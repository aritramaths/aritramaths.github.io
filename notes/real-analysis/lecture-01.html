Real Numbers, Sets, and Completeness

Read this lecture online, then continue to the next chapter. Figures and exercises are being adapted separately for the web edition.

Why Study Real Numbers?

Motivation 1.1

Human beings first needed numbers for counting. Later, measurement forced us to use fractions. Geometry then produced lengths that cannot be written as fractions at all. The real number system is the number system large enough to contain all these quantities and complete enough to support limits and calculus.

The most familiar number systems appear because different questions require different kinds of answers.

  • The natural numbers \(\mathbb{N}=\{1,2,3,\ldots\}\) answer questions such as “How many objects are there?”

  • The integers \(\mathbb{Z}=\{\ldots,-2,-1,0,1,2,\ldots\}\) allow subtraction even when the result is negative.

  • The rational numbers \[\mathbb{Q}=\left\{\frac pq:p,q\in\mathbb{Z},\ q\ne0\right\}\] allow exact division, such as sharing three loaves equally among four people.

  • The irrational numbers are the real numbers that are not rational. They include numbers such as \(\sqrt2\), \(\pi\), and \(e\).

Checkpoint 1.1

Every integer is rational because \(m=m/1\). Every rational number is real. However, not every real number is rational. In symbols, \[\mathbb{N}\subset\mathbb{Z}\subset\mathbb{Q}\subset\mathbb{R}.\]

Common Number Sets

Definition 1.1

The Standard Number Sets

\[\mathbb{N}=\{1,2,3,\ldots\},\qquad \mathbb{Z}=\{\ldots,-2,-1,0,1,2,\ldots\},\] \[\mathbb{Q}=\left\{\frac pq:p,q\in\mathbb{Z},\ q\ne0\right\}, \qquad \mathbb{R}=\text{the real number system}.\] A real number outside \(\mathbb{Q}\) is called irrational.

The Diagonal of a Unit Square

Consider a square of side length \(1\). By the Pythagorean theorem, its diagonal \(d\) satisfies \[d^2=1^2+1^2=2,\] so \(d=\sqrt2\).

The line segment is visibly present. Therefore, the length \(\sqrt2\) is not an imaginary invention. The important question is whether this length can be expressed as a fraction \(p/q\).

Historical note 1.1

A traditional account associates the discovery of irrational magnitudes with the Pythagorean school in ancient Greece. The exact historical details are uncertain, but the mathematical shock is easy to understand: geometry produced a perfectly valid length that arithmetic with fractions could not represent.

Quantifiers

Before we use symbols, let us say exactly what we mean. “Every student has a book” and “There is a book which every student has” make different promises. In the first sentence, different students may have different books. In the second, one particular book must work for everybody. Mathematics needs the same care about who is chosen first and what is allowed to depend on that choice.

\[\forall\quad\text{means ``for every,''} \qquad \exists\quad\text{means ``there exists.''}\] In \(\forall x\in A\;P(x)\), the set \(A\) is the domain: it tells us which values of \(x\) we must consider. The assertion \(P(x)\) must hold for every one of those values. To disprove it, one value in \(A\) for which \(P(x)\) fails is enough; we call this a counterexample. By contrast, \(\exists x\in A\;P(x)\) asks for at least one suitable value, often called a witness. It does not say that there is only one.

Read from left to right: the order of the choices

Example 1.1

One response for each choice, or one response for everybody?

Compare the following two statements: \[\text{(A)}\quad \forall n\in\mathbb Z\;\exists m\in\mathbb Z:\ n<m, \qquad \text{(B)}\quad \exists m\in\mathbb Z\;\forall n\in\mathbb Z:\ n<m.\] Write each in words and decide whether it is true.

Show worked solution

Statement (A) says: “For every integer \(n\), there is an integer \(m\) greater than \(n\).” You give me \(n\) first; I then choose \(m=n+1\). My choice may depend on yours. Since \(n<n+1\), (A) is true.

Statement (B) says: “There is an integer \(m\) greater than every integer \(n\).” Here I must announce one \(m\) before you choose \(n\), and it must survive every choice you make. Whatever \(m\) I announce, you may choose \(n=m\). The required inequality becomes \(m<m\), which is false. Thus (B) is false.

The difference is not in the inequality. It is in the order of the choices: (A) allows \(m=m(n)\), whereas (B) demands one fixed \(m\) for all \(n\).

Now consider the closely related statements from our discussion, with the ranges of both variables written explicitly: \[\text{(C)}\quad \forall n\in\mathbb{N}\;\exists M\in(0,\infty): n<M, \qquad \text{(D)}\quad \exists M\in(0,\infty)\;\forall n\in\mathbb{N}: n<M.\] Here \(M\) is a positive real number, not necessarily an integer. Statement (C) says that each natural number has some positive real number above it; \(M=n+1\) proves this immediately. Statement (D) asks for a single positive real number above all the natural numbers. This statement is false, but the integer argument above does not prove that: setting \(n=M\) may not give a natural number. We shall justify its falsity later in this lecture, when we prove the Archimedean property from completeness. For now, the point is to distinguish the two promises, without borrowing a result we have not yet proved.

What does it mean to negate a statement?

To negate a statement is to describe exactly when it fails. “Not every student passed” means that at least one student did not pass. It does not mean that every student failed. Conversely, “There is no student who passed” means that every student failed.

Formula 1.1

Negating Quantified Statements

For a fixed domain \(A\), \[\neg\bigl(\forall x\in A\;P(x)\bigr) \equiv \exists x\in A\;\neg P(x),\] \[\neg\bigl(\exists x\in A\;P(x)\bigr) \equiv \forall x\in A\;\neg P(x).\] The symbol \(\neg\) means “not”, and \(\equiv\) means that the two statements have the same truth value. The domain stays the same; each quantifier changes, and the assertion inside is negated.

An implication fails precisely when its hypothesis is true and its conclusion is false: \[\neg(P\Rightarrow Q)\equiv P\ \text{and}\ \neg Q.\] For real numbers, the boundary cases matter: \[\neg(a<b)\equiv a\ge b, \qquad \neg(a\le b)\equiv a>b.\] Equality belongs to “not less than”. It does not belong to “greater than”.

Example 1.2

Negate the whole claim, keeping the domain

Negate the statement “Every positive real number is greater than \(1\).” Decide which of the original statement and its negation is true.

Show worked solution

First write the complete statement: \[\forall x\in(0,\infty): x>1.\] For it to fail, we need one positive real number for which \(x>1\) fails. Thus its negation is \[\exists x\in(0,\infty): x\le1.\] In words: “There is a positive real number which is at most \(1\).” The choice \(x=\tfrac12\) proves the negation, so the original statement is false. The choice \(x=1\) would also work. The proposed negation “Every positive real number is less than \(1\)” would change both the quantifier and the inequality incorrectly.

With more than one quantifier, work from the outside inward. For example, the negation of (C) is obtained as follows: \[\begin{aligned} &\neg\bigl[\forall n\in\mathbb{N}\;\exists M\in(0,\infty): n<M\bigr]\\ &\quad\equiv \exists n\in\mathbb{N}\; \neg\bigl[\exists M\in(0,\infty): n<M\bigr]\\ &\quad\equiv \exists n\in\mathbb{N}\;\forall M\in(0,\infty): n\ge M. \end{aligned}\] Read the last line: “There is a natural number which is at least every positive real number.” This is false, as expected: for any proposed \(n\), the positive real number \(M=n+1\) defeats it.

Notice that this is not statement (D). Merely interchanging two quantifiers does not produce a negation. To negate, we keep the variables in their original order, change each quantifier, and negate the assertion. For comparison, the negation of (D) is \[\forall M\in(0,\infty)\;\exists n\in\mathbb{N}: n\ge M.\] It says: “Whatever positive real bound you propose, some natural number reaches or exceeds it.” Its proof is part of the later Archimedean discussion.

Example 1.3

Negating a statement with an “if”

Negate: “For every real number \(x\), if \(x>2\), then \(x^2>4\).” Explain what a counterexample would have to satisfy.

Show worked solution

The statement is \[\forall x\in\mathbb{R}:\ (x>2\Rightarrow x^2>4).\] First change “for every” to “there exists”. Then ask when the implication fails: its hypothesis must hold, and its conclusion must fail. Therefore the negation is \[\exists x\in\mathbb{R}:\ (x>2\ \text{and}\ x^2\le4).\] In words: “There is a real number greater than \(2\) whose square is at most \(4\).” A number such as \(x=0\) is not a counterexample, although its square is at most \(4\), because it does not satisfy the hypothesis \(x>2\).

In fact no counterexample exists. If \(x>2\), then both \(x-2\) and \(x+2\) are positive, so \(x^2-4=(x-2)(x+2)>0\). Thus the original statement is true and its negation is false. We have written the negation first and checked its truth second; these are separate tasks.

Method 1.1

Write the domains. Read the statement aloud, respecting the order of the choices. Change each quantifier, one at a time. Negate the final assertion, including any implication, and check whether equality is allowed. Finally, read your new statement aloud: does it describe precisely a failure of the original promise?

Standard Proof Patterns

Direct Proof. Begin with the hypotheses and use definitions or known results until the desired conclusion is reached.

Contradiction. Assume the desired conclusion is false. Derive a statement that contradicts a hypothesis or a known fact. The irrationality of \(\sqrt2\) is a model example.

Induction. Prove a base case. Assume the statement for \(n\) and use that assumption to prove it for \(n+1\). We will write out an induction argument in Section 1.10.

A counterexample. To disprove a statement about every object in a specified domain, give one object in that domain for which it fails. For instance, \(2\) disproves the claim that every even integer is divisible by \(4\).

Proof That \(\sqrt2\) Is Irrational

We now prove carefully that no fraction can equal \(\sqrt2\).

Theorem 1.1

Irrationality of \(\sqrt2\)

The number \(\sqrt2\) is irrational.

Proof strategy 1.1

Assume the opposite: write \(\sqrt2=p/q\) in lowest terms. We shall show that both \(p\) and \(q\) must be even. That contradicts the phrase “in lowest terms.”

Show proof

Proof. Suppose, for contradiction, that \(\sqrt2\) is rational. Then \[\sqrt2=\frac pq\] for some coprime integers \(p\) and \(q\) with \(q\ne0\).

Squaring both sides gives \[2=\frac{p^2}{q^2}, \qquad\text{hence}\qquad p^2=2q^2.\] Thus \(p^2\) is even. An odd integer has odd square, so \(p\) itself must be even. Write \(p=2k\) for some \(k\in\mathbb{Z}\). Substitution gives \[(2k)^2=2q^2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad 4k^2=2q^2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad q^2=2k^2.\] Therefore \(q^2\) is even, and hence \(q\) is even as well.

We have proved that \(2\) divides both \(p\) and \(q\). This contradicts the assumption that \(p\) and \(q\) are coprime. Therefore \(\sqrt2\) cannot be rational. ◻

Connection 1.1

Every integer is either even or odd. If \(p\) were odd, we could write \(p=2m+1\), and then \[p^2=(2m+1)^2=4m^2+4m+1=2(2m^2+2m)+1,\] which is odd. Thus an even square cannot come from an odd integer.

Warning 1.1

It is not enough to write \(\sqrt2=p/q\) with arbitrary integers \(p,q\). The fraction must first be reduced to lowest terms. Otherwise, concluding that both \(p\) and \(q\) are even would not yet be a contradiction.

Other Irrational Numbers

The number \(\sqrt2\) is only the first example. Many other real numbers are irrational.

  • \(\sqrt3,\sqrt5,\ldots\) are irrational whenever the integer under the square root is not a perfect square.

  • \(\pi\) is the ratio of a circle’s circumference to its diameter.

  • \(e\) arises naturally in continuous growth, logarithms, and differential equations.

An elementary proof that \(e\) is irrational can be given using infinite series in the next level; proving the irrationality of \(\pi\) requires further tools. These examples show that irrational numbers are not rare exceptions; they are woven throughout mathematics.

Rational Approximations and the “Gaps”

The decimal expansion of \(\sqrt2\) begins \[\sqrt2=1.41421356237\ldots.\] We can trap it between rational numbers: \[1.414<\sqrt2<1.415,\] and more accurately, \[1.4142<\sqrt2<1.4143.\] Each endpoint is rational, but the number being trapped is not.

Intuition 1.1

The rational numbers are dense: between any two different real numbers there is a rational number. Yet \(\mathbb{Q}\) is not complete. A sequence of rational approximations can settle toward a number that is not rational. The real numbers are obtained by adding all such missing limits.

The Real Number System

The real numbers form an ordered field. Informally, this means that we can add, subtract, multiply, divide by nonzero numbers, and compare numbers using \(<\) in the usual consistent way. The crucial additional property is completeness.

Definition 1.2

Completeness, Informal Form

The real number system is complete: whenever a collection of real numbers determines a finite boundary that should exist, that boundary is itself a real number.

One precise formulation is the least-upper-bound property.

Theorem 1.2

Least-Upper-Bound Property of \(\mathbb{R}\)

Every nonempty subset of \(\mathbb{R}\) that is bounded above has a least upper bound in \(\mathbb{R}\).

For now, the main message is this:

Completeness is what makes the main theorems of calculus possible. The Bolzano–Weierstrass theorem, the intermediate value theorem, the extreme value theorem, and the mean value theorem all ultimately depend on it.

Induction, Least Elements, and a Reading Check

Mathematical induction says that if a property holds for \(1\), and its truth for \(n\) implies its truth for \(n+1\), then it holds for every \(n\in\mathbb{N}\). The hypothesis at the inductive step is not an assumption that the theorem is already true for all \(n\): it is a conditional argument showing that truth passes from one stage to the next.

An equivalent form is the well-ordering principle: every nonempty subset of \(\mathbb{N}\) has a least element. To see how induction gives this, suppose a nonempty set \(S\subseteq\mathbb{N}\) had no least element. Then \(1\notin S\). If \(1,\ldots,n\) were absent, \(n+1\) could not belong to \(S\), since it would be least. Induction would make \(S\) empty. Conversely, if an inductive statement failed, the least index of failure would contradict either the base case or the induction step.

Example 1.4

A First Inductive Estimate

For \(t\ge-1\) and \(n\in\mathbb{N}\), prove \((1+t)^n\ge1+nt\).

Show worked solution

The assertion is equality for \(n=1\). If it holds for \(n\), multiply by \(1+t\ge0\) to obtain \[(1+t)^{n+1}\ge(1+nt)(1+t) =1+(n+1)t+nt^2\ge1+(n+1)t.\] The nonnegative multiplier explains the restriction \(t\ge-1\).

Sets and the Language of Membership

A set is determined by its elements. Thus \(\{1,2,2,3\}=\{3,2,1\}\): repetition and order do not change a set. A sequence, by contrast, records order and repetition. This distinction will matter when we enumerate a set or study a sequence of real numbers.

The notation \(A\subseteq B\) means that every member of \(A\) is a member of \(B\). To prove \(A=B\), one often proves both inclusions. The empty set \(\varnothing\) is a subset of every set: there is no element of \(\varnothing\) that could violate the required implication. The statements \(\varnothing\subseteq A\) and \(\varnothing\in A\) are different. The first is always true; the second says that the empty set is one of the objects listed in \(A\).

Example 1.5

Membership Is Not Inclusion

Let \(A=\{1,\{1\},\varnothing\}\). Explain which of the following are true: \(1\in A\), \(\{1\}\in A\), \(\{1\}\subseteq A\), \(\varnothing\in A\), and \(\{\varnothing\}\subseteq A\).

Show worked solution

All five are true, for different reasons. The objects \(1\), \(\{1\}\), and \(\varnothing\) are explicitly members of \(A\). The only member of \(\{1\}\) is \(1\), which belongs to \(A\). The only member of \(\{\varnothing\}\) is \(\varnothing\), which also belongs to \(A\). A useful discipline is to read a membership statement aloud as a statement about one object, and an inclusion statement as a statement about every element of a set.

Unions, Intersections, and Indexed Families

For subsets of a fixed set \(X\), \[A\cup B=\{x:x\in A\text{ or }x\in B\},\qquad A\cap B=\{x:x\in A\text{ and }x\in B\}.\] The complement \(X\setminus A\) consists of the points of \(X\) outside \(A\). A family \((A_i)_{i\in I}\) is a collection of sets indexed by another set \(I\). Its union and intersection mean \[x\in\bigcup_{i\in I}A_i\iff\exists i\in I,\ x\in A_i, \qquad x\in\bigcap_{i\in I}A_i\iff\forall i\in I,\ x\in A_i.\] Thus union is related to an existential quantifier and intersection to a universal quantifier.

Example 1.6

Shrinking and Expanding Intervals

Find \(\bigcap_{n\ge1}(-1/n,1/n)\) and \(\bigcup_{n\ge1}(-n,n)\).

Show worked solution

The first intersection is \(\{0\}\). Certainly \(0\) belongs to every interval. If \(x\ne0\), choose \(n>1/|x|\); then \(|x|>1/n\), so \(x\) is absent from that particular interval. The union is \(\mathbb{R}\): given \(x\in\mathbb{R}\), choose \(n>|x|\), and then \(x\in(-n,n)\). The index chosen depends on the point \(x\).

Theorem 1.3

De Morgan’s Laws

For a family of subsets \(A_i\subseteq X\), \[X\setminus\bigcup_iA_i=\bigcap_i(X\setminus A_i),\qquad X\setminus\bigcap_iA_i=\bigcup_i(X\setminus A_i).\]

Show proof

Proof. A point lies outside the union exactly when it lies in none of its members, which means that it lies in every complement. A point lies outside the intersection exactly when it fails to belong to at least one member, which means that it lies in at least one complement. These statements establish equality by checking membership. ◻

Functions, Images, and Preimages

Definition 1.3

Function, Domain, Codomain, and Range

A function \(f:X\to Y\) assigns exactly one element \(f(x)\in Y\) to each \(x\in X\). Its domain is \(X\), its codomain is \(Y\), and its range is \(f(X)=\{f(x):x\in X\}\subseteq Y\).

The range may be smaller than the codomain. In particular, whether a function is onto depends on its codomain.

Definition 1.4

Injective, Surjective, and Bijective Maps

A map \(f:X\to Y\) is injective if \(f(x_1)=f(x_2)\) implies \(x_1=x_2\). It is surjective if every \(y\in Y\) equals \(f(x)\) for some \(x\in X\). It is bijective if it is both injective and surjective.

The Cartesian product \(X\times Y\) is the set of ordered pairs \((x,y)\). The graph of \(f\) is the subset \(\{(x,f(x)):x\in X\}\) of this product. The graph of a function has exactly one pair above each input.

Definition 1.5

Image and Preimage

If \(A\subseteq X\) and \(B\subseteq Y\), set \[f(A)=\{f(x):x\in A\},\qquad f^{-1}(B)=\{x\in X:f(x)\in B\}.\] The second notation denotes a set, and does not require an inverse function to exist.

Example 1.7

A Preimage without an Inverse Function

For \(f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}\), \(f(x)=x^2\), calculate \(f((-2,1))\), \(f^{-1}((1,4))\), and \(f^{-1}(\{-1\})\).

Show worked solution

The image is \([0,4)\): the value \(0\) occurs, and values arbitrarily close to \(4\) occur through inputs near \(-2\), but \(4\) does not occur. The inequality \(1<x^2<4\) gives \(f^{-1}((1,4))=(-2,-1)\cup(1,2)\). No real square is negative, so \(f^{-1}(\{-1\})=\varnothing\). None of these computations requires \(f\) to be one-to-one.

Theorem 1.4

Preimages Preserve Set Operations

For any function \(f:X\to Y\) and any family \(B_i\subseteq Y\), \[f^{-1}\Bigl(\bigcup_iB_i\Bigr)=\bigcup_i f^{-1}(B_i),\qquad f^{-1}\Bigl(\bigcap_iB_i\Bigr)=\bigcap_i f^{-1}(B_i),\] and \(f^{-1}(Y\setminus B)=X\setminus f^{-1}(B)\).

Show proof

Proof. For the union, \[x\in f^{-1}\Bigl(\bigcup_iB_i\Bigr) \iff f(x)\in\bigcup_iB_i \iff \exists i,\ f(x)\in B_i \iff x\in\bigcup_i f^{-1}(B_i).\] Replace the existential quantifier by a universal one to prove the intersection identity. For complements, the condition \(f(x)\notin B\) is exactly \(x\notin f^{-1}(B)\), with \(x\) restricted to \(X\). ◻

Images always preserve unions, but for intersections only \(f(A\cap B)\subseteq f(A)\cap f(B)\) is automatic. For \(f(x)=x^2\), \(A=\{-1\}\) and \(B=\{1\}\) have empty intersection, while \(f(A)\cap f(B)=\{1\}\). If \(f\) is injective, equality follows: two preimages producing the same output must then be the same point.

Composition and Inverse Functions

Definition 1.6

Composition of Functions

For \(f:X\to Y\) and \(g:Y\to Z\), the composition \(g\circ f:X\to Z\) is \((g\circ f)(x)=g(f(x))\) for each \(x\in X\).

The order of evaluation is from the inside out. Composition is associative, but generally not commutative. For \(f(x)=x+1\) and \(g(x)=x^2\), we have \(g(f(x))=x^2+2x+1\) and \(f(g(x))=x^2+1\).

Definition 1.7

Inverse Function

A bijection \(f:X\to Y\) has an inverse function \(f^{-1}:Y\to X\), defined by \(f^{-1}(y)=x\) exactly when \(f(x)=y\).

Surjectivity supplies existence and injectivity supplies uniqueness. Consequently \[f^{-1}\circ f=\id_X,\qquad f\circ f^{-1}=\id_Y.\] If \(f\) and \(g\) are bijections, then \((g\circ f)^{-1}=f^{-1}\circ g^{-1}\). This order is forced by undoing the last operation first.

The equation \(f(x)=x^2\) gives a bijection from \([0,\infty)\) to \([0,\infty)\), but not from \(\mathbb{R}\) to \([0,\infty)\). Which hypothesis changes? What happens if the codomain is instead \(\mathbb{R}\)?

Relations, Order, and Equivalence

A relation on \(X\) is a rule determining which pairs of elements are related; formally it is a subset of \(X\times X\). An equivalence relation is reflexive, symmetric, and transitive. These three conditions express a consistent notion of being the same for a particular purpose.

For integers, having the same remainder on division by \(3\) is an equivalence relation. It groups the integers into three classes. For nonzero-denominator fractions, \((p,q)\) and \((r,s)\) represent the same rational number when \(ps=rq\).

Theorem 1.5

Equivalence Classes Form a Partition

If \(\sim\) is an equivalence relation on \(X\), the classes \([x]=\{y\in X:y\sim x\}\) are nonempty and two classes are either equal or disjoint.

Show proof

Proof. Reflexivity gives \(x\in[x]\), so every class is nonempty and every element lies in a class. If \(z\in[x]\cap[y]\) and \(u\in[x]\), then \(u\sim x\), \(x\sim z\), and \(z\sim y\). Transitivity gives \(u\sim y\), so \([x]\subseteq[y]\). Interchanging \(x\) and \(y\) proves the reverse inclusion. Thus intersecting classes are equal. ◻

A partial order is reflexive, antisymmetric, and transitive. Antisymmetry says that \(x\preceq y\) and \(y\preceq x\) imply \(x=y\). Set inclusion is a partial order. It is not usually a total order: \(\{1\}\) and \(\{2\}\) are incomparable by inclusion. The usual order on \(\mathbb{R}\) is total because any two numbers can be compared.

A Function with an Empty Domain

There is exactly one function \(\varnothing\to Y\), for any set \(Y\): its graph is empty. The requirement of one output for every input has no input to check. This function is injective. It is onto only when \(Y=\varnothing\). In contrast, there is no function from a nonempty set to \(\varnothing\), because even one input would need an output that does not exist.

A short preparation in mathematical language

A statement such as “every multiple of \(4\) is even” has a hypothesis and a conclusion. A proof explains why the hypothesis forces the conclusion. An example illustrates the statement, but does not establish it for every integer. To disprove a universal statement, however, one counterexample is enough: \(2\) disproves “every even number is a multiple of \(4\).”

We use \(\mathbb{N}=\{1,2,3,\ldots\}\). The symbols \(\forall\) and \(\exists\) mean “for every” and “there exists.” An implication \(P\Rightarrow Q\) does not usually work backwards. For example, \(x=2\Rightarrow x^2=4\), but \(x^2=4\) also allows \(x=-2\).

Method 1.2

Write down what is given, what must be shown, and the definition of the new word in the question. If you are stuck on “injective”, replace it by: assume \(f(x)=f(y)\) and show \(x=y\). If you are stuck on “surjective”, start with an arbitrary point of the codomain and construct a preimage.

The familiar number systems satisfy \(\mathbb{N}\subset\mathbb{Z}\subset\mathbb{Q}\subset\mathbb{R}\). The geometric reason for passing from fractions to real numbers was developed in Lecture [app:real-background]. We now ask a further question: can the elements of a set be put into a list?

Intervals on the Real Line

Definition 1.8

Intervals on the Real Line

For \(a<b\), the open and closed intervals with endpoints \(a,b\) are \[(a,b)=\{x\in\mathbb{R}:a<x<b\},\qquad [a,b]=\{x\in\mathbb{R}:a\le x\le b\}.\] The half-open intervals \([a,b)\) and \((a,b]\) include exactly one endpoint.

An open interval contains all the points strictly between its endpoints, but neither endpoint itself. We shall use such intervals as windows around a point when we study sequences.

The Real Numbers as an Ordered Field

The statement that the real numbers fill the gaps in the rational line is an excellent motivation, but it is not yet an axiom. To use real numbers in a proof, we must specify which properties we are allowed to use. These properties have three layers: arithmetic, order, and completeness.

The arithmetic rules say that \(\mathbb{R}\) is a field. Addition and multiplication are associative and commutative; there are identities \(0\) and \(1\), with \(0\ne1\); every real number has an additive inverse; every nonzero real number has a multiplicative inverse; and multiplication distributes over addition. For example, distributivity is the assertion \[x(y+z)=xy+xz.\] These axioms justify familiar cancellation rules. If \(a+b=a+c\), adding \(-a\) to both sides gives \(b=c\). If \(ab=ac\) and \(a\ne0\), multiplication by \(a^{-1}\) gives \(b=c\). The condition \(a\ne0\) cannot be removed: \(0b=0c\) says nothing about whether \(b=c\).

The order is a total order compatible with these operations: \[x\le y\Longrightarrow x+z\le y+z,\qquad 0\le x,\ 0\le y\Longrightarrow 0\le xy.\] Every pair of real numbers can be compared. Multiplication by a positive number preserves an inequality; multiplication by a negative number reverses it. To see the second assertion rather than merely remember it, suppose \(x<y\) and \(z<0\). Then \(y-x>0\) and \(-z>0\), so \((y-x)(-z)>0\). Rearranging yields \(xz>yz\).

Example 1.8

Why Squaring Requires a Sign Check

When is it legitimate to square the inequality \(x<y\)?

Show worked solution

If \(0\le x<y\), then \[y^2-x^2=(y-x)(y+x)>0,\] so \(x^2<y^2\). Without nonnegativity the assertion fails: \(-3<2\) but \(9>4\). If both numbers are negative, their absolute values occur in the opposite order. Thus an inequality involving squares must be interpreted together with the signs of the original quantities.

The rational numbers satisfy all the rules above as well. Arithmetic and order therefore do not distinguish \(\mathbb{Q}\) from \(\mathbb{R}\). The distinction is the existence of certain boundaries, which we now make precise.

Bounds, Maximum, Supremum, and Infimum

Suppose a set of real numbers lies entirely to the left of some point \(M\). There may be many choices of \(M\). We are interested in the smallest possible one, even when that point does not belong to the set.

Definition 1.9

Upper and Lower Bounds

Let \(A\subseteq\mathbb{R}\) be nonempty. A number \(M\) is an upper bound of \(A\) if \(a\le M\) for every \(a\in A\). A number \(m\) is a lower bound if \(m\le a\) for every \(a\in A\). A maximum is an upper bound that belongs to \(A\); a minimum is a lower bound that belongs to \(A\).

The supremum \(\sup A\) is the least upper bound, when one exists in \(\mathbb{R}\). The infimum \(\inf A\) is the greatest lower bound, when one exists in \(\mathbb{R}\).

For \(A=(0,1)\), the upper bounds are exactly the numbers in \([1,\infty)\). Thus \(\sup A=1\), although \(A\) has no maximum. Similarly \(\inf A=0\) and there is no minimum. For \(B=\{1/n:n\in\mathbb{N}\}\), the maximum and supremum are both \(1\), whereas the infimum is \(0\) and there is no minimum.

Theorem 1.6

The Approximation Property of a Supremum

Let \(A\) be nonempty and bounded above. A number \(s\) is \(\sup A\) if and only if

  1. \(a\le s\) for every \(a\in A\);

  2. for every \(\varepsilon>0\), there exists \(a\in A\) with \(s-\varepsilon<a\).

Show proof

Proof. Suppose \(s=\sup A\). It is an upper bound by definition. If the second property failed for some \(\varepsilon>0\), every \(a\in A\) would satisfy \(a\le s-\varepsilon\). Then \(s-\varepsilon\) would be a smaller upper bound, a contradiction.

Conversely, suppose the two properties hold. If \(u<s\), choose \(\varepsilon=s-u>0\). The second property gives \(a\in A\) with \(u<a\), so \(u\) is not an upper bound. Therefore no number smaller than \(s\) is an upper bound, and \(s\) is the least upper bound. ◻

The order of the choices matters. Take \(A=(0,1)\) and \(s=1\). For \(0<\varepsilon<1\), the point \(a=1-\varepsilon/2\) lies in \(A\) and satisfies \(1-\varepsilon<a<1\). For \(\varepsilon\ge1\), \(a=1/2\) works. But if someone fixes one \(a\in A\) first, choosing \(\varepsilon=(1-a)/2\) leaves \(a\) below \(1-\varepsilon\). We can get as close as requested without ever reaching the supremum.

Completeness and the Archimedean Property

Definition 1.10

The Completeness Axiom

Every nonempty subset of \(\mathbb{R}\) that is bounded above has a supremum in \(\mathbb{R}\).

This is the additional property we assume about real numbers. It is not proved from the field and order axioms: those axioms also hold in \(\mathbb{Q}\), where completeness fails. A construction using Dedekind cuts gives a system satisfying all these axioms. Once the axioms have been adopted, our later proofs do not depend on a particular construction.

The corresponding statement for lower bounds follows immediately. If \(A\) is nonempty and bounded below, define \(-A=\{-a:a\in A\}\). Then \(-A\) is bounded above, and \[\inf A=-\sup(-A).\] Indeed \(m\) is a lower bound for \(A\) precisely when \(-m\) is an upper bound for \(-A\).

Theorem 1.7

The Archimedean Property

For every \(x\in\mathbb{R}\), there exists \(n\in\mathbb{N}\) with \(n>x\). Consequently, for every \(\varepsilon>0\) there is \(n\in\mathbb{N}\) such that \(0<1/n<\varepsilon\).

Show proof

Proof. If \(\mathbb{N}\) were bounded above, completeness would give \(s=\sup\mathbb{N}\). The number \(s-1\) is not an upper bound, so some \(n\in\mathbb{N}\) satisfies \(s-1<n\). But then \(n+1\in\mathbb{N}\) and \(n+1>s\), contradicting that \(s\) is an upper bound. Thus \(\mathbb{N}\) is unbounded. Apply this result to \(x=1/\varepsilon\) for the last assertion. ◻

Example 1.9

A Supremum Found by Approximation

Find the supremum of \(A=\{2-3/n:n\in\mathbb{N}\}\).

Show worked solution

Every element is less than \(2\), so \(2\) is an upper bound. Given \(\varepsilon>0\), choose a natural number \(n>3/\varepsilon\). Then \[2-\varepsilon<2-\frac3n<2.\] The approximation property gives \(\sup A=2\). No term equals \(2\), so the set has no maximum. The required choice of \(n\) is justified by the Archimedean property, now proved.

Theorem 1.8

The Integer-Part Property

For every \(x\in\mathbb{R}\) there is a unique integer \(m\) such that \(m\le x<m+1\). This integer is denoted by \(\lfloor x\rfloor\).

Show proof

Proof. Choose \(N\in\mathbb{N}\) with \(N>|x|+1\). Among the finitely many integers \(-N,-N+1,\ldots,N\) that do not exceed \(x\), choose the largest, say \(m\). There is at least one such integer because \(-N<x\). Since \(N>x\), maximality gives \(x<m+1\). If \(k\) also had this property and \(k>m\), then \(k\ge m+1>x\), a contradiction. The case \(m>k\) is the same. ◻

Why is \(\lfloor-1.2\rfloor=-2\) rather than \(-1\)? Explain why rounding and taking the integer part are different operations.

Constructing the Square Root from Completeness

The diagonal of a square motivates \(\sqrt2\). Completeness proves that a real number with the required square exists. This separates two assertions: there is a real solution of \(x^2=2\), and there is no rational solution.

Theorem 1.9

Existence and Uniqueness of a Positive Square Root

For each \(a>0\) there is a unique \(s>0\) such that \(s^2=a\).

Show proof

Proof. Let \(A=\{x\in\mathbb{R}:x\ge0,\ x^2<a\}\). The set contains \(0\) and is bounded above by \(a+1\): if \(x>a+1\), then \(x>1\) and \(x^2>x>a\). Let \(s=\sup A\). We first note that \(s>0\); for example, \(u=\min(1,a)/2\) is positive and satisfies \(u^2<a\).

Suppose \(s^2<a\). Choose \[0<h<\min\left(1,\frac{a-s^2}{2s+1}\right).\] Then \((s+h)^2=s^2+2sh+h^2<s^2+(2s+1)h<a\). Thus \(s+h\in A\), contradicting the upper-bound property of \(s\).

Suppose instead that \(s^2>a\). Choose \[0<h<\min\left(s,\frac{s^2-a}{2s}\right).\] Then \((s-h)^2=s^2-2sh+h^2>a\). If \(x\in A\), nonnegativity and the order property of squaring imply \(x<s-h\). Hence \(s-h\) is an upper bound for \(A\), contradicting minimality of \(s\). Therefore \(s^2=a\).

Finally, if \(0<s<t\), then \(t^2-s^2=(t-s)(t+s)>0\), so two distinct positive numbers cannot have the same square. ◻

Notice the mechanism in both contradictions. A strict inequality leaves some positive room. We choose a small \(h\) whose effect is less than that room. This elementary technique will reappear in continuity arguments.

Calculating with Suprema

Theorem 1.10

Suprema of Sums and Scalar Multiples

Let \(A,B\subseteq\mathbb{R}\) be nonempty and bounded, and let \(A+B=\{a+b:a\in A,\ b\in B\}\). Then \[\sup(A+B)=\sup A+\sup B,\qquad \inf(A+B)=\inf A+\inf B.\] For \(c>0\), \(\sup(cA)=c\sup A\); for \(c<0\), \(\sup(cA)=c\inf A\).

Show proof

Proof. Write \(\alpha=\sup A\) and \(\beta=\sup B\). Every \(a+b\) is at most \(\alpha+\beta\). Conversely, given \(\varepsilon>0\), choose \(a>\alpha-\varepsilon/2\) and \(b>\beta-\varepsilon/2\). Then \(a+b>\alpha+\beta-\varepsilon\). The supremum approximation property proves equality. Applying this to \(-A\) and \(-B\) gives the infimum formula.

If \(c>0\), the upper-bound inequality is preserved by multiplication. To approximate \(c\sup A\) within \(\varepsilon\), approximate \(\sup A\) within \(\varepsilon/c\). If \(c<0\), write \(cA=(-c)(-A)\) and use \(\sup(-A)=-\inf A\). ◻

Example 1.10

Why the Same Variable Changes the Answer

Let \(f,g:D\to\mathbb{R}\) have nonempty, bounded ranges. Write \(\sup f=\sup\{f(x):x\in D\}\) and similarly for \(g\) and \(f+g\), where \((f+g)(x)=f(x)+g(x)\). Does \(\sup(f+g)=\sup f+\sup g\) hold?

Show worked solution

Only the inequality \(\sup(f+g)\le\sup f+\sup g\) is automatic. On \([0,1]\), take \(f(x)=x\) and \(g(x)=1-x\). Both suprema on the right are \(1\), but \(f(x)+g(x)=1\) for every \(x\). In the set-sum theorem, the two approximating elements can be chosen independently. For functions at the same input, the points making \(f\) large may make \(g\) small. The distinction is a matter of quantifiers.

Dedekind Cuts: A Precise Way to Fill a Gap

Richard Dedekind gave a rigorous construction of real numbers using partitions of \(\mathbb{Q}\). The full construction belongs to a later course, but the picture is accessible.

A Dedekind cut separates the rational numbers into a lower part \(A\) and an upper part \(B\) so that every element of \(A\) is smaller than every element of \(B\). The parts are nonempty, \(A\cup B=\mathbb{Q}\), \(A\cap B=\varnothing\), and \(A\) has no greatest element. Equivalently, the lower set \(A\) is a nonempty proper subset of \(\mathbb{Q}\), is downward closed, and has no greatest element. The location of the cut represents a real number.

For \(\sqrt2\), a correct lower set is \[A=\{q\in\mathbb{Q}:q<0\text{ or }q^2<2\},\] and \(B=\mathbb{Q}\setminus A\). There is no rational number at the boundary, but the cut itself represents the missing number \(\sqrt2\).
