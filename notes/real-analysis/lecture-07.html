Cauchy Sequences, Accumulation, and Density

Read this lecture online, then continue to the next chapter. Figures and exercises are being adapted separately for the web edition.

In Lecture [lec:6], monotonicity and a bound let us prove convergence before calculating the limit. Now we ask for a method that can also handle sequences which do not move in one direction. Can we recognise that the terms are settling down merely by comparing sufficiently late terms with one another? This question leads to the Cauchy condition.

Cauchy Sequences

The definition of convergence mentions the limit \(L\). Sometimes we want to decide whether a sequence converges before knowing what \(L\) is. A convergent sequence has a tail whose terms are close to one another: \[\abs{x_n-x_m} \le\abs{x_n-L}+\abs{x_m-L}.\]

Definition 7.1

Cauchy Sequence

A sequence \((x_n)\) is Cauchy if, for every \(\varepsilon>0\), there exists \(N\in\mathbb{N}\) such that \[m,n\ge N\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\abs{x_m-x_n}<\varepsilon.\]

The same \(N\) must work for every pair \(m,n\ge N\). The indices may be next to one another, or extremely far apart. There is no proposed limit in this definition: we compare late terms with other late terms. This answers the question with which we began the lecture, provided that we can prove the criterion below.

[Figure being adapted for the online edition.]

Theorem 7.1

Cauchy Criterion in \(\mathbb{R}\)

A sequence of real numbers converges if and only if it is Cauchy.

Proof strategy 7.1

The forward direction uses the triangle inequality. For the reverse direction: \[\begin{aligned} \text{Cauchy} &\Longrightarrow \text{bounded}\\ &\Longrightarrow \text{has a convergent subsequence}\\ &\Longrightarrow \text{the whole sequence follows that subsequence}. \end{aligned}\] The middle step is Bolzano–Weierstrass.

Show proof

Proof. A convergent sequence is Cauchy. Suppose \(x_n\to L\), and let \(\varepsilon>0\). Apply the definition of convergence with \(\varepsilon/2\) to choose \(N\) such that \(|x_j-L|<\varepsilon/2\) whenever \(j\ge N\). For any \(m,n\ge N\), the triangle inequality gives \[|x_m-x_n| =|(x_m-L)+(L-x_n)| \le |x_m-L|+|x_n-L| <\frac\varepsilon 2+\frac\varepsilon 2=\varepsilon.\] Thus \((x_n)\) is Cauchy.

A Cauchy sequence of real numbers converges. Suppose now that \((x_n)\) is Cauchy. We do not yet have a limit \(L\), so we cannot begin by estimating \(|x_n-L|\). We first produce a candidate.

Apply the Cauchy condition with tolerance \(1\). There is an index \(N_0\) such that \(|x_n-x_{N_0}|<1\) for all \(n\ge N_0\), and therefore \[|x_n|\le |x_n-x_{N_0}|+|x_{N_0}|<1+|x_{N_0}|\qquad(n\ge N_0).\] Set \[B=\max\{|x_1|,\ldots,|x_{N_0}|\}+1.\] The finitely many terms up to \(N_0\) have absolute value at most \(B\), and the same is true of every later term by the preceding estimate. Hence the entire sequence is bounded.

The Bolzano–Weierstrass theorem now gives a subsequence \((x_{n_k})\) and a real number \(L\) such that \(x_{n_k}\to L\). This supplies our candidate. We must still show that all late terms, not only those selected for the subsequence, approach \(L\).

Let \(\varepsilon>0\). Choose \(N_1\) from the Cauchy condition so that \[m,n\ge N_1\quad\Longrightarrow\quad |x_m-x_n|<\varepsilon/2.\] Choose \(K\) from convergence of the subsequence so that \[k\ge K\quad\Longrightarrow\quad |x_{n_k}-L|<\varepsilon/2.\] Fix one \(k_0\ge\max\{K,N_1\}\). Since \(n_{k_0}\ge k_0\ge N_1\), the selected term \(x_{n_{k_0}}\) lies both in the Cauchy tail and within \(\varepsilon/2\) of \(L\). For every \(n\ge N_1\), use this same term as a bridge: \[|x_n-L| \le |x_n-x_{n_{k_0}}|+|x_{n_{k_0}}-L| <\frac\varepsilon 2+\frac\varepsilon 2=\varepsilon.\] Thus \(N=N_1\) works for every \(n\ge N\), proving \(x_n\to L\). ◻

Connection 7.1

The statement “every Cauchy sequence converges” is a form of completeness. It is true in \(\mathbb{R}\) but false in \(\mathbb{Q}\): rational approximations to \(\sqrt2\) form a Cauchy sequence of rationals with no rational limit.

Cantor’s Construction from Rational Approximations

The intervals in Lecture [lec:6] gave us a picture of a number located more and more accurately. Now the Cauchy condition lets us describe that picture without naming a limit in advance. This section explains the construction idea; a full verification of every ordered-field and completeness axiom is a separate foundational development.

The endpoints describe the same destination

Suppose \(a_n,b_n\in\mathbb{Q}\) and \[a_n\le a_{n+1}\le b_{n+1}\le b_n, \qquad b_n-a_n\longrightarrow0.\] For \(m\ge n\), nesting gives \[0\le a_m-a_n\le b_n-a_n, \qquad 0\le b_n-b_m\le b_n-a_n.\] Given \(\varepsilon>0\), choose \(N\) so that \(b_n-a_n<\varepsilon\) for \(n\ge N\). If \(m,n\ge N\), order the two indices and use these estimates. Both endpoint sequences are Cauchy. Moreover \(|b_n-a_n|\to0\), so the endpoints become indistinguishable at arbitrarily fine precision. These estimates involve only rational numbers and do not require a pre-existing irrational limit.

A number is an approximation process, with equivalent processes identified

Begin with the set \(\mathcal C\) of all rational Cauchy sequences. At this stage the tolerances in the definition may be restricted to positive rational numbers, so the definition can be made within rational arithmetic. For \((q_n),(r_n)\in\mathcal C\), write \[(q_n)\sim(r_n)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad |q_n-r_n|\longrightarrow0.\] We are saying that the two lists describe the same number if their difference eventually becomes smaller than every positive rational tolerance. This is an equivalence relation. A sequence differs from itself by zero, and \(|q_n-r_n|=|r_n-q_n|\). For transitivity, if \((q_n)\sim(r_n)\) and \((r_n)\sim(s_n)\), choose a common stage at which both errors are less than \(\varepsilon/2\); then \[|q_n-s_n|\le |q_n-r_n|+|r_n-s_n|<\varepsilon.\]

A new number is the equivalence class \([(q_n)]\), containing every approximation process equivalent to \((q_n)\). We define the new system by \(\mathcal C/{\sim}\). The rational number \(q\) is represented by the constant sequence \(q,q,q,\ldots\). Distinct rational constants remain distinct: their distance is one fixed positive number, which cannot tend to zero.

Addition and multiplication are defined using representatives: \[[(q_n)]+[(r_n)]=[(q_n+r_n)], \qquad [(q_n)]\,[(r_n)]=[(q_nr_n)].\] The Cauchy estimates justify these choices. For sums, use the triangle inequality and give each sequence half the tolerance. For products, Cauchy sequences are bounded, by the same proof used in the Cauchy criterion. Choose a rational \(B\ge1\) bounding both sequences. Then \[|q_mr_m-q_nr_n| \le |q_m|\,|r_m-r_n|+|r_n|\,|q_m-q_n| \le B\bigl(|r_m-r_n|+|q_m-q_n|\bigr).\] Making each last error less than \(\varepsilon/(2B)\) proves that the product sequence is Cauchy. The same estimate applied to equivalent representatives shows that multiplication does not depend on the chosen lists; the sum is independent of representatives by the triangle inequality. Further verifications define the order and division and establish completeness. The resulting complete ordered field is \(\mathbb{R}\).

Supplying the missing positive square root of \(2\)

Start with the rational interval \([1,2]\). Bisect it. Compare the square of its rational midpoint with \(2\), and keep the half in which the square passes from below \(2\) to above \(2\). Repeat. No rational midpoint can have square exactly \(2\), by the irrationality argument in Lecture [lec:1]. We obtain rational endpoints with \[a_n^2<2<b_n^2,\qquad [a_{n+1},b_{n+1}]\subseteq[a_n,b_n], \qquad b_n-a_n=2^{-n}\] when \(n\) counts the number of bisections. Both endpoints lie in \([1,2]\). Their left endpoint sequence is rational and Cauchy. Declare \(\alpha=[(a_n)]\). It is a positive number in the newly constructed system. Since \(a_n+b_n\le4\), \[0<2-a_n^2<b_n^2-a_n^2 =(b_n-a_n)(b_n+a_n)\le4\cdot2^{-n}\longrightarrow0.\] Thus \((a_n^2)\) is equivalent to the constant sequence \(2,2,2,\ldots\): in the new arithmetic, \(\alpha^2=2\). This is the number called \(\sqrt2\). It is not equivalent to a rational constant; such a constant would have rational square \(2\), which we already proved impossible.

The two viewpoints now fit together. Inside \(\mathbb{R}\), nested intervals locate an existing number. Starting with \(\mathbb{Q}\), a rational Cauchy approximation process supplies the number itself.

Contractive Sequences Are Cauchy

We now have a convergence test that does not mention the answer \(L\). Let us use it on a situation where the distance between consecutive terms shrinks by a fixed factor. If each new step is at most half the preceding step, for example, then even a very long journey through later terms has a small total length. The point is to control all those later steps together.

Definition 7.2

Contractive Sequence

A real sequence \((x_n)\) is called contractive (or a contraction sequence) if there is one fixed real number \(q\) with \(0\le q<1\) such that \[|x_{n+2}-x_{n+1}|\le q\,|x_{n+1}-x_n| \qquad\text{for every }n\ge1.\]

The same \(q\) must work for every \(n\). Merely finding a different factor less than \(1\) at each step is weaker: those factors might approach \(1\), and then there is no fixed geometric bound.

Before proving the result, notice why smaller and smaller adjacent differences alone are not enough. For \(x_n=\sqrt n\), \[|x_{n+1}-x_n|=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n+1}+\sqrt n}\longrightarrow0.\] Nevertheless the sequence is not Cauchy. For every \(N\), choose an integer \(n\ge\max\{N,1\}\) and let \(m=4n\). Then \(m,n\ge N\) but \[|x_m-x_n|=\sqrt{4n}-\sqrt n=\sqrt n\ge1.\] So tolerance \(\varepsilon=1\) fails. Knowing that neighbouring terms are close does not yet tell us that arbitrary late terms are close. The fixed factor \(q<1\) in a contractive sequence gives precisely the extra control we need.

Theorem 7.2

Every Contractive Sequence Is Cauchy

Every contractive sequence of real numbers is Cauchy, and hence converges in \(\mathbb{R}\). If \(0<q<1\) is a contraction factor and \(D=|x_2-x_1|\), then \[|x_m-x_n|\le\frac{Dq^{n-1}}{1-q}\qquad(m>n\ge1).\] The right side depends on the smaller index \(n\), but not on how far beyond it \(m\) lies.

We shall use this bound to find a fixed point by iteration in Lecture 16.

Show proof

Proof. If \(q=0\), the defining inequality gives \(x_{n+2}=x_{n+1}\) for all \(n\ge1\). Thus \(x_2,x_3,x_4,\ldots\) are all equal, so the sequence is Cauchy: take \(N=2\) for every \(\varepsilon>0\).

Now suppose \(0<q<1\). Write \(D=|x_2-x_1|\). If \(D=0\), the defining inequality successively forces every adjacent difference to be zero. The sequence is constant and is Cauchy. We may therefore assume \(D>0\).

First control one step. We claim that \[|x_{j+1}-x_j|\le Dq^{j-1}\qquad(j\ge1).\] For \(j=1\) this is equality. If the claim holds for \(j\), then contractivity gives \[|x_{j+2}-x_{j+1}| \le q|x_{j+1}-x_j| \le qDq^{j-1}=Dq^j.\] The claim follows by induction.

Then join two arbitrary late terms. Let \(m>n\). The finite telescoping identity \[x_m-x_n=(x_m-x_{m-1})+(x_{m-1}-x_{m-2})+\cdots+(x_{n+1}-x_n)\] and the triangle inequality give \[\begin{aligned} |x_m-x_n| &\le\sum_{j=n}^{m-1}|x_{j+1}-x_j|\\ &\le D\sum_{j=n}^{m-1}q^{j-1}\\ &=Dq^{n-1}(1+q+\cdots+q^{m-n-1}). \end{aligned}\] This is a finite sum. Multiplying it by \(1-q\) cancels the intermediate terms, so \[(1-q)(1+q+\cdots+q^{m-n-1})=1-q^{m-n}.\] Since \(0<q^{m-n}<1\) and \(1-q>0\), we obtain \[|x_m-x_n| \le Dq^{n-1}\frac{1-q^{m-n}}{1-q} \le\frac{Dq^{n-1}}{1-q}.\] We have now removed \(m\) from the bound. However far out the second index lies, this one estimate still controls the distance.

Finally choose a single tail. Let \(\varepsilon>0\). Because \(0<q<1\), the geometric sequence \(q^{n-1}\) tends to \(0\). Choose \(N\) so large that \[\frac{Dq^{N-1}}{1-q}<\varepsilon.\] For any \(m,n\ge N\), there are three possibilities. If \(m=n\), the distance is zero. If \(m>n\), the estimate above and \(q^{n-1}\le q^{N-1}\) give \[|x_m-x_n|\le\frac{Dq^{n-1}}{1-q} \le\frac{Dq^{N-1}}{1-q}<\varepsilon.\] If \(n>m\), interchange the indices and apply the same argument with \(m\) as the smaller index. Thus every pair of terms beyond \(N\) is within \(\varepsilon\). The sequence is Cauchy, and the Cauchy criterion gives convergence in \(\mathbb{R}\). ◻

We have proved that a limit exists without calculating, or even guessing, its value. The shrinking-step estimate supplied the Cauchy property, and the completeness of the real line supplied the limit.

Accumulation Points and Isolated Points

Motivation 7.1

Suppose you are at a family gathering. You may be standing with a little space around you: there is a small region in which nobody else is standing. Now imagine that Virat Kohli arrives at the gathering. What happens? People want to come closer, speak to him, and take a photograph. The space around him begins to fill.

Keep these two pictures in mind. In the first, we can draw a small window around the person and find nobody else inside it. In the second, our attention goes to the people gathering near one place. These suggest the ideas of an isolated point and an accumulation point.

The mathematical condition is more precise than a finite gathering. For an accumulation point, there must be another point however small we make the window. A large but finite crowd does not by itself guarantee that. The word every in the next definition is doing essential work.

Definition 7.3

Accumulation Point of a Set

Let \(A\subseteq\mathbb{R}\). A point \(x\in\mathbb{R}\) is an accumulation point, or limit point, of \(A\) if \[\text{for every }\delta>0,\quad \text{there exists }a\in A\text{ such that }0<|a-x|<\delta.\] Equivalently, every interval \((x-\delta,x+\delta)\) contains a point of \(A\) different from \(x\).

We exclude \(x\) itself. Otherwise, every member of every set would satisfy the condition merely by being present, even if there were no other points nearby. Also, the definition does not require \(x\in A\): a set can gather around a point that it does not contain.

Definition 7.4

Isolated Point

A point \(x\in A\) is isolated in \(A\) if there exists \(\delta>0\) such that \[A\cap(x-\delta,x+\delta)=\{x\}.\] Thus some window around \(x\) contains no other point of \(A\).

For a point belonging to \(A\), these conditions are exact alternatives. If it is not an accumulation point, then some window contains no other point of \(A\); that is precisely isolation. A point outside \(A\), however, cannot be an isolated point of \(A\). It may be an accumulation point, or it may lie completely away from \(A\).

[Figure being adapted for the online edition.]

Here \(A=\{-3,-1\}\cup\{1/n:n\in\mathbb{N}\}\). Only finitely many of the red points are drawn. The point \(-3\) is isolated; \(0\) is an accumulation point even though \(0\notin A\).

Five examples to separate the definitions

  1. The natural numbers have no accumulation point in \(\mathbb{R}\). An interval of length less than \(1\) contains at most one integer. If a proposed accumulation point \(x\) is itself a natural number, a radius less than \(1\) excludes all the other natural numbers. If \(x\notin\mathbb{N}\), first take a sufficiently short window containing at most one natural number. If there is one, shrink the radius below its positive distance from \(x\). The smaller window contains none. In both cases, one window fails the accumulation-point condition.

  2. For \(A=\{1/n:n\in\mathbb{N}\}\), the only accumulation point is \(0\). Given \(\delta>0\), choose \(n>1/\delta\). Then \(0<1/n<\delta\), so every window around \(0\) contains a member of \(A\) different from \(0\). Notice that \(0\) itself is not in \(A\).

    Every member \(1/n\) of \(A\) is isolated. For \(n\ge2\), choose a radius smaller than both gaps to the neighbouring values \(1/(n-1)\) and \(1/(n+1)\). For \(n=1\), a radius less than \(1/2\) suffices. There are no other accumulation points. If \(x\ne0\), all sufficiently late terms \(1/n\) lie near \(0\) and stay a positive distance from \(x\). Only finitely many earlier values remain, and a small enough window can avoid those values other than \(x\).

  3. The accumulation points of \((a,b)\) are exactly \([a,b]\), where \(a<b\). Every point inside the interval has other interval points arbitrarily close to it. The endpoints do too: points just to the right of \(a\) approach \(a\), and points just to the left of \(b\) approach \(b\). Thus an endpoint may be an accumulation point without belonging to the set. If \(x<a\) or \(x>b\), choose a radius smaller than its distance from \([a,b]\); the resulting window misses \((a,b)\).

  4. Removing one interior point does not remove its neighbours. For \(a<c<b\), the set \[(a,c)\cup(c,b)\] still has accumulation-point set \([a,b]\). In particular, every window around the missing point \(c\) contains points on either side sufficiently close to \(c\). Removing \(c\) has not created an empty window around it.

  5. A nondegenerate interval has no isolated points. Here nondegenerate means that the interval contains more than one point. If \(x\) belongs to it, choose another point \(y\) in the interval. Points strictly between \(x\) and \(y\), taken sufficiently close to \(x\), belong to the same interval and lie in any prescribed window about \(x\). This works for an included endpoint as well as for an interior point.

There is also a useful sequential description of a set accumulation point: \[x\text{ is an accumulation point of }A \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \text{some sequence in }A\setminus\{x\}\text{ converges to }x.\] For the forward implication, choose \(a_n\in A\) with \(0<|a_n-x|<1/n\). The estimate proves \(a_n\to x\). Conversely, if \(a_n\to x\) and every \(a_n\ne x\), then any window around \(x\) contains a sufficiently late \(a_n\), which is the other point required by the definition. We shall return in the next lecture to the distinction between a limit point of a set and a limit point of a sequence.

Three Ways to Say That a Set Is Dense

We now ask for something stronger than gathering near one point. Can a set come as close as we wish to every point of the real line? We can express this by windows, by approximation sequences, or by intervals that never remain empty.

Definition 7.5

Three Equivalent Views of Density

For \(S\subseteq\mathbb{R}\), the following are equivalent and define the statement “\(S\) is dense in \(\mathbb{R}\)”:

  1. For every \(x\in\mathbb{R}\) and every \(\varepsilon>0\), there exists \(s\in S\) with \[|x-s|<\varepsilon.\]

  2. Every \(x\in\mathbb{R}\) is the limit of a sequence of elements of \(S\).

  3. Every nonempty open interval \((a,b)\), with \(a<b\), contains an element of \(S\).

The first statement lets us prescribe the centre and the accuracy. The second asks us to keep improving approximations to one chosen number. The third says that no open interval, however short, misses the set. These are three descriptions of the same property.

In the first statement, \(s=x\) is allowed when \(x\in S\). Compare this carefully with an accumulation point of a set, where the nearby point must be different from the centre. Density concerns the ability to approximate every real point; accumulation concerns other points arbitrarily close to one specified point.

We shall begin the next lecture by unpacking these three statements and proving their equivalence. Then we shall prove formally the familiar claims that rational and irrational numbers occur between any two distinct real numbers.
