Seeing What a Function Limit Means

Read this lecture online, then continue to the next chapter. Figures and exercises are being adapted separately for the web edition.

Why This Definition Is Natural

Motivation 9.1

Remember the pendulum experiment from our discussion of sequences. One fortunate reading close to the resting position was not enough: the pendulum might swing far away again. What we wanted was a guarantee. First we specified how much error we were prepared to allow, and then asked for a stage after which every reading met that demand. A narrower allowed band required a stronger guarantee.

For a function, keep the same question but change the way we choose the inputs. Imagine an experiment with a controlled setting \(x\) and a measured response \(f(x)\). We are interested in settings near a fixed value \(a\), and we suspect that the readings should be near \(L\). If I say, “I want every reading to be within \(0.1\) of \(L\)”, can you tell me how close the setting must be to \(a\)? If I change my demand to \(0.01\), can you give me a suitable new restriction? We are describing the logic of the experiment; we are not assuming that every physical experiment necessarily has a limit.

The order is natural: first decide the accuracy required of the output; then decide how accurately the input must be controlled. For instance, with the response \(f(x)=x+3\) near \(a=3\), keeping the input error below \(0.1\) keeps the output error from \(6\) below \(0.1\), since \(|f(x)-6|=|x-3|\). The important promise concerns every allowed input in that small region, not just the few readings we happen to try.

The symbols \(\varepsilon\) and \(\delta\) will simply give names to these two positive tolerances: \(\varepsilon\) for the permitted output error, and \(\delta\) for a sufficient input restriction. We never ask for an error tolerance of zero. Instead, we ask whether every positive demand, however small, can be met. We shall now explore a concrete example, draw the two windows, and then write this everyday guarantee as a precise definition.

A Missing Value, but a Visible Destination

Motivation 9.2

Let us begin with a question which can be explored before we introduce any new symbols: \[f(x)=\frac{x^2-9}{x-3},\qquad x\ne3.\] What happens to the outputs if the input gets closer and closer to \(3\)? We cannot substitute \(x=3\) in this formula: both numerator and denominator would be zero. But we can choose inputs near \(3\) and look at their outputs.

For every allowed input, factorisation gives \[\frac{x^2-9}{x-3}=\frac{(x-3)(x+3)}{x-3}=x+3.\] The simplified formula makes the following table easy to calculate.

Inputs below \(3\) Inputs above \(3\)
\(x\) \(f(x)\) \(x\) \(f(x)\)
\(2\) \(5\) \(4\) \(7\)
\(2.9\) \(5.9\) \(3.1\) \(6.1\)
\(2.99\) \(5.99\) \(3.01\) \(6.01\)
\(2.999\) \(5.999\) \(3.001\) \(6.001\)

On either side, the inputs move towards \(3\) and the outputs move towards \(6\). This suggests \[\lim_{x\to3}\frac{x^2-9}{x-3}=6.\] Notice that this is a claim about the nearby outputs. It does not assign a value to the original formula at \(3\).

How the Idea of a Limit Became Precise

Historical note 9.1

Before meeting the symbols \(\varepsilon\) and \(\delta\), let us ask why mathematicians needed them. A table can strongly suggest that an output is approaching a number. A graph can make the approach look convincing. But how do we turn that impression into a statement that works for every sufficiently close input, including inputs that our table never lists?

This difficulty is part of the history of calculus. In the seventeenth century, Pierre de Fermat developed methods for tangents and for maximum and minimum problems, using what he called adequality. These methods helped mathematicians calculate with small changes. They were important predecessors of calculus; they were not the modern \(\varepsilon\)–\(\delta\) definition. We should distinguish an early method for finding an answer from the later language used to justify such answers.1

Isaac Newton also had to explain what happens to ratios when the quantities involved become very small. In Book I, Section I of the Principia, he described first and ultimate ratios. In Andrew Motte’s English translation, he says that the ultimate ratios are

“not truly the ratios of ultimate quantities, but limits towards which the ratios of quantities decreasing without limit do always converge”.

The point is especially relevant to a quotient whose numerator and denominator both become small: the answer is not obtained by taking a ratio of two final zeros. Newton’s discussion concerns the limiting behaviour of ratios.2

The nineteenth century brought a more systematic search for precise foundations. Bernard Bolzano’s work of 1817 was an important step towards an analytic treatment of continuity. In his Cours d’analyse of 1821, Augustin-Louis Cauchy explained a limit through variable values approaching a fixed value with an error that can become as small as desired. These were major steps towards rigorous analysis, although their language and foundations should not simply be identified with every detail of a present-day textbook.3

Karl Weierstrass’s nineteenth-century teaching developed the systematic use of inequalities of the kind we now express using \(\varepsilon\) and \(\delta\). His 1861 differential-calculus lectures are an important source for this development. The crucial feature for us is the order of the demands: first prescribe how accurate the output must be; then find an input restriction that guarantees that accuracy. The statement is about numbers and inequalities, and does not require an imagined particle to move with time.4

Later, G. H. Hardy’s A Course of Pure Mathematics, first published in 1908, helped bring rigorous analysis into English university teaching. Hardy did not invent the definition. His role here is that of an influential expositor: his book carefully teaches limits through prescribed errors and suitable bounds on the input.5

So the definition that follows is not an arbitrary collection of Greek letters. It answers a very old question: what exact guarantee is hidden inside the phrase “the output approaches \(L\)”? Our pictures will make the guarantee visible; the inequalities will make it provable.

From an Output Demand to an Input Guarantee

Write \(f:A\to\mathbb{R}\), where \(A\subseteq\mathbb{R}\). We approach \(c\) through points of \(A\). We assume that \(c\) is an accumulation point of \(A\): every interval \((c-r,c+r)\), \(r>0\), contains a point of \(A\) different from \(c\). This gives us nearby inputs to work with. The point \(c\) itself need not belong to \(A\).

Definition 9.1

Deleted Neighbourhood

The deleted or punctured \(\delta\)-neighbourhood of \(c\) is \[(c-\delta,c)\cup(c,c+\delta) =\{x\in\mathbb{R}:0<|x-c|<\delta\},\qquad\delta>0.\] For a function on \(A\), only points in the intersection of this set with \(A\) are considered.

Definition 9.2

Limit of a Function

Let \(c\) be an accumulation point of \(A\). We write \[\lim_{x\to c}f(x)=L\] if, for every \(\varepsilon>0\), there exists \(\delta>0\) such that \[x\in A,\qquad0<|x-c|<\delta \quad\Longrightarrow\quad |f(x)-L|<\varepsilon.\] This is the \(\varepsilon\)–\(\delta\) definition, also called Cauchy’s definition of a function limit.

The number \(\varepsilon\) measures the permitted output error; \(\delta\) tells us how small the input error must be. Read the order carefully: \[\forall\varepsilon>0\quad\exists\delta>0\quad\forall x\in A: \quad0<|x-c|<\delta\Longrightarrow|f(x)-L|<\varepsilon.\] First someone chooses \(\varepsilon\). We then choose \(\delta\), possibly depending on \(\varepsilon\), \(c\), and the function. After that, the same \(\delta\) must work for every admissible \(x\). We cannot wait to see \(x\) and then change \(\delta\).

[Figure being adapted for the online edition.]

Why is the centre removed?

The inequality \(0<|x-c|\) says that \(x\ne c\). A limit examines values near \(c\); it does not ask for the value at \(c\). We may change \(f(c)\), or leave it undefined, without changing the limit.

[Figure being adapted for the online edition.]

Warning 9.1

A picture can suggest a limit, but it does not prove one. Also, a function is not required to be defined on a whole interval around \(c\): its own domain determines which inputs are admissible. At an isolated domain point there is no approach through other domain points, so we do not define a function limit there by a vacuous condition.

What does “approaches” mean in this definition?

In this picture, \(a\) is the fixed input point denoted by \(c\) in the definition. The expression \(x\to a\) does not describe one particular journey, and it does not require the input to move at a particular speed. It asks for a guarantee about all sufficiently close inputs. To understand that guarantee, start at the output end.

Choose an open interval \[V=(L-\varepsilon,L+\varepsilon)\] around the proposed limit \(L\). We want every nearby output to fall into this interval. We then seek an open interval \[U=(a-\delta,a+\delta)\] around the input point \(a\) which makes that happen. If the domain is \(A\), the exact requirement is \[\boxed{\quad f\bigl((U\setminus\{a\})\cap A\bigr)\subseteq V.\quad}\] In words: take the points of the input neighbourhood which belong to the domain, remove the centre point, and send all the remaining points through the function. Their entire image must lie inside the prescribed output neighbourhood.

[Figure being adapted for the online edition.]

Now make \(V\) smaller, so the permitted output error is smaller. We are allowed to choose a different, usually smaller, input neighbourhood \(U\). A limit exists with value \(L\) when this can be done for every positive output tolerance. There is no final, smallest tolerance to reach. We never put \(\varepsilon=0\): instead, we meet every positive demand.

Checkpoint 9.1

First choose the output tolerance \(\varepsilon>0\). Next choose one input radius \(\delta>0\). Finally, test any domain point with \(0<|x-a|<\delta\). The chosen radius must work for all of them. It may depend on \(\varepsilon\) and the fixed function, but it may not be changed after the test input is chosen.

A working radius need not be the largest radius

For \(f(x)=x+3\) at \(a=3\), with output tolerance \(\varepsilon=0.1\), the choice \(\delta=0.1\) works. So do \(\delta=0.05\) and \(\delta=0.001\). These smaller choices merely admit fewer inputs. In general, if \(\delta\) works, then every \(0<\delta'\le\delta\) also works.

We need to find a suitable positive radius, not the best possible one. Choosing a minimum such as \(\min\{1,\varepsilon/5\}\) is therefore perfectly legitimate: one restriction keeps the input in a region we understand, and the other makes the output error sufficiently small.

Remark 9.1

An arbitrary open neighbourhood \(V\) of \(L\) contains some interval \((L-\varepsilon,L+\varepsilon)\). If we put the entire relevant image inside that smaller interval, it is also inside \(V\). Conversely, these symmetric intervals are themselves open neighbourhoods. Thus the window formulation and the \(\varepsilon\)–\(\delta\) formulation say exactly the same thing.

The axes have the same scales in all three pictures. Blue marks the permitted output band around \(6\); orange marks a chosen input strip around \(3\). The thick green graph inside the strip remains inside the blue band. The open circle records the missing value at \(x=3\).

Let us read the pictures numerically. To make the output error less than \(1\), it is enough to make the input error less than \(1\). To demand an output error less than \(0.5\), use an input error less than \(0.5\). For an output error less than \(0.1\), use an input error less than \(0.1\). This works because the errors are exactly equal: \[|f(x)-6|=|x+3-6|=|x-3|\qquad(x\ne3).\] For any positive demand \(\varepsilon\), choosing \(\delta=\varepsilon\) therefore gives \[0<|x-3|<\delta\quad\Longrightarrow\quad |f(x)-6|<\varepsilon.\] That single calculation works for every positive \(\varepsilon\), not merely the three values we drew.

Warning 9.2

A table checks only finitely many chosen inputs. A graph has finite resolution. Neither can check every real input near \(3\). The estimate \(|f(x)-6|=|x-3|\) is stronger: it controls all the allowed inputs at once, however close they are to the missing point.

Designing a Delta: Direct Proofs

Method 9.1

Start with \(|f(x)-L|\) and find an estimate in terms of \(|x-c|\). This calculation suggests a choice of \(\delta\). The finished proof then runs forwards: fix \(\varepsilon>0\), state the choice of \(\delta\), assume \(0<|x-c|<\delta\), and prove the required output bound.

Example 9.1

Constants and the identity function

Prove directly that \(\lim_{x\to c}b=b\) and \(\lim_{x\to c}x=c\).

Explanation 9.1

For the constant function, any \(\delta>0\) works, because \(|b-b|=0<\varepsilon\). For the identity function choose \(\delta=\varepsilon\). Then \(0<|x-c|<\delta\) immediately gives \(|x-c|<\varepsilon\). These are the simplest examples of the definition.

Remark 9.2

Each \(\varepsilon>0\) is a fixed demand, and we choose a positive \(\delta\) to meet it. The definition does not require a particular formula \(\delta(\varepsilon)\), or even require that our choices tend to zero. For a constant function equal to its proposed limit, \(\delta=1\) works for every positive \(\varepsilon\). A smaller radius also works, but there is no need to use one.

Example 9.2

Two linear functions

Prove, using the definition, \[\lim_{x\to1}(3x-8)=-5,\qquad \lim_{x\to1}(3x-2)=1.\]

Explanation 9.2

In the first problem the output error is \[|(3x-8)-(-5)|=|3x-3|=3|x-1|.\] Given \(\varepsilon>0\), choose \(\delta=\varepsilon/3\). Then \(0<|x-1|<\delta\) implies that this error is less than \(3\delta=\varepsilon\). In the second problem, \[|(3x-2)-1|=3|x-1|,\] so exactly the same choice works. More generally, a nonconstant linear function \(ax+b\) has limit \(ac+b\) at \(c\), with \(\delta=\varepsilon/|a|\).

A steeper line needs a narrower input strip

For \(f(x)=3x-8\) at \(a=1\), the proposed output limit is \(-5\) and \[|f(x)-(-5)|=3|x-1|.\] The output error is three times the input error. Hence an output demand \(\varepsilon\) is met by choosing \(\delta=\varepsilon/3\).

The three chosen radii are \(1/3\), \(1/6\), and \(1/30\), respectively. The same error calculation applies to \(g(x)=3x-2\) at \(1\), whose output limit is \(1\):

Moving the graph up or down changes the proposed output limit, but it does not change the relation between the two errors.

Seeing a failed choice, then repairing it

Suppose the required output error is \(0.3\) for \(f(x)=3x-8\) at \(1\). The choice \(\delta=0.2\) is too generous: the admissible input \(x=1.15\) has input error \(0.15<0.2\), but output error \(3(0.15)=0.45>0.3\). Some of the graph in the orange strip escapes the blue band. Reducing the radius to \(\delta=0.1\) repairs the problem, since \(3|x-1|<0.3\) for every allowed input.

Left: the proposed radius fails. Right: the repaired radius works. A failed radius does not disprove the limit; it tells us to choose a more suitable radius.

Example 9.3

A quadratic at an arbitrary point

Prove that \(\lim_{x\to c}x^2=c^2\) for every real \(c\).

Explanation 9.3

Factor the error: \[|x^2-c^2|=|x-c||x+c|.\] The factor \(|x-c|\) is under our control, but \(|x+c|\) still moves with \(x\). First impose \(|x-c|<1\). Then \[|x+c|\le|x-c|+2|c|<1+2|c|.\] For a given \(\varepsilon>0\) choose \[\delta=\min\left\{1,\frac{\varepsilon}{1+2|c|}\right\}.\] If \(0<|x-c|<\delta\), both restrictions hold, and \[|x^2-c^2|<(1+2|c|)|x-c|<\varepsilon.\] The minimum simply records two jobs: keep the extra factor bounded, and make the final error small.

A curved graph: the square function

For \(f(x)=x^2\) at \(a=2\), the proposed limit is \(4\). The error factorises as \[|x^2-4|=|x-2||x+2|.\] If \(|x-2|<1\), then \(1<x<3\) and \(|x+2|<5\). Thus the choice \[\delta=\min\{1,\varepsilon/5\}\] works. Here are three positive tolerances with the radii supplied by that rule.

The curvature means that equal input displacements on the two sides need not give equal output errors. The estimate \(|x+2|<5\) handles both sides at once. We chose an easily proved radius rather than calculating the largest possible one.

A magnified view of the third panel. Here we have changed only the axis scales, so that the narrow input strip can be inspected more closely. The function, the point \(a=2\), the proposed limit \(4\), and the chosen numbers \(\varepsilon=0.1\), \(\delta=0.02\) are exactly the same. The enlarged picture shows why every selected output stays between \(3.9\) and \(4.1\).

Example 9.4

Controlling a denominator

For \(c\ne0\), prove that \(\lim_{x\to c}1/x=1/c\).

Explanation 9.4

We have \[\left|\frac1x-\frac1c\right|=\frac{|x-c|}{|x||c|}.\] To keep the denominator away from zero, require \(|x-c|<|c|/2\). The reverse triangle inequality gives \[|x|\ge |c|-|x-c|>|c|/2.\] Hence the error is less than \(2|x-c|/|c|^2\). Choose \[\delta=\min\left\{\frac{|c|}{2},\frac{\varepsilon|c|^2}{2}\right\}.\] For \(0<|x-c|<\delta\), the displayed bounds give an error smaller than \(\varepsilon\). Notice that this proof works for negative as well as positive \(c\).

A denominator tells us to keep away from zero

For \(f(x)=1/x\) at \(a=1\), the output limit is \(1\). If \(|x-1|<1/2\), then \(x>1/2\) and \[\left|\frac1x-1\right|=\frac{|x-1|}{|x|}<2|x-1|.\] It is enough to choose \(\delta=\min\{1/2,\varepsilon/2\}\). The first restriction prevents the denominator from getting too small; the second controls the output error.

The radii are sufficient, even though they are not generally maximal. As the requested blue band narrows, our chosen orange strip narrows enough to keep all the corresponding outputs inside it.

Example 9.5

A radical with a missing value

Prove directly that \[\lim_{x\to1}\frac{x-1}{\sqrt x-1}=2.\]

Explanation 9.5

The domain is \([0,\infty)\setminus\{1\}\). At every admissible input, \[\frac{x-1}{\sqrt x-1}=\sqrt x+1.\] Consequently, \[\left|\frac{x-1}{\sqrt x-1}-2\right| =|\sqrt x-1|=\frac{|x-1|}{\sqrt x+1}\le|x-1|.\] Given \(\varepsilon>0\), choose \(\delta=\varepsilon\). The required bound follows. There is no need to define the original quotient at \(1\).

Cancelling a missing factor does not fill the hole

For \(f(x)=(x-1)/(\sqrt{x}-1)\), the original domain excludes \(1\). Away from that point, the function equals \(\sqrt{x}+1\), and \[|f(x)-2|=\frac{|x-1|}{\sqrt{x}+1}\le|x-1|.\] The proof permits \(\delta=\varepsilon\). To keep the pictures in a small positive-input region, we may use the smaller choice \(\delta=\min\{1/2,\varepsilon\}\) instead.

In the first two pictures the same radius works for two different tolerances. The definition does not require the chosen radius to change every time the tolerance changes. It requires only that the chosen radius meet the particular demand.

Example 9.6

Another missing point and an endpoint

Prove that \[\lim_{x\to9}\frac{x-9}{\sqrt x-3}=6, \qquad \lim_{\substack{x\to0\\x\ge0}}\sqrt x=0.\]

Explanation 9.6

For the first limit, cancellation gives \(\sqrt x+3\) when \(x\ne9\). Thus \[\left|\frac{x-9}{\sqrt x-3}-6\right| =\frac{|x-9|}{\sqrt x+3}\le\frac{|x-9|}{3}.\] Choose \(\delta=3\varepsilon\). For the second limit choose \(\delta=\varepsilon^2\): every domain point with \(0<x<\delta\) satisfies \(|\sqrt x|<\varepsilon\). The domain restriction is part of the statement; negative real inputs are not being tested.

At an endpoint, use only the available inputs

For \(f(x)=\sqrt{x}\) on \([0,\infty)\) at \(a=0\), the proposed output limit is \(0\). The input interval around \(0\) meets the domain only on the nonnegative side. The condition we must control is therefore \(0<x<\delta\). \[0<x<\varepsilon^2\quad\Longrightarrow\quad0<\sqrt{x}<\varepsilon.\] Choose \(\delta=\varepsilon^2\).

Here making \(\varepsilon\) ten times smaller makes this particular choice of \(\delta\) one hundred times smaller. There is no universal formula linking the two symbols: the function determines the error estimate. The increasingly narrow orange strip is a feature of the square-root graph, not a change in the meaning of a limit.

The same third panel, magnified. The axes now show a smaller region near \((0,0)\), but the tolerance and chosen radius remain \(\varepsilon=0.1\) and \(\delta=0.01\). A useful geometric estimate for trigonometric functions

All angles in these notes are in radians. Elementary unit-circle geometry gives \[|\sin u|\le|u|\qquad(u\in\mathbb{R}).\] For \(0<u<\pi/2\), compare the area of the inscribed triangle with the circular sector to get \(\sin u\le u\); use symmetry for negative \(u\). For \(|u|\ge1\), the inequality follows from \(|\sin u|\le1\le|u|\), so the estimate covers all real \(u\). The addition identities then give \[|\sin x-\sin c| =2\left|\cos\frac{x+c}{2}\sin\frac{x-c}{2}\right| \le|x-c|,\] \[|\cos x-\cos c| =2\left|\sin\frac{x+c}{2}\sin\frac{x-c}{2}\right| \le|x-c|.\] These are estimates, obtained before using any general theorem about continuity.

Example 9.7

Three trigonometric limits from the definition

Prove \[\lim_{x\to0}\sin x=0,\qquad \lim_{x\to0}\cos x=1,\qquad \lim_{x\to\pi/6}\sin x=\frac12.\]

Explanation 9.7

For the sine limits, the preceding inequality gives \[|\sin x-\sin c|\le|x-c|.\] Choose \(\delta=\varepsilon\) and then put \(c=0\) or \(c=\pi/6\), recalling that \(\sin0=0\) and \(\sin(\pi/6)=1/2\). For cosine use \(|\cos x-\cos0|\le|x|\) and choose \(\delta=\varepsilon\) again. Each output error is less than \(\varepsilon\) for every allowed input.

\(\varepsilon=0.3\), \(\delta=0.3\)

\(\varepsilon=0.15\), \(\delta=0.15\)

\(\varepsilon=0.05\), \(\delta=0.05\)

For \(\sin x\) at \(a=\pi/6\), the blue output window is centred at \(L=1/2\). The orange input window is centred at \(a\). The axes are identical in all three pictures. Since \(|\sin x-1/2|\le|x-\pi/6|\), choosing \(\delta=\varepsilon\) guarantees that the whole highlighted curve segment, apart from the excluded centre input, lies within the blue window. The same error estimate explains the choices for \(\sin x\) and \(\cos x\) at zero.

Example 9.8

Approaching Does Not Mean Moving Steadily

The function \[g(x)=(x-3)\sin\frac1{x-3},\qquad x\ne3,\] keeps changing direction near \(3\). Can its output nevertheless approach \(0\)?

Explanation 9.8

Yes. Since the absolute value of every sine is at most \(1\), \[|g(x)-0|\le|x-3|.\] For any prescribed \(\varepsilon>0\), choose \(\delta=\varepsilon\). Every input with \(0<|x-3|<\delta\) then gives \(|g(x)|<\varepsilon\). This is a direct proof from the definition. The output may go up and down, or equal the limiting value many times, while still obeying every sufficiently small output window.

A Limit Cannot Have Two Values

Theorem 9.1

Uniqueness of a Function Limit

At an accumulation point of its domain, a function has at most one finite limit.

Show proof

Proof. Suppose \(L_1\ne L_2\) are both limits and put \(d=|L_1-L_2|>0\). Apply each limit definition with tolerance \(d/3\), obtaining radii \(\delta_1,\delta_2\). Since \(c\) is an accumulation point, there is a domain point \(x\) with \(0<|x-c|<\min(\delta_1,\delta_2)\). At that point, \[d=|L_1-L_2|\le|L_1-f(x)|+|f(x)-L_2|<\frac{2d}{3},\] which is impossible. ◻

When a Proposed Limit Fails

Motivation 9.3

At the beginning of our course, we learnt how to negate a statement. Let us use that skill again. “For every” changes to “there exists”, and “there exists” changes to “for every”. But changing the quantifiers is only part of the work: we must also say precisely how the final condition fails.

Someone proposes a number \(L\) as the limit. We now try to defeat that claim. Can we choose one output tolerance which no input radius can guarantee? If we can, then this particular \(L\) is not the limit.

Recall the assertion we are trying to negate: \[\forall\varepsilon>0\ \exists\delta>0\ \forall x\in A: \quad 0<|x-a|<\delta\ \Longrightarrow\ |f(x)-L|<\varepsilon.\] The negation of an implication \(P\Rightarrow Q\) is \(P\) holds and \(Q\) fails. It is not the implication “\(P\) implies not \(Q\)”. Thus, to make the limit condition fail, we need a nearby admissible input whose output is still too far away: \[0<|x-a|<\delta \qquad\text{and}\qquad |f(x)-L|\ge\varepsilon_0.\] Notice the weak inequality \(\ge\): this is the exact negation of \(<\).

Definition 9.3

A Number Which Is Not the Limit

Let \(a\) be an accumulation point of the domain \(A\). A proposed real number \(L\) is not the limit of \(f\) as \(x\to a\) if and only if \[\exists\varepsilon_0>0\ \forall\delta>0\ \exists x\in A: \qquad \begin{cases} 0<|x-a|<\delta,\\ |f(x)-L|\ge\varepsilon_0. \end{cases}\] In words: one fixed output demand defeats every positive input radius.

Read the order as a short conversation. First we choose \(\varepsilon_0>0\). Someone then offers any \(\delta>0\), however small. After seeing that radius, we find a troublesome input \(x\) inside its punctured neighbourhood. The troublesome input may depend on \(\delta\); the positive number \(\varepsilon_0\) must remain fixed throughout.

Warning 9.3

Showing that one choice of \(\delta\) fails does not disprove a proposed limit. A smaller radius might work. To disprove it, we must defeat every positive radius using the same fixed output tolerance. Also, the troublesome input must satisfy \(x\ne a\) and belong to the domain.

Example 9.9

Why Four Is Not the Limit of the Square at Three

Use the definition to disprove \[\lim_{x\to3}x^2=4.\]

Explanation 9.9

The proposed output limit is \(4\). Choose the fixed tolerance \(\varepsilon_0=1\), so the required output window would be \((3,5)\).

Now let any \(\delta>0\) be offered. Choose \[x=3+\frac\delta2.\] This is an admissible input, different from \(3\), and \[0<|x-3|=\frac\delta2<\delta.\] But \(x>3\), so \(x^2>9\), and therefore \[|x^2-4|=x^2-4>5>1=\varepsilon_0.\] We have found a troublesome input for every offered radius. Thus \(4\) is not the limit, directly by the negated definition.

[Figure being adapted for the online edition.]

The drawing illustrates the failed output window with one radius, \(\delta=0.2\). The argument above is stronger: it defeats every radius, not merely the one shown.

Warning 9.4

The square function does have a limit at \(3\): its limit is \(9\), as the earlier direct proof of \(x^2\to a^2\) shows. We have disproved the answer \(4\), not the existence of a limit. These are different statements.

Disproving Every Finite Candidate

To say that a function has no finite limit at \(a\), we must rule out every proposed real number, rather than just one of them. The full statement is \[\forall L\in\mathbb{R}\ \exists\varepsilon_0>0\ \forall\delta>0\ \exists x\in A: \quad 0<|x-a|<\delta, \quad |f(x)-L|\ge\varepsilon_0.\] Here \(\varepsilon_0\) may depend on the candidate \(L\), but it may not depend on the subsequently offered radius \(\delta\).

Example 9.10

Two Sides Which Cannot Fit One Output Window

Prove directly from the definition that \[f(x)=\frac{|x|}{x},\qquad x\ne0,\] has no finite limit as \(x\to0\).

Explanation 9.10

Let \(L\in\mathbb{R}\) be any proposed limit. We will use the fixed tolerance \(\varepsilon_0=1\). Let \(\delta>0\) be arbitrary.

If \(L\ge0\), choose \(x=-\delta/2\). Then \(0<|x|<\delta\), while \(f(x)=-1\) and \[|f(x)-L|=|-1-L|=1+L\ge1.\] If \(L<0\), choose \(x=\delta/2\). Again \(0<|x|<\delta\), but \(f(x)=1\) and \[|f(x)-L|=|1-L|=1-L>1.\] Thus, whichever real number is proposed, every positive radius admits an input whose output misses the required window. No finite limit exists.

Every punctured interval around zero contains inputs giving each of the two heights. Making the input interval narrower cannot remove that disagreement.

Example 9.11

Rational and Irrational Inputs Keep Both Outputs Nearby

Let \[D(x)=\begin{cases} 1,&x\in\mathbb{Q},\\ 0,&x\notin\mathbb{Q}. \end{cases}\] Use the density results from Lecture 8 and the negated limit definition to prove that \(D\) has no finite limit at \(0\).

Explanation 9.11

Let \(L\in\mathbb{R}\) be any proposed limit. Choose \(\varepsilon_0=1/2\). For every \(\delta>0\), the interval \((0,\delta)\) contains both a rational number and an irrational number, by density. These are nonzero inputs whose distances from zero are less than \(\delta\).

If \(L\ge1/2\), choose an irrational \(x\) in that interval. Then \(D(x)=0\), so \(|D(x)-L|=|L|\ge1/2\). If \(L<1/2\), choose a rational \(x\) there. Then \(D(x)=1\), so \(|D(x)-L|=1-L>1/2\).

For every proposed \(L\), one fixed output demand therefore defeats every input radius. Consequently \(D\) has no finite limit at zero. The same argument works at any real input point, using \((a,a+\delta)\).

[Figure being adapted for the online edition.]

The dots are a schematic sample, not a numerical plot or the whole graph. The density theorem gives the essential information which no finite drawing can display: both output heights occur in every nonempty open input interval, however small.

We can now describe both success and failure precisely. A successful limit meets every positive output demand with a suitable input radius. For a failed candidate, one positive demand survives every attempt to shrink the input neighbourhood. To prove that no finite limit exists, we must defeat every real candidate in this way.
