One-Sided Limits, Squeezing, and Worked Examples

Read this lecture online, then continue to the next chapter. Figures and exercises are being adapted separately for the web edition.

When Do the Two Sides Give a Full Limit?

At the end of Lecture 11 we defined the left- and right-hand limits. In the piecewise example, the left side approached \(1\) and the right side approached \(-2\). A full limit could not satisfy both requirements. If the two answers agree, can we find one radius that works on both sides?

Theorem 12.1

Two-Sided Limit Criterion

Assume that \(A\) has points arbitrarily close to \(c\) on both sides. Then \[\lim_{x\to c}f(x)=L \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \lim_{x\to c^-}f(x)=\lim_{x\to c^+}f(x)=L.\] In particular, existence of the two one-sided limits gives a two-sided limit precisely when the two answers agree.

Show proof

Proof. Suppose first that \(f(x)\to L\) as \(x\to c\). Given \(\varepsilon>0\), take \(\delta\) from the two-sided definition. Every \(x\in A\) satisfying \(0<x-c<\delta\) also satisfies \(0<|x-c|<\delta\), and hence \(|f(x)-L|<\varepsilon\). This proves the right-hand limit. Exactly the same \(\delta\) proves the left-hand limit.

Conversely, suppose that both one-sided limits equal \(L\). Fix \(\varepsilon>0\). The left-hand limit provides \(\delta_->0\), and the right-hand limit provides \(\delta_+>0\). Set \[\delta=\min\{\delta_-,\delta_+\}.\] Take any \(x\in A\) with \(0<|x-c|<\delta\). If \(x<c\), the left-hand definition gives \(|f(x)-L|<\varepsilon\); if \(x>c\), the right-hand definition gives the same inequality. These are all the possibilities. Thus this single \(\delta\) works for every admissible \(x\), which proves \(f(x)\to L\). ◻

Warning 12.1

Both one-sided limits may exist and still be different. Their mere existence does not imply a two-sided limit. Also, at an endpoint of a domain there may be only one available side: for \(f:[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}\), a limit at \(0\) relative to its domain is its right-hand limit. We do not invent a left-hand limit where there are no domain points approaching from the left.

One-Sided Infinite Limits

The same side restrictions apply to infinite limits. For instance, \(\lim_{x\to c^+}f(x)=+\infty\) means that, for every \(B>0\), there exists \(\delta>0\) such that \[x\in A,\qquad 0<x-c<\delta\quad\Longrightarrow\quad f(x)>B.\] Replace \(f(x)>B\) by \(f(x)<-B\) for \(-\infty\), and replace \(0<x-c<\delta\) by \(0<c-x<\delta\) for the left side. An infinite limit describes unbounded behaviour; it is not a finite real number.

Reading the Two Sides: Detailed Examples

Example 12.1

An Absolute Value Changes the Sign

Find the one-sided limits of \(\displaystyle\frac{x^2-1}{|x-1|}\) as \(x\to1\).

Explanation 12.1

Factor first, but keep track of the denominator’s sign: \[\frac{x^2-1}{|x-1|}=(x+1)\frac{x-1}{|x-1|}.\] For \(x<1\), \(|x-1|=-(x-1)\), so the expression equals \(-(x+1)\) and its left-hand limit is \(-2\). For \(x>1\), it equals \(x+1\), whose right-hand limit is \(2\). Consequently the two-sided limit does not exist.

Example 12.2

A Square Root Produces an Absolute Value

Find the one-sided limits of \(\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{1-\cos2x}}x\) at \(0\).

Explanation 12.2

The identity \(1-\cos2x=2\sin^2x\) gives \[\frac{\sqrt{1-\cos2x}}x=\sqrt2\,\frac{|\sin x|}{x}.\] For \(0<x<\pi/2\), \(\sin x>0\), so this equals \(\sqrt2\,\sin x/x\) and tends to \(\sqrt2\). For \(-\pi/2<x<0\), \(\sin x<0\), so it equals \(-\sqrt2\,\sin x/x\) and tends to \(-\sqrt2\). Thus \[\lim_{x\to0^-}\frac{\sqrt{1-\cos2x}}x=-\sqrt2, \qquad \lim_{x\to0^+}\frac{\sqrt{1-\cos2x}}x=\sqrt2.\]

Warning 12.2

The square root is nonnegative: \(\sqrt{u^2}=|u|\). Writing \(\sqrt{\sin^2x}=\sin x\) on both sides of zero would erase the entire distinction in this example.

Example 12.3

A Rapid Transition Hidden inside an Exponent

Find both one-sided limits at \(1\) of \[f(x)=3+\frac1{1+7^{1/(1-x)}}.\]

Explanation 12.3

As \(x\to1^-\), the positive number \(1-x\) tends to zero, so \(1/(1-x)\to+\infty\). Hence \(7^{1/(1-x)}\to+\infty\), the fraction tends to zero, and the left-hand limit is \(3\).

As \(x\to1^+\), \(1-x\) is negative and tends to zero, so \(1/(1-x)\to-\infty\). This time \(7^{1/(1-x)}\to0\), the fraction tends to \(1/(1+0)=1\), and the right-hand limit is \(4\). Therefore there is no two-sided limit at \(1\).

Notice the order of the reasoning: first determine the sign of \(1-x\), next determine the exponent’s behaviour, and only then evaluate the outer expression.

Example 12.4

Oscillation on Each Side Separately

Does \(f(x)=\cos(\pi/x)\) have a limit on either side of \(0\)?

Explanation 12.4

To test the right-hand limit, choose two positive input sequences \[x_n=\frac1{2n},\qquad y_n=\frac2{2n+1}.\] Both tend to \(0\) from the right, but \[f(x_n)=\cos(2n\pi)=1, \qquad f(y_n)=\cos\frac{(2n+1)\pi}{2}=0.\] If the right-hand limit existed, the sequential criterion applied to the right-side domain would force both output sequences to have the same limit. They do not, so the right-hand limit does not exist.

Since \(f(-x)=f(x)\), the negative sequences \(-x_n\) and \(-y_n\) give the same two output values while approaching from the left. Thus the left-hand limit also fails to exist. Splitting into two sides does not remove oscillation within either side.

Example 12.5

Opposite Infinite Limits

Find the one-sided limits of \(\displaystyle\frac5{(x-2)^3}\) at \(2\).

Explanation 12.5

On the right the denominator is positive and tends to zero, so the answer is \(+\infty\). On the left it is negative and tends to zero, so the answer is \(-\infty\). Here is the threshold proof. Given \(B>0\), take \[\delta=\left(\frac5B\right)^{1/3}.\] If \(0<x-2<\delta\), then \(5/(x-2)^3>B\). If \(0<2-x<\delta\), then \(5/(x-2)^3=-5/(2-x)^3<-B\). Thus \[\lim_{x\to2^-}\frac5{(x-2)^3}=-\infty, \qquad \lim_{x\to2^+}\frac5{(x-2)^3}=+\infty.\] There is neither a finite nor a single signed infinite two-sided limit.

Example 12.6

A Second Piecewise Function

Prove the one-sided limits at \(1\) for \[f(x)=\begin{cases}x+1,&0\le x<1,\\3x+2,&1\le x<3.\end{cases}\]

Explanation 12.6

For \(0\le x<1\), \(|f(x)-2|=|x-1|\), so \(\delta_-=\min\{1,\varepsilon\}\) proves \(\lim_{x\to1^-}f(x)=2\). For \(1<x<3\), \(|f(x)-5|=3|x-1|\), so \(\delta_+=\min\{1,\varepsilon/3\}\) proves \(\lim_{x\to1^+}f(x)=5\). The value \(f(1)=5\) agrees with the right-hand answer, but the left-hand answer remains \(2\). The two-sided limit does not exist.

Example 12.7

Three More Side Tests

Find the left- and right-hand limits at \(0\) of \[\text{(a) }\frac1{2-2^{1/x}},\qquad \text{(b) }e^{1/x},\qquad \text{(c) }\frac{|\sin x|}{x}.\]

Explanation 12.7

(a) On the left, \(1/x\to-\infty\), so \(2^{1/x}\to0\) and the quotient tends to \(1/2\). On the right, \(2^{1/x}\to+\infty\); hence \(2-2^{1/x}\to-\infty\), and its reciprocal tends to \(0\) through negative values. For an explicit right-side estimate, given \(\varepsilon>0\) take \(\delta>0\) so small that \(0<x<\delta\) implies \(2^{1/x}>2+1/\varepsilon\). Then \[\left|\frac1{2-2^{1/x}}\right| =\frac1{2^{1/x}-2}<\varepsilon.\] Thus the left limit is \(1/2\) and the right limit is \(0\).

(b) On the left, \(1/x\to-\infty\), giving \(e^{1/x}\to0\). On the right, \(1/x\to+\infty\), giving \(e^{1/x}\to+\infty\). To make the latter statement explicit, for any \(B>0\) choose \(K>0\) with \(e^K>B\) and put \(\delta=1/K\). Then \(0<x<\delta\) implies \(e^{1/x}>e^K>B\). The left-side proof is similar: choose \(K>0\) with \(e^{-K}<\varepsilon\); if \(-1/K<x<0\), then \(1/x<-K\) and \(0<e^{1/x}<\varepsilon\).

(c) Near zero, \(\sin x\) has the same sign as \(x\). Hence on the right \(|\sin x|/x=\sin x/x\to1\), while on the left \(|\sin x|/x=-\sin x/x\to-1\). Each side has a finite limit, but the answers disagree.

Order and the Squeeze Theorem

Motivation 12.1

Sometimes the middle function is difficult to simplify, but two simpler functions hold it between them. If both boundaries close in on the same number, where can the middle function go? This is the idea behind squeezing. It is especially useful when oscillation prevents direct substitution.

Theorem 12.2

Order Is Preserved by Finite Limits

Let \(f,g:A\to\mathbb{R}\) and let \(c\) be an accumulation point of \(A\). Suppose \(f(x)\le g(x)\) for all domain points sufficiently close to \(c\), except possibly \(c\) itself. If \(f(x)\to L\) and \(g(x)\to M\), then \(L\le M\).

Show proof

Proof. Suppose, to obtain a contradiction, that \(L>M\). Set \(\varepsilon=(L-M)/3>0\). Choose a common \(\delta>0\) so that the inequality \(f(x)\le g(x)\) holds and both estimates \(|f(x)-L|<\varepsilon\), \(|g(x)-M|<\varepsilon\) hold whenever \(x\in A\) and \(0<|x-c|<\delta\). Such domain points exist because \(c\) is an accumulation point. For any one of them, \[f(x)>L-\varepsilon>M+\varepsilon>g(x),\] contradicting \(f(x)\le g(x)\). Thus \(L\le M\). ◻

Remark 12.1

A strict inequality need not remain strict after taking limits. For example, \(0<x^2\) for \(x\ne0\), but both \(0\) and \(x^2\) tend to \(0\) as \(x\to0\).

Theorem 12.3

Squeeze Theorem for Functions

Let \(f,g,h:A\to\mathbb{R}\), and let \(c\) be an accumulation point of \(A\). Suppose \[f(x)\le g(x)\le h(x)\] for all \(x\in A\) sufficiently close to \(c\), with \(x\ne c\). If \[\lim_{x\to c}f(x)=\lim_{x\to c}h(x)=L,\] then \(\lim_{x\to c}g(x)=L\).

Show proof

Proof. Fix \(\varepsilon>0\). Let \(\delta_0>0\) make the trapping inequalities valid. Since the two outer limits are \(L\), choose \(\delta_1,\delta_2>0\) so that \[\begin{aligned} 0<|x-c|<\delta_1&\ \Longrightarrow\ L-\varepsilon<f(x)<L+\varepsilon,\\ 0<|x-c|<\delta_2&\ \Longrightarrow\ L-\varepsilon<h(x)<L+\varepsilon, \end{aligned}\] for \(x\in A\). Put \(\delta=\min\{\delta_0,\delta_1,\delta_2\}\). If \(x\in A\) and \(0<|x-c|<\delta\), then \[L-\varepsilon<f(x)\le g(x)\le h(x)<L+\varepsilon.\] Therefore \(|g(x)-L|<\varepsilon\), exactly as the limit definition requires. ◻

The same proof works for one-sided limits by restricting \(x\) to the relevant side. At \(+\infty\), choose a common threshold larger than the finitely many thresholds in the argument; at \(-\infty\), choose one smaller than all of them.

Warning 12.3

The outer limits must be equal. The inequalities \(-1\le\sin(1/x)\le1\) are true, but the two constant boundaries have different limits. They give boundedness, not convergence.

Example 12.8

Oscillation Whose Size Shrinks

Prove that \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to0}x\sin(1/x)=0\).

Explanation 12.8

The sine may oscillate, but \(|\sin(1/x)|\le1\). Multiplication by \(|x|\) gives \[\left|x\sin\frac1x\right|\le|x|, \qquad -|x|\le x\sin\frac1x\le|x|.\] Both outer functions tend to zero, so the squeeze theorem gives the limit. Equivalently, the direct choice \(\delta=\varepsilon\) works because \(0<|x|<\delta\) implies \(|x\sin(1/x)|\le|x|<\varepsilon\).

Example 12.9

Faster Oscillation, Smaller Amplitude

Evaluate \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to0}x^2\cos(1/x^2)\).

Explanation 12.9

Since \(|\cos(1/x^2)|\le1\), we have \(-x^2\le x^2\cos(1/x^2)\le x^2\). The outer limits are both zero. Thus the answer is \(0\). The increasing frequency causes no difficulty because the amplitude \(x^2\) tends to zero.

Example 12.10

A Bounded Numerator at Infinity

Evaluate \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to+\infty}\frac{\sin x}{x}\) and \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to-\infty}\frac{\sin x}{x}\).

Explanation 12.10

For every \(x\ne0\), \[\left|\frac{\sin x}{x}\right|\le\frac1{|x|}.\] As \(x\to+\infty\) or \(x\to-\infty\), the right side tends to zero. The squeeze theorem therefore gives \(0\) in both cases. Notice the difference between this expression and \(\sin x\): the sine alone keeps oscillating, but division by an input of large magnitude reduces the size of every oscillation.

Theorem 12.4

A Vanishing Factor Times a Bounded Factor

If \(u(x)\to0\) as \(x\to c\) and \(v\) is bounded in a deleted neighbourhood of \(c\), then \(u(x)v(x)\to0\).

Show proof

Proof. Choose \(K\ge0\) with \(|v(x)|\le K\) throughout a sufficiently small deleted neighbourhood. Then \(|u(x)v(x)|\le K|u(x)|\). The right side tends to zero, so squeezing proves the claim. Directly, choose \(\delta\) so that the bound holds and \(|u(x)|<\varepsilon/(K+1)\); then \(|u(x)v(x)|\le K\varepsilon/(K+1)<\varepsilon\). ◻

Example 12.11

A Function Defined Differently on Rational Inputs

Let \(f(x)=x^2\) when \(x\) is rational and \(f(x)=-x^2\) when \(x\) is irrational. Find \(\lim_{x\to0}f(x)\).

Explanation 12.11

We do not need to decide which kind of input is being used. Every real \(x\) satisfies \(-x^2\le f(x)\le x^2\). The two boundaries tend to zero, so \(f(x)\to0\). This is a useful example of one estimate controlling every sequence of inputs at once.

What a Vanishing Denominator Tells Us

Motivation 12.2

Suppose the numerator approaches a nonzero number \(k\), while the denominator approaches zero. Then the numerator’s magnitude stays above \(|k|/2\), but we are dividing by numbers of arbitrarily small magnitude. The magnitude of the quotient grows beyond every bound. The expression \(k/0\) is undefined; it only reminds us why no finite answer is possible in this case. If the quotient does have a finite limit, how small must the numerator become?

Theorem 12.5

A Necessary Condition for a Finite Quotient Limit

Let \(f,g:A\to\mathbb{R}\), and let \(c\) be a limit point of \(A\). Assume \(g(x)\ne0\) for all \(x\in A\) sufficiently close to \(c\) with \(x\ne c\). If \[\lim_{x\to c}\frac{f(x)}{g(x)}=L\in\mathbb{R} \qquad\text{and}\qquad \lim_{x\to c}g(x)=0,\] then \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to c}f(x)=0\).

Show proof

Proof. Since the quotient tends to the finite number \(L\), using tolerance \(1\) in its limit definition gives \(\delta_1>0\) such that, for \(x\in A\) with \(0<|x-c|<\delta_1\), the quotient is defined and \[\left|\frac{f(x)}{g(x)}-L\right|<1.\] Thus \(\left|f(x)/g(x)\right|<|L|+1\) there. Put \(M=|L|+1\). Given \(\varepsilon>0\), use \(g(x)\to0\) with tolerance \(\varepsilon/M\). This gives \(\delta_2>0\) such that \(|g(x)|<\varepsilon/M\) whenever \(x\in A\) and \(0<|x-c|<\delta_2\).

Now set \(\delta=\min\{\delta_1,\delta_2\}\). For any \(x\in A\) with \(0<|x-c|<\delta\), both estimates apply, so \[|f(x)-0|=\left|\frac{f(x)}{g(x)}\right|\,|g(x)| \le M|g(x)|<M\frac{\varepsilon}{M}=\varepsilon.\] This proves the claimed limit by the \(\varepsilon\)–\(\delta\) definition. ◻

Warning 12.4

The converse can fail. As \(x\to0\), both \(x^2\) and \(x\) tend to zero. For \(x\ne0\), the quotient \(x^2/x=x\) tends to zero, whereas \(x/x^2=1/x\) has no finite limit. The form \(0/0\) is a cue to compare how the numerator and denominator tend to zero.

Limits at Infinity: First Find the Dominant Size

At infinity the largest powers usually determine the scale. Dividing by that scale exposes terms which tend to zero. A radical needs particular care: \(\sqrt{x^2}=|x|\), so a calculation at \(-\infty\) can differ from the one at \(+\infty\).

Example 12.12

Polynomial and Radical Expressions at Infinity

Evaluate:

\(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to+\infty}\left(\frac{x^3}{3x^2-4}-\frac{x^2}{3x+2}\right)\).

\(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to+\infty}(\sqrt{9x^2+1}-3x)\).

\(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to+\infty}\frac{2\sqrt{x}+3\sqrt[3]{x}+5\sqrt[5]{x}}{\sqrt{3x-2}+\sqrt[3]{2x-3}}\).

\(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to-\infty}(\sqrt{2x^2-3}-5x)\).

\(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to+\infty}x(\sqrt{x^2+1}-x)\).

\(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to+\infty}\frac{\sqrt{2x^2+3}}{4x+2}\) and \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to-\infty}\frac{\sqrt{2x^2+3}}{4x+2}\).

\(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to+\infty}5^{2x/(x+3)}\).

Explanation 12.12

(a) Each fraction grows without bound, so we first combine them: \[\frac{x^3}{3x^2-4}-\frac{x^2}{3x+2} =\frac{x^3(3x+2)-x^2(3x^2-4)}{(3x^2-4)(3x+2)} =\frac{2x^3+4x^2}{9x^3+6x^2-12x-8}.\] Divide numerator and denominator by \(x^3\). The resulting limit is \[\frac{2+4/x}{9+6/x-12/x^2-8/x^3}\longrightarrow\frac29.\]

(b) Rationalising gives \[\sqrt{9x^2+1}-3x=\frac1{\sqrt{9x^2+1}+3x}.\] For \(x>0\), the denominator is at least \(3x\), so the expression lies between \(0\) and \(1/(3x)\). Its limit is \(0\).

(c) Divide by \(\sqrt{x}\), the largest power appearing. For positive large \(x\) the quotient becomes \[\frac{2+3x^{-1/6}+5x^{-3/10}} {\sqrt{3-2/x}+x^{-1/6}\sqrt[3]{2-3/x}}.\] The negative powers tend to zero, the square root tends to \(\sqrt3\), and the remaining cube root stays bounded. The answer is \(2/\sqrt3\).

(d) Both terms are positive for sufficiently negative \(x\). In particular \(\sqrt{2x^2-3}-5x\ge-5x\to+\infty\), so the limit is \(+\infty\).

(e) Here rationalisation leaves a finite quantity: \[x(\sqrt{x^2+1}-x)=\frac{x}{\sqrt{x^2+1}+x} =\frac1{\sqrt{1+1/x^2}+1}\longrightarrow\frac12.\] The last equality uses \(x>0\) on the tail.

(f) Since \(\sqrt{2x^2+3}=|x|\sqrt{2+3/x^2}\), the expression is \[\frac{|x|}{x}\,\frac{\sqrt{2+3/x^2}}{4+2/x}.\] As \(x\to+\infty\) its limit is \(\sqrt2/4\); as \(x\to-\infty\) its limit is \(-\sqrt2/4\). Keeping the absolute value until the direction is fixed prevents a sign error.

(g) The exponent satisfies \(2x/(x+3)=2-6/(x+3)\to2\). Thus \[5^{2x/(x+3)}=25\exp\left(-\frac{6\ln5}{x+3}\right)\longrightarrow25.\] Here \(e^t\to1\) as \(t\to0\) follows from the standard limit \((e^t-1)/t\to1\).

A Substitution Can Reveal a Finite Factor

Example 12.13

Roots and Shifted Trigonometric Expressions

Find:

\(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to1}\frac{2x-2}{\sqrt[3]{26+x}-3}\).

\(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to-1}\frac{x+1}{\sqrt[4]{x+17}-2}\).

\(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to-1}\frac{1+\sqrt[3]{x}}{1+\sqrt[5]{x}}\).

\(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to0}\frac{\sqrt[k]{1+x}-1}{x}\), \(k\in\mathbb{N}\).

\(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\pi/6}\frac{\sin(x-\pi/6)}{\sqrt3-2\cos x}\).

\(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\pi/2}\frac{\cos x}{\sqrt[3]{(1-\sin x)^2}}\).

\(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\pi/6}\frac{2\sin^2x+\sin x-1}{2\sin^2x-3\sin x+1}\).

Explanation 12.13

(a) Put \(u=\sqrt[3]{26+x}\), so \(u\to3\) and \(x-1=u^3-27\). For \(x\ne1\), \[\frac{2x-2}{\sqrt[3]{26+x}-3} =2\frac{u^3-27}{u-3}=2(u^2+3u+9)\longrightarrow54.\]

(b) Put \(u=\sqrt[4]{x+17}\), so \(u\to2\) and \(x+1=u^4-16\). The quotient equals \[\frac{u^4-16}{u-2}=u^3+2u^2+4u+8\longrightarrow32.\]

(c) To handle both odd roots together, put \(t=\sqrt[15]{x}\). Then \(t\to-1\), \(\sqrt[3]{x}=t^5\), and \(\sqrt[5]{x}=t^3\). Thus \[\frac{1+t^5}{1+t^3} =\frac{t^4-t^3+t^2-t+1}{t^2-t+1}\longrightarrow\frac53.\] We cancelled \(t+1\), which is nonzero for \(x\ne-1\).

(d) Let \(u=\sqrt[k]{1+x}\); then \(u\to1\) and \(x=u^k-1\). The finite factorisation gives \[\frac{u-1}{u^k-1} =\frac1{1+u+\cdots+u^{k-1}}\longrightarrow\frac1k.\] For \(k=1\), the quotient is identically \(1\), as the same formula says.

(e) Put \(t=x-\pi/6\). The angle-addition identity gives \[\sqrt3-2\cos(t+\pi/6)=\sqrt3(1-\cos t)+\sin t.\] After using the half-angle identities and cancelling \(2\sin(t/2)\), the quotient becomes \[\frac{\cos(t/2)}{\sqrt3\sin(t/2)+\cos(t/2)}\longrightarrow1.\] The cancellation is legitimate for sufficiently small nonzero \(t\).

(f) This example requires both sides. Set \(t=x-\pi/2\). Then \(\cos x=-\sin t\) and \(1-\sin x=1-\cos t\). For small nonzero \(t\), \[\frac{-\sin t}{(1-\cos t)^{2/3}} =-\frac{\sin t/t}{((1-\cos t)/t^2)^{2/3}} \frac{t}{|t|^{4/3}}.\] The first quotient tends to \(2^{2/3}>0\). The final factor tends to \(-\infty\) when \(t\to0^-\) and to \(+\infty\) when \(t\to0^+\). Therefore the original expression tends to \(+\infty\) from the left and to \(-\infty\) from the right. Its two-sided limit does not exist, even as a single signed infinite limit.

(g) Write \(s=\sin x\), so \(s\to1/2\). Factor: \[2s^2+s-1=(2s-1)(s+1),\qquad 2s^2-3s+1=(2s-1)(s-1).\] After cancellation the quotient is \((s+1)/(s-1)\), whose limit is \(-3\). Near \(\pi/6\), the cancelled factor vanishes only at the excluded point, so this simplification determines the limit.

Example 12.14

Trigonometric Identities before Standard Limits

Evaluate \[\text{(a) }\lim_{x\to0}\frac{1-\cos x}{x^2},\qquad \text{(b) }\lim_{x\to0}\frac{\tan x-\sin x}{x^3},\qquad \text{(c) }\lim_{x\to1}\frac{\cos(\pi x/2)}{1-x}.\]

Explanation 12.14

(a) Use \(1-\cos x=2\sin^2(x/2)\): \[\frac{1-\cos x}{x^2} =\frac12\left(\frac{\sin(x/2)}{x/2}\right)^2\longrightarrow\frac12.\] (b) The difference has an exact useful factorisation: \[\tan x-\sin x=\frac{\sin x(1-\cos x)}{\cos x}.\] Consequently \[\frac{\tan x-\sin x}{x^3} =\frac1{\cos x}\,\frac{\sin x}{x}\,\frac{1-\cos x}{x^2} \longrightarrow1\cdot1\cdot\frac12=\frac12.\] (c) Set \(t=1-x\). Then \(t\to0\) and \[\frac{\cos(\pi x/2)}{1-x} =\frac{\sin(\pi t/2)}{t} =\frac\pi2\frac{\sin(\pi t/2)}{\pi t/2}\longrightarrow\frac\pi2.\]

A Sequence Limit Can Define a New Function

Example 12.15

From Geometric Sequences to a Step-Shaped Graph

For each fixed real number \(x\), define \[F(x)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{x^{2n}-1}{x^{2n}+1}.\] Find \(F(x)\) and draw its graph.

Explanation 12.15

The limit here is taken in \(n\), with \(x\) held fixed. We must therefore distinguish three cases.

If \(|x|<1\), then \((x^2)^n\to0\), so \[F(x)=\frac{0-1}{0+1}=-1.\] If \(|x|>1\), divide numerator and denominator by \(x^{2n}\): \[\frac{x^{2n}-1}{x^{2n}+1} =\frac{1-x^{-2n}}{1+x^{-2n}}\longrightarrow1,\] because \(x^{-2n}\to0\). Finally, if \(x=1\) or \(x=-1\), then \(x^{2n}=1\) for every \(n\), so the quotient is exactly \(0\). Hence \[\boxed{F(x)=\begin{cases} 1,& |x|>1,\\ -1,& |x|<1,\\ 0,& |x|=1. \end{cases}}\] The filled points record the actual values \(F(-1)=F(1)=0\); the open circles are excluded endpoints of the horizontal pieces.

[Figure being adapted for the online edition.]

For instance, the function has left-hand limit \(-1\) and right-hand limit \(1\) at \(x=1\), while its value there is \(0\). The three pieces of information are different.

Mixed Algebraic and Trigonometric Practice

Example 12.16

Six Calculations: First Inspect the Form

Evaluate

\(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to0}\frac{\cos x+4\tan x}{2-x-2x^4}\);

\(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to2}\frac{2x^2+5x-7}{3x^2-x-2}\);

\(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to1}\frac{\sqrt{5-x}-2}{\sqrt{2-x}-1}\);

\(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to+\infty}\frac{2x^2-5x+4}{5x^2-2x-3}\);

\(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to+\infty}\bigl(\sqrt{x^2+1}-\sqrt{x^2-1}\bigr)\);

\(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to+\infty}\left(\frac{1-2x}{\sqrt[3]{1+8x^3}}+2^{-x^2}\right)\).

Explanation 12.16

As \(x\to0\), the numerator tends to \(1+4\cdot0=1\) and the denominator to \(2\). Since this denominator limit is nonzero, the quotient law gives \(\boxed{1/2}\).

Both polynomials have finite limits, and the denominator tends to \(12-2-2=8\ne0\). Direct use of the limit laws gives \[\frac{2\cdot4+5\cdot2-7}{3\cdot4-2-2} =\boxed{\frac{11}{8}}.\]

Direct substitution produces \(0/0\), so first rationalise each difference. For \(x\ne1\) sufficiently close to \(1\), \[\sqrt{5-x}-2=\frac{1-x}{\sqrt{5-x}+2},\qquad \sqrt{2-x}-1=\frac{1-x}{\sqrt{2-x}+1}.\] Cancelling the common factor \(1-x\) leaves \[\frac{\sqrt{2-x}+1}{\sqrt{5-x}+2} \longrightarrow\frac{2}{4}=\boxed{\frac12}.\]

Divide every term by \(x^2\): \[\frac{2-5/x+4/x^2}{5-2/x-3/x^2}\longrightarrow\boxed{\frac25}.\]

The expression is defined for all sufficiently large positive \(x\). Multiplication by the conjugate gives \[\sqrt{x^2+1}-\sqrt{x^2-1} =\frac{2}{\sqrt{x^2+1}+\sqrt{x^2-1}}.\] For \(x>1\), the denominator is at least \(x\), so the expression lies between \(0\) and \(2/x\). It therefore tends to \(\boxed0\).

For positive \(x\), take the factor \(x\) out of the real cube root: \[\frac{1-2x}{\sqrt[3]{1+8x^3}} =\frac{1/x-2}{\sqrt[3]{1/x^3+8}}\longrightarrow-1.\] Also \(2^{-x^2}\to0\) as \(x\to+\infty\). The sum consequently tends to \(\boxed{-1}\).

Example 12.17

Shift the Angle Before Applying a Standard Limit

Evaluate

\(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to0}\frac{\sqrt{x+4}-2}{\sin5x}\);

\(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to1}\frac{\sin(1-x)}{\sqrt{x}-1}\);

\(\displaystyle\lim_{\alpha\to\pi}\frac{\sin\alpha}{1-\alpha^2/\pi^2}\);

\(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\pi/4}\tan2x\,\tan(\pi/4-x)\);

\(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\pi/3}\frac{\tan^3x-3\tan x}{\cos(x+\pi/6)}\).

Explanation 12.17

Rationalising and inserting \(x\) gives \[\frac{\sqrt{x+4}-2}{\sin5x} =\frac{x}{\sin5x}\frac1{\sqrt{x+4}+2} \longrightarrow\frac15\cdot\frac14=\boxed{\frac1{20}}.\]

Put \(h=x-1\). Since \(\sqrt{x}-1=(x-1)/(\sqrt{x}+1)\), \[\frac{\sin(1-x)}{\sqrt{x}-1} =-\frac{\sin h}{h}(\sqrt{x}+1)\longrightarrow\boxed{-2}.\]

Put \(h=\alpha-\pi\). Then \(\sin\alpha=-\sin h\) and \[1-\frac{\alpha^2}{\pi^2} =-\frac{h(2\pi+h)}{\pi^2}.\] The quotient becomes \[\frac{\sin h}{h}\frac{\pi^2}{2\pi+h} \longrightarrow\boxed{\frac\pi2}.\]

Put \(h=x-\pi/4\). Then \[\tan2x=-\cot2h,\qquad \tan(\pi/4-x)=-\tan h.\] Their product is \[\cot2h\tan h =\frac{\cos2h}{\cos h}\frac{\sin h}{\sin2h} \longrightarrow1\cdot\frac12=\boxed{\frac12}.\]

The triple-angle identity yields \[\tan^3x-3\tan x=-\frac{\sin3x}{\cos^3x}.\] Set \(h=x-\pi/3\). Then \(\sin3x=-\sin3h\) and \(\cos(x+\pi/6)=-\sin h\). Consequently the quotient is \[-\frac{\sin3h}{\sin h}\frac1{\cos^3x} \longrightarrow-3\cdot\frac1{(1/2)^3}=\boxed{-24}.\] The apparent \(0/0\) form disappears after choosing an identity adapted to the angle where the limit is taken.

Inverse Trigonometric and Logarithmic Limits

Example 12.18

Three Final Changes of Variable

Evaluate

\(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to0}\frac{\arccos(1-x)}{\sqrt{x}}\);

\(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\pi/4}\frac{\ln(\tan x)}{1-\cot x}\);

\(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to0}\frac{\ln(1+a\sin x)}{\sin x}\), where \(a\in\mathbb{R}\) is fixed.

Explanation 12.18

The natural domain near zero has \(x>0\), so the displayed limit is approached from the right. Put \(t=\arccos(1-x)\). Then \(t\to0^+\) and \[x=1-\cos t=2\sin^2(t/2).\] For completeness, \(t\to0\) follows directly from the decrease of cosine on \([0,\pi]\): for any \(0<\eta<\pi\), taking \(0<x<1-\cos\eta\) forces \(t<\eta\). Since \(\sin(t/2)>0\), \[\frac{\arccos(1-x)}{\sqrt{x}} =\frac{t}{\sqrt2\sin(t/2)} =\sqrt2\frac{t/2}{\sin(t/2)}\longrightarrow\boxed{\sqrt2}.\]

Put \(u=\tan x-1\). Then \(u\to0\), and near \(\pi/4\) the tangent is positive. Also \[1-\cot x=1-\frac1{1+u}=\frac{u}{1+u}.\] Consequently \[\frac{\ln(\tan x)}{1-\cot x} =(1+u)\frac{\ln(1+u)}u\longrightarrow\boxed1.\]

If \(a=0\), the expression is zero wherever it is defined. If \(a\ne0\), put \(u=a\sin x\to0\). The logarithm is defined close to zero because \(1+a\sin x>0\) there, and \[\frac{\ln(1+a\sin x)}{\sin x} =a\frac{\ln(1+u)}u\longrightarrow a.\] Thus in every case the answer is \(\boxed a\).
