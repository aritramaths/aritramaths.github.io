The Intermediate Value Theorem and Its Consequences

Read this lecture online, then continue to the next chapter. Figures and exercises are being adapted separately for the web edition.

The intermediate value property: can a graph skip a height?

Definition 16.1

Intermediate value property (IVP)

A function \(f:I\to\mathbb{R}\) on an interval \(I\) has the intermediate value property if, for every \(a<b\) in \(I\) and every number \(k\) strictly between \(f(a)\) and \(f(b)\), there is a point \(c\) with \(a<c<b\) and \(f(c)=k\).

Motivation 16.1

A thermometer reads \(18^\circ\) at noon and \(24^\circ\) an hour later. Did the temperature reach \(20^\circ\) in between? The IVP says yes. It does not say when, or how often: the temperature could have crossed \(20^\circ\), fallen back, and risen again.

Theorem 16.1

Bolzano’s intermediate value theorem (IVT)

Every continuous real-valued function on an interval has the intermediate value property. In particular, if \(f\) is continuous on \([a,b]\), every height strictly between \(f(a)\) and \(f(b)\) occurs at some \(c\in(a,b)\). An endpoint height, of course, is already attained at its endpoint.

Does the converse hold? Continuity implies the IVP. But hitting every intermediate height does not mean the values settle down near each input. The next example shows how these two ideas can come apart.

Example 16.1

All intermediate heights, but no continuity at zero

Define \(S(0)=0\) and \(S(x)=\sin(1/x)\) for \(x\ne0\). Then \(S\) has the intermediate value property on \(\mathbb{R}\) even though it is discontinuous at \(0\).

Explanation 16.1

Why continuity fails. Near zero, can we force every output to stay close to \(S(0)=0\)? Consider the positive inputs \[u_n=\frac{2}{(4n+1)\pi},\qquad v_n=\frac{2}{(4n+3)\pi} \qquad(n=0,1,2,\ldots).\] Both sequences tend to \(0\), but \(S(u_n)=1\) and \(S(v_n)=-1\). This violates the sequential criterion for continuity. At the same time it explains why no height is missed.

[Figure being adapted for the online edition.]

The filled dots on the top line and open circles on the bottom line mark the first three peaks and troughs. The swings continue in the shaded strip; each full swing crosses the dashed line \(y=1/2\).

Why it still takes every intermediate value. Take any two inputs \(s<t\). If \(0\notin[s,t]\), use the intermediate value theorem on \([s,t]\), where \(S\) is continuous. If \(0\in[s,t]\) and \(t>0\), choose \(n\) so large that \(0<v_n<u_n<t\). On \([v_n,u_n]\), away from zero, \(S\) is continuous and goes from \(-1\) to \(1\). Therefore every height in \([-1,1]\) already occurs inside \([s,t]\). If \(t=0\), then \(s<0\); reflect the same argument to the left using \(S(-x)=-S(x)\). This also covers a pair with zero as one endpoint. In every case, each height strictly between \(S(s)\) and \(S(t)\) is attained at an interior input: neither endpoint has that height.

Two proofs of the intermediate value theorem

When applying the theorem, check its hypotheses and identify the value it guarantees. The two proofs below explain why that guarantee holds.

For the first proof, imagine drawing a line at the chosen height \(k\). Start below the line at \(a\) and finish above it at \(b\). Mark every input where the graph is below the line. How far to the right do the marks reach?

[Figure being adapted for the online edition.]

The bars mark the inputs with \(f(x)<k\). In this example they form two intervals; their rightmost edge is \(c=\sup S\).

Show proof

Proof 1: take the supremum. Suppose \(f(a)<k<f(b)\). Put \(S=\{x\in[a,b]:f(x)<k\}\). It is nonempty because \(a\in S\), and it is bounded above by \(b\). By completeness of the real numbers, its supremum \(c\) exists, with \(a\le c\le b\). We show that \(f(c)\) can be neither below nor above \(k\).

If \(f(c)<k\), then \(c<b\), since \(f(b)>k\). By continuity, sufficiently near \(c\) the values of \(f\) are still below \(k\): take the output tolerance \(\varepsilon=(k-f(c))/2\). In detail, if \(\delta\) is the radius supplied by continuity, then \(t=c+\frac12\min\{\delta,b-c\}\) belongs to \([a,b]\), lies to the right of \(c\), and satisfies \(f(t)<k\). Thus \(t\in S\). This contradicts the fact that \(c\) is an upper bound.

If \(f(c)>k\), take \(\varepsilon=(f(c)-k)/2\). Now continuity gives a \(\delta>0\) such that \(f(x)>k\) whenever \(x\in[a,b]\) and \(|x-c|<\delta\). But the approximation property of a supremum gives \(s\in S\) with \(c-\delta<s\le c\). At that point we would have both \(f(s)>k\) and \(f(s)<k\).

It follows that \(f(c)=k\). Neither endpoint has value \(k\), so \(a<c<b\). If \(f(a)>k>f(b)\), apply the result just proved to \(-f\) at height \(-k\). ◻

Show proof

Proof 2: bisect the interval and use Cantor’s theorem. Again suppose \(f(a)<k<f(b)\). Write \([a_0,b_0]=[a,b]\) and look at the midpoint \(m=(a_0+b_0)/2\). If \(f(m)=k\), we have found the point. If \(f(m)<k\), keep \([m,b_0]\); if \(f(m)>k\), keep \([a_0,m]\). In either case the retained half starts below \(k\) and ends above \(k\).

Apply the same rule to the retained half. If we ever hit \(k\), the proof is finished. Otherwise, after \(n\) steps we have nested nonempty closed intervals \([a_n,b_n]\) satisfying \[f(a_n)<k<f(b_n),\qquad b_n-a_n=\frac{b-a}{2^n}\longrightarrow0.\] By the Cantor intersection theorem from Lecture 6, exactly one point \(c\) belongs to all of them. Why do the two endpoints approach that point? Both \(c-a_n\) and \(b_n-c\) are nonnegative and at most \(b_n-a_n\), which tends to zero. Thus \(a_n\to c\) and \(b_n\to c\).

Now sequential continuity gives \(f(a_n)\to f(c)\) and \(f(b_n)\to f(c)\). Since all the left-hand values are below \(k\), their limit is at most \(k\). Since all the right-hand values are above \(k\), their limit is at least \(k\). These are the same limit, so \(f(c)=k\). Neither endpoint has that value; hence \(a<c<b\). The case \(f(a)>k>f(b)\) follows by using \(-f\) and \(-k\). ◻

A sign change guarantees a root

Corollary. If \(f\) is continuous on \([a,b]\) and \(f(a)f(b)<0\), there is a \(c\in(a,b)\) such that \(f(c)=0\). Indeed, zero lies strictly between the endpoint values. Apply the intermediate value theorem at height \(k=0\).

For example, \(P(x)=x^3-x-1\) is continuous, with \(P(1)=-1\) and \(P(2)=5\). Thus \(P\) has a zero in \((1,2)\), even though we have not calculated it. One sign change gives at least one root. For instance, \((x-1)(x-2)(x-3)\) has opposite signs at \(0\) and \(4\) and has three roots between them. Conversely, \(x^2\) has a root at zero without changing sign.

Using Intermediate Values: Roots and Equal Heights

Sometimes the equation we want to solve is already of the form \(F(x)=0\). Sometimes the first step is to invent the right \(F\). Here are examples of both uses of the intermediate value theorem.

Example 16.2

Why an odd-degree polynomial must cross the axis

Every polynomial of odd degree with real coefficients has at least one real root.

Explanation 16.2

Let \(P(x)=a_nx^n+\cdots+a_1x+a_0\), with \(n\) odd and \(a_n\ne0\). Dividing by \(a_n\) changes no root, so suppose \(a_n=1\). For \(x\ne0\), \[\frac{P(x)}{x^n}=1+\frac{a_{n-1}}{x}+\cdots+\frac{a_0}{x^n}.\] At both ends of the number line this ratio tends to \(1\). Choose \(R>0\) large enough that the ratio is positive at both \(R\) and \(-R\). Since \((-R)^n<0<R^n\), we have \(P(-R)<0<P(R)\). A polynomial is continuous on \([-R,R]\), so the intermediate value theorem supplies a real root between \(-R\) and \(R\). The theorem promises a root even when we have no formula for it. Even degree alone gives no such guarantee: \(x^2+1\) has no real root.

Example 16.3

A horizontal chord of width one half

Suppose \(f:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}\) is continuous and \(f(0)=f(1)\). Then there is \(c\in[0,\tfrac12]\) such that \(f(c)=f(c+\tfrac12)\).

Explanation 16.3

Put two fingers on the graph, initially at inputs \(0\) and \(1/2\). Slide them to the right, keeping their horizontal distance fixed at \(1/2\). The height of the right finger minus the height of the left is \[D(t)=f(t+\tfrac12)-f(t),\qquad 0\le t\le\tfrac12.\] This difference is continuous: as \(t\) approaches \(t_0\), the first term approaches \(f(t_0+1/2)\) and the second approaches \(f(t_0)\). The limit law for a difference gives \(D(t)\to D(t_0)\) within its domain. This is also an instance of the composition rule and the difference rule from Lecture 15. Now \(D(0)=f(1/2)-f(0)\), whereas \(D(1/2)=f(1)-f(1/2)=-D(0)\). If \(D(0)=0\), the initial positions already work. Otherwise, the two differences have opposite signs: the higher finger at the start is the lower finger at the end. The intermediate value theorem gives \(c\in(0,1/2)\) with \(D(c)=0\).

[Figure being adapted for the online edition.]

At a fixed instant, let \(f(t)\) be the temperature a fraction \(t\) of the way around the equator. A full turn returns us to the start, so \(f(0)=f(1)\). If temperature varies continuously with position, the chord result gives two diametrically opposite points with the same temperature.

Why did we ask for continuity in the horizontal-chord problem? Its proof applies the intermediate value theorem to the difference \(D\). Knowing only that \(f\) takes intermediate values does not justify the claim that \(D\) is continuous.

What Does a Continuous Function Do to an Interval?

To decide whether a set of numbers is an interval, take any two of its members. Does it also contain every number between them? This test works for open and closed intervals, half-open intervals, and unbounded intervals. A single point is also an interval.

Theorem 16.2

Continuous images of intervals are intervals

Let \(I\subseteq\mathbb{R}\) be a nonempty interval. If \(f:I\to\mathbb{R}\) is continuous, then its range \(f(I)=\{f(x):x\in I\}\) is an interval.

Show proof

Proof. Take \(u<v\) in \(f(I)\). Find inputs \(s,t\in I\) with \(f(s)=u\) and \(f(t)=v\). As \(I\) is an interval, the whole closed segment between \(s\) and \(t\) lies in \(I\), whichever input comes first. For every \(h\in(u,v)\) the intermediate value theorem puts \(h\) in the image of this segment, and therefore in \(f(I)\). This is exactly the test above. ◻

Theorem 16.3

The exact image of a closed, bounded interval

If \(f:[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}\) is continuous, with \(a\le b\), and \(m\) and \(M\) are its minimum and maximum values, respectively, then \(f([a,b])=[m,M]\).

Show proof

Proof. We use two closed-interval results from Lecture 15. The boundedness theorem gives finite bounds; the maximum–minimum theorem gives actual inputs taking the values \(m\) and \(M\). Every output lies in \([m,M]\), and the preceding theorem shows that every value between these two outputs also occurs. If \(m=M\), the image consists of that one value. ◻

Which endpoints? In our chord picture, \(f(t)=4t(1-t) =1-(2t-1)^2\) on \([0,1]\). Its endpoint outputs are both zero, but its range is \([0,1]\): the maximum \(1\) occurs at \(t=1/2\). Thus \([m,M]\) need not be the interval between \(f(a)\) and \(f(b)\).

A continuous image is always an interval when its domain is an interval, but its endpoints can change from open to closed or vice versa. For \(g(x)=1/(1+x^2)\), put \(y=g(x)\). For \(0<y\le1\), the equation \(g(x)=y\) is equivalent to \(x^2=1/y-1\). This tells us exactly which heights occur: \[\begin{array}{c|c|l} I & g(I) & \text{reason for the endpoints} \\ \hline {[-1,1]} & [1/2,1] & g(-1)=g(1)=1/2,\quad g(0)=1 \\ (-1,1) & (1/2,1] & 0\text{ is allowed; neither }-1\text{ nor }1\text{ is} \\ {[0,\infty)} & (0,1] & g(0)=1,\quad g(x)\to0\text{ but }g(x)>0 \end{array}\]

Fixed points: where does the graph meet the diagonal?

Definition 16.2

Fixed point

A fixed point of a function \(f:A\to A\) is an input \(p\in A\) such that \(f(p)=p\). The point \((p,f(p))\) on the graph then has equal coordinates: it lies on the diagonal \(y=x\).

[Figure being adapted for the online edition.]

PAUSE AND THINK

On the board, \(y=1-x\) starts above the diagonal and ends below it. What does their meeting point tell you? At that input the vertical gap \(f(x)-x\) is zero: the output equals the input.

Theorem 16.4

Brouwer’s fixed-point theorem on an interval

Let \(a\le b\) and let \(f:[a,b]\to[a,b]\) be continuous. There is at least one \(p\in[a,b]\) for which \(f(p)=p\).

This is the one-dimensional case of Brouwer’s fixed-point theorem.

Show proof

Proof. If \(a=b\), the only possible value is \(f(a)=a\). Otherwise set \(h(x)=f(x)-x\). To see that \(h\) is continuous, take any sequence \(x_n\to c\) in the interval. Since \(f(x_n)\to f(c)\), subtraction gives \(h(x_n)\to f(c)-c=h(c)\). This is the sequential criterion for continuity. Now use the fact that all outputs belong to the same interval: \[h(a)=f(a)-a\ge0,\qquad h(b)=f(b)-b\le0.\] If equality holds at either end, that endpoint is a fixed point. Otherwise \(h(a)>0>h(b)\), and the intermediate value theorem gives \(a<p<b\) with \(h(p)=0\). In other words, \(f(p)=p\). ◻

Existence alone says little about what happens when we keep applying \(f\). For \(f(x)=1-x\) on \([0,1]\), the only fixed point is \(1/2\), but starting from \(0\) gives \(0,1,0,1,\ldots\). What condition would make repeated application actually find a fixed point?

Contraction maps: one factor for every pair

Definition 16.3

Contraction map and contraction factor

Let \(A\) be a nonempty subset of \(\mathbb{R}\). A map \(T:A\to A\) is a contraction if there is one number \(q\), with \(0\le q<1\), such that \[|T(x)-T(y)|\le q\,|x-y|\qquad(x,y\in A).\] We call \(q\) a contraction factor. The same \(q\) must work for every pair. The notation \(T:A\to A\) also tells us that we can feed each output back into the rule.

Suppose two inputs are \(8\) units apart. If \(q=1/2\), their outputs are at most \(4\) units apart. Apply \(T\) again: the new gap is at most \(2\). Before asking what repeated application does, let us see what even one application tells us about continuity.

Theorem 16.5

Every contraction is continuous

If \(T:A\to A\) is a contraction, then \(T\) is continuous at every \(a\in A\).

Show proof

First proof: the \(\varepsilon\)–\(\delta\) definition. Fix \(a\in A\) and let \(\varepsilon>0\). We must give a radius \(\delta>0\) that works for all inputs in \(A\) near \(a\). Choose \(\delta=\varepsilon\). If \(x\in A\) and \(|x-a|<\delta\), the contraction inequality gives \[|T(x)-T(a)|\le q|x-a|\le |x-a|<\delta=\varepsilon .\] This is exactly the definition of continuity at \(a\). The choice works also when \(q=0\). ◻

Show proof

Second proof: the sequential criterion. Fix \(a\in A\). Take any sequence \((u_n)\) in \(A\) with \(u_n\to a\). We have \(|u_n-a|\to0\), so for every \(n\), \[0\le |T(u_n)-T(a)|\le q|u_n-a|.\] The right side tends to zero. Squeezing gives \(|T(u_n)-T(a)|\to0\), which says \(T(u_n)\to T(a)\). Since this holds for every such sequence, the sequential criterion gives continuity at \(a\). ◻

Remark. For continuity alone, the factor may even exceed \(1\). More generally, a bound \(|T(x)-T(y)|\le C|x-y|\) with any finite constant \(C\ge0\) is called a Lipschitz bound. Take \(\delta=\varepsilon/(C+1)\) in the first proof; in the second, \(C|u_n-a|\) still tends to zero. The strict inequality \(q<1\) will do a different job in the next theorem: it forces the successive steps to shrink geometrically. For comparison, \(T(x)=x+1\) on \(\mathbb{R}\) has \(C=1\) and is continuous, but it has no fixed point.

Theorem 16.6

Banach fixed-point theorem on the real line

Let \(A\subseteq\mathbb{R}\) be nonempty and closed, and let \(T:A\to A\) be a contraction. Then there is exactly one \(p\in A\) such that \(T(p)=p\). Starting at any \(x_0\in A\), define \(x_{n+1}=T(x_n)\) for \(n\ge0\). The resulting sequence converges to \(p\).

Motivation 16.2

We do not know \(p\) yet, so we cannot begin by estimating \(|x_n-p|\). We can compare \(x_n\) with \(x_m\): all the steps between them have already been defined. This is why the Cauchy condition is useful here.

Show proof

Proof. Existence: construct the point. Choose \(x_0\in A\). Because \(T(A)\subseteq A\), every iterate stays in \(A\). If \(q=0\), all outputs coincide: \(|T(x)-T(y)|\le0\). Call the common output \(p\). Then \(p\in A\), \(T(p)=p\), and \(x_1=x_2=\cdots=p\).

Suppose now \(0<q<1\). Put \(D=|x_1-x_0|\). For \(k\ge1\) the next gap satisfies \[|x_{k+1}-x_k|=|T(x_k)-T(x_{k-1})|\le q|x_k-x_{k-1}|.\] Thus the gaps are bounded by \(D,qD,q^2D,\ldots\); induction gives \(|x_{k+1}-x_k|\le q^kD\) for every \(k\ge0\). This is a contractive sequence, as in Lecture 7. For \(m>n\), add the gaps from \(x_n\) to \(x_m\) and use the triangle inequality: \[|x_m-x_n| \le \sum_{k=n}^{m-1}|x_{k+1}-x_k| \le Dq^n\frac{1-q^{m-n}}{1-q} \le \frac{Dq^n}{1-q}.\] The last bound is independent of \(m\). Given \(\varepsilon>0\), take \(N\) so large that \(Dq^N/(1-q)<\varepsilon\). Then any two terms with indices at least \(N\) are within \(\varepsilon\) of one another. This is the key step in every contractive sequence is Cauchy.

By the Cauchy criterion in \(\mathbb{R}\), \(x_n\to p\) for some real number \(p\). Now we use closedness: a convergent sequence in \(A\) has its limit in \(A\), so \(p\in A\) and \(T(p)\) is defined. The shifted sequence \(x_{n+1}\) still tends to \(p\). Meanwhile the continuity of \(T\) gives \(T(x_n)\to T(p)\). But \(x_{n+1}=T(x_n)\) for every \(n\). By uniqueness of a sequence limit, \(T(p)=p\).

Uniqueness: rule out a second point. Suppose \(r\in A\) also satisfies \(T(r)=r\). Because both \(p\) and \(r\) are fixed, \[|p-r|=|T(p)-T(r)|\le q|p-r|.\] Hence \((1-q)|p-r|\le0\). The first factor is positive and the second is nonnegative. Therefore \(|p-r|=0\), and \(r=p\). This covers \(q=0\) as well. Since the starting value was arbitrary, every starting value leads to this same fixed point. ◻

Why must the domain keep the limit? On \(A=(0,1)\) the rule \(T(x)=x/2\) has contraction factor \(1/2\). Its iterates tend to \(0\), but \(0\notin A\). There is no fixed point in the domain.

How close is the current iterate? Let \(m\to\infty\) in the tail estimate above. If \(0<q<1\), this yields \[|p-x_n|\le \frac{q^n}{1-q}|x_1-x_0|.\] There is also a bound using the last step we actually measured. Because \(|x_n-p|=|T(x_{n-1})-T(p)|\le q|x_{n-1}-p|\), the triangle inequality gives, for \(n\ge1\), \[|x_n-p|\le q\bigl(|x_{n-1}-x_n|+|x_n-p|\bigr), \qquad |x_n-p|\le\frac{q}{1-q}|x_n-x_{n-1}|.\] If we have computed the next step, then likewise \(|x_n-p|\le |x_{n+1}-x_n|+q|x_n-p|\), so \(|x_n-p|\le |x_{n+1}-x_n|/(1-q)\).

Reading a cobweb: a stable equilibrium

In discrete dynamics, \(x_n\) is the state at time \(n\) and \(T\) is the rule for one time step. Try \(T(x)=1+x/2\) on \([0,3]\). Its outputs stay in \([0,3]\), and \(|T(x)-T(y)|=|x-y|/2\). The fixed point is \(p=2\). Starting at \(x_0=0\) gives \(x_1=1\), \(x_2=3/2\), \(x_3=7/4\). Here is how to see those same numbers without putting them in a table.

[Figure being adapted for the online edition.]

Follow one round.

Start at \((x_n,x_n)\) on the dashed diagonal.

Vertical: keep the input \(x_n\) and meet the graph at height \(T(x_n)=x_{n+1}\).

Horizontal: keep that height and return to the diagonal at \((x_{n+1},x_{n+1})\). The old output is now the new input. Repeat.

An equilibrium is a state that stays where it is: \(T(p)=p\). What if we give it a small nudge? For any contraction with \(0<q<1\), \[|x_n-p|\le q^n|x_0-p|\qquad(n\ge0),\] because each application shrinks the distance to \(T(p)=p\) by a factor of at most \(q\). This proves two different things. The equilibrium is stable: if \(|x_0-p|<\varepsilon\), then every later state stays within \(\varepsilon\) of \(p\). It is also attracting: from any starting point in \(A\), the distance to \(p\) tends to zero. For \(q=0\) we arrive in one step. In our picture, starting even at \(3\) instead of \(0\) leads to the same equilibrium, this time approaching from above.

Kepler’s equation: finding an unknown angle

Example 16.4

Kepler’s equation for an elliptical orbit

In an elliptical orbit the eccentricity \(e\) satisfies \(0\le e<1\). For a given angle \(M\) (the mean anomaly), Kepler’s equation asks for the angle \(E\) (the eccentric anomaly) satisfying \[E-e\sin E=M .\] Here \(M\) tracks the passage of time at a constant rate, while \(E\) helps locate the body on its ellipse; see JPL’s guide to planetary positions for the orbital context. In our equation all angles are in radians. Show that for every real \(M\) there is exactly one real answer, and give a way to approximate it.

Explanation 16.4

The unknown angle occurs both on its own and inside a sine. Instead of trying to isolate it completely, move the sine to the other side: \(E=M+e\sin E\). That is a fixed-point equation. Set \[T(t)=M+e\sin t,\qquad A=[M-e,M+e].\] The bound \(|\sin t|\le1\) puts every output in \(A\), even if the starting input is outside \(A\). In particular \(T:A\to A\). To see the contraction, recall the sine estimate from Lecture 9: \[|\sin u-\sin v| =2\left|\cos\frac{u+v}{2}\,\sin\frac{u-v}{2}\right| \le |u-v|.\] Therefore \(|T(u)-T(v)|\le e|u-v|\); the eccentricity itself is a contraction factor. The contraction theorem gives exactly one fixed point in \(A\). No real solution can lie outside \(A\), because the equation forces \(|E-M|=|e\sin E|\le e\).

For an actual computation choose \(E_0=M\) and repeat \(E_{n+1}=M+e\sin E_n\). For \(e=1/2\) and \(M=1\), the first few values, rounded only for display, are \[\begin{array}{c|ccccc} n&0&1&2&3&4\\ \hline E_n&1&1.420735&1.494381&1.498541&1.498695 \end{array}\] How do we know that the digits are settling down around a true solution? The theorem proves convergence, and since \(|E_1-E_0|=\tfrac12\sin 1<\tfrac12\), its error estimate gives \(|E-E_n|<2^{-n}\). Ten iterations guarantee an error below \(0.001\) radian, without knowing the exact answer.

The orbit picture: what does the angle \(E\) measure? Draw the ellipse with centre \(O\), semiaxes \(a,b\), and right focus \(F\), where \(OF=ae\). Mark \(A\), the point of the orbit nearest \(F\). The dashed circle below has radius \(a\). Above the orbit point \(P\), mark \(Q\) on that circle. The angle \(E\) is \(\angle AOQ\): it is measured at the centre, not at the focus.

[Figure being adapted for the online edition.]

Why does a sine appear? Squeezing the circle vertically by \(b/a\) sends \(Q\) to \(P=(a\cos E,b\sin E)\) and turns the circular sector \(OAQ\) into an elliptical sector of area \((b/a)(a^2 E/2)=abE/2\). Subtract triangle \(OFP\), of area \((ae)(b\sin E)/2\): the shaded area swept out from the focus is \(\tfrac12 ab(E-e\sin E)\). Kepler’s equal-area law says that the fraction of the period elapsed since \(A\) equals the fraction of the total area \(\pi ab\) swept out. Multiplying this fraction by \(2\pi\) gives the mean angle \(M\), hence \(M=E-e\sin E\). The picture explains the equation; the contraction proof tells us how to solve it.
