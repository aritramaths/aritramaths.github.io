Limit Laws, Averages, and Nonconvergence

Bounded Sequences

We met the word bounded in the note below Homework 4.3. Let us begin by giving the full definition, including bounds from just one side.

Definition 5.1

Bounded Sequence

A sequence \((x_n)\) is bounded above if there is a real number \(M\) such that \(x_n\le M\) for every \(n\). It is bounded below if there is a real number \(m\) such that \(m\le x_n\) for every \(n\). It is bounded if there exists \(B>0\) such that \[\abs{x_n}\le B\qquad\text{for every }n\in\mathbb{N}.\]

Theorem 5.1

Convergent Sequences Are Bounded

Every convergent sequence of real numbers is bounded.

Show proof

Proof. Suppose \(x_n\to L\). Use the definition with \(\varepsilon=1\). There exists \(N\) such that \[\abs{x_n-L}<1\qquad(n\ge N).\] Hence \(\abs{x_n}\le\abs L+1\) for \(n\ge N\). Only the finitely many earlier terms remain. Set \[B=\max\{\abs{x_1},\ldots,\abs{x_{N-1}},\abs L+1\}.\] Then \(\abs{x_n}\le B\) for every \(n\). ◻

Counterexample 5.1

Boundedness alone does not imply convergence. The sequence \((-1)^n\) is bounded between \(-1\) and \(1\), but it oscillates forever.

This raises a central question: \[\boxed{\text{Boundedness}+\text{what additional condition}\Longrightarrow\text{convergence}?}\] Here convergence always means a finite real limit. Divergence includes both unbounded growth and bounded oscillation. We shall obtain several answers.

A Sequence Is Judged by Its Tail

Suppose the final examination is in December. You have probably seen a student who does very little work until August. Then something changes: the student begins studying seriously, continues working, and does very well in the examination. When the result comes, the parents and teachers are pleased. The unpromising beginning has not prevented a good outcome.

Keep that picture in mind when you look at a sequence. Its first few terms may behave very badly. One may be enormous, the next negative, and the next somewhere completely different. But suppose that eventually the terms begin to behave well: they enter every prescribed window around one number and stay there. Do we reject the limit because of what happened at the beginning? Of course not. The definition allows us to wait.

There is one detail in the comparison that matters. A sequence has no last examination day. We require good behaviour for every index after the chosen point, not just for one impressive term. For each \(\varepsilon>0\), we must reach a stage after which all terms stay within \(\varepsilon\) of \(L\). The waiting stage may change when the window changes.

This is why removing a finite beginning, or starting the list a fixed number of places later, cannot change its limit. We have changed where we start watching; we have not changed what happens arbitrarily far along the list.

Theorem 5.2

Deleting a Finite Beginning or Shifting the Index

Changing finitely many terms does not change convergence or its limit. For any fixed integer \(r\ge0\), \(x_n\to L\) if and only if \(x_{n+r}\to L\).

Show proof

Proof. If two sequences agree whenever \(n\ge K\), combine a waiting index \(N_0\) for one sequence with \(K\): every \(n\ge\max\{N_0,K\}\) gives the same term and the same error in both sequences. This proves the first statement in both directions.

If \(|x_n-L|<\varepsilon\) for \(n\ge N_0\), then \(n\ge N_0\) implies \(n+r\ge N_0\), so \(|x_{n+r}-L|<\varepsilon\). Conversely, if \(|x_{n+r}-L|<\varepsilon\) for \(n\ge N_1\), then for every \(m\ge N_1+r\) we may put \(n=m-r\ge N_1\) and obtain \(|x_m-L|<\varepsilon\). This proves the second statement directly from the definition. ◻

Algebra and Order of Limits

Theorem 5.3

Algebra of Sequence Limits

If \(x_n\to x\), \(y_n\to y\), and \(c\in\mathbb{R}\), then \[\begin{aligned} cx_n&\to cx,\\ x_n+y_n&\to x+y,\\ x_ny_n&\to xy. \end{aligned}\] If \(y\ne0\) and \(y_n\ne0\) eventually, then \(x_n/y_n\to x/y\).

Show proof

Proof. A constant sequence has its constant value as limit, since its error is zero. We now prove each rule from the definition.

Scalar multiples. If \(c=0\), there is no error. If \(c\ne0\), choose \(N\) so that \(|x_n-x|<\varepsilon/|c|\) for \(n\ge N\). Then \(|cx_n-cx|=|c|\,|x_n-x|<\varepsilon\).

Sums. Choose \(N_1,N_2\) giving \(|x_n-x|<\varepsilon/2\) and \(|y_n-y|<\varepsilon/2\), respectively. For \(n\ge\max\{N_1,N_2\}\), \[|(x_n+y_n)-(x+y)|\le|x_n-x|+|y_n-y|<\varepsilon.\] Combining this with the scalar rule for \(c=-1\) also proves the difference rule.

Products. By boundedness of a convergent sequence, choose \(M\ge1\) such that \(|x|\le M\) and \(|y_n|\le M\) for every \(n\). Choose \(N_1,N_2\) giving errors below \(\varepsilon/(2M)\). For \(n\ge\max\{N_1,N_2\}\), \[\begin{aligned} |x_ny_n-xy| &\le|x_n-x|\,|y_n|+|x|\,|y_n-y|\\ &\le M\bigl(|x_n-x|+|y_n-y|\bigr)<\varepsilon. \end{aligned}\]

Reciprocals and quotients. Suppose \(y\ne0\). Choose \(N_0\) so that \(|y_n-y|<|y|/2\) whenever \(n\ge N_0\). The reverse triangle inequality gives \(|y_n|\ge|y|-|y_n-y|>|y|/2>0\). For a given \(\varepsilon>0\), also choose \(N_1\) so that \(|y_n-y|<\varepsilon|y|^2/2\) for \(n\ge N_1\). Then \[\left|\frac1{y_n}-\frac1y\right| =\frac{|y_n-y|}{|y_n|\,|y|} \le\frac2{|y|^2}|y_n-y|<\varepsilon \qquad(n\ge\max\{N_0,N_1\}).\] Thus \(1/y_n\to1/y\). The product rule just proved now gives \(x_n/y_n=x_n(1/y_n)\to x/y\). Any undefined initial quotients may be assigned arbitrary values; the finite-beginning rule shows that these choices cannot change the limit. ◻

Remark 5.1

The convergence of \(x_n+y_n\) does not force the separate convergence of \(x_n\) and \(y_n\). For example, \(x_n=n\) and \(y_n=-n\) have constant sum \(0\).

Theorem 5.4

Order Preservation

If \(x_n\le y_n\) eventually, \(x_n\to x\), and \(y_n\to y\), then \(x\le y\).

Show proof

Proof. Suppose instead that \(x>y\), and put \(\varepsilon=(x-y)/3>0\). Choose \(N_0\) after which \(x_n\le y_n\), \(N_1\) after which \(|x_n-x|<\varepsilon\), and \(N_2\) after which \(|y_n-y|<\varepsilon\). For \(n\ge\max\{N_0,N_1,N_2\}\) we would have \[x_n>x-\varepsilon>y+\varepsilon>y_n,\] since \(x-\varepsilon-(y+\varepsilon)=(x-y)/3>0\). This contradicts \(x_n\le y_n\), and therefore \(x\le y\). ◻

Strict inequalities need not remain strict: \(1/n>0\) for every \(n\), but \(1/n\to0\).

Two useful estimates behind the limit rules

Proof strategy 5.1

If \(y_n\to y\ne0\), choose \(N_0\) so that \(|y_n-y|<|y|/2\) for \(n\ge N_0\). Then \(|y_n|\ge |y|-|y_n-y|>|y|/2\). Thus \[\left|\frac1{y_n}-\frac1y\right| =\frac{|y_n-y|}{|y_n||y|}\le\frac{2}{|y|^2}|y_n-y|\longrightarrow0.\] Multiplication by \(x_n\to x\) now proves the quotient rule for sequences.

For order preservation, suppose instead that \(x>y\). Eventually \(x_n>x-(x-y)/3\) and \(y_n<y+(x-y)/3\), so \(x_n>y_n\), contradicting the hypothesis. A strict inequality may become equality in the limit, but a weak inequality cannot reverse.

Why squeezing works

The outer sequences need not touch the middle one. Their role is to force all sufficiently late middle terms into every prescribed window around the same number. Here is the complete argument, including the three indices that must be controlled at once.

Theorem 5.5

Squeeze Theorem for Sequences

Suppose there exists \(N_0\in\mathbb{N}\) such that \[a_n\le b_n\le c_n\qquad(n\ge N_0).\] If \(a_n\to L\) and \(c_n\to L\), then \(b_n\to L\).

Show proof

Proof. Fix an arbitrary \(\varepsilon>0\). From \(a_n\to L\), choose \(N_1\) such that \(|a_n-L|<\varepsilon\) for every \(n\ge N_1\). This gives \(L-\varepsilon<a_n\). From \(c_n\to L\), choose \(N_2\) such that \(|c_n-L|<\varepsilon\) for every \(n\ge N_2\), so \(c_n<L+\varepsilon\). Set \[N=\max\{N_0,N_1,N_2\}.\] If \(n\ge N\), the ordering and both error estimates hold simultaneously: \[L-\varepsilon<a_n\le b_n\le c_n<L+\varepsilon.\] Subtracting \(L\) gives \(-\varepsilon<b_n-L<\varepsilon\), which is exactly \(|b_n-L|<\varepsilon\). Since this works for every \(\varepsilon>0\), the definition of convergence gives \(b_n\to L\). ◻

Remark 5.2

The maximum is essential: \(N_0\) makes the trapping inequalities valid, \(N_1\) controls the lower sequence, and \(N_2\) controls the upper sequence. No information about the finitely many terms before \(N\) is needed.

The middle sequence may rise and fall. It need not move in one direction: control comes from its two bounds.

Worked Calculations with Limit Laws

The laws are applied after expressions have been rewritten as combinations of convergent sequences. In particular, division by \(n^d\) reveals the scale of a rational expression, while cancellation must come first if its pieces diverge.

Example 5.1

Polynomial Ratios and Finite Sums

Determine the limits of the following five sequences:

  1. \(\displaystyle\frac{3n^2+5n+4}{n^2+2}\);

  2. \(\displaystyle\frac{5n^3+2n^2-3n+7}{4n^3-2n+11}\);

  3. \(\displaystyle\frac{4n^2-4n+3}{2n^3+3n+4}\);

  4. \(\displaystyle\frac{1^2+2^2+\cdots+n^2}{5n^3+n+1}\);

  5. \(\displaystyle\frac{1+2+\cdots+n}{n^2}\).

Show worked solution

  1. Divide by \(n^2\): the expression becomes \((3+5/n+4/n^2)/(1+2/n^2)\to3\). The limiting denominator is \(1\ne0\).

  2. Divide by \(n^3\): the expression becomes \((5+2/n-3/n^2+7/n^3)/(4-2/n^2+11/n^3)\to5/4\).

  3. Divide by \(n^3\): the numerator becomes \(4/n-4/n^2+3/n^3\to0\) and the denominator becomes \(2+3/n^2+4/n^3\to2\), so the limit is \(0\).

  4. The finite square-sum identity gives \(1^2+\cdots+n^2=n(n+1)(2n+1)/6\). Hence \[\frac{1^2+\cdots+n^2}{5n^3+n+1} =\frac{(1+1/n)(2+1/n)/6}{5+1/n^2+1/n^3} \longrightarrow\frac1{15}.\] The identity itself follows by induction: adding \((n+1)^2\) to \(n(n+1)(2n+1)/6\) produces \((n+1)(n+2)(2n+3)/6\).

  5. Since \(1+\cdots+n=n(n+1)/2\), division by \(n^2\) gives \((1+1/n)/2\to1/2\).

A finite sum with \(n\) terms is itself a sequence as \(n\) varies; these examples need no theory of infinite series.

Example 5.2

Powers and \(n\) th Roots

Evaluate

  1. \(\displaystyle\left(\frac{3n^2+n-2}{4n^2+2n+7}\right)^3\);

  2. \(\displaystyle\left(\frac{2n^3+2n^2+1}{4n^3+7n^2+3n+4}\right)^4\);

  3. \(\displaystyle\sqrt[n]{5n}\);

  4. \(\displaystyle\sqrt[n]{n^8}\);

  5. \(\displaystyle\sqrt[n]{n^5}\);

  6. \(\displaystyle\sqrt[n]{6n+3}\)

as \(n\to\infty\).

Show worked solution

The inner quotients in (a) and (b) tend to \(3/4\) and \(1/2\) after division by the highest denominator power. The product law applied a fixed number of times gives \((3/4)^3=27/64\) and \((1/2)^4=1/16\).

For (c), use \(\sqrt[n]{5n}=5^{1/n}n^{1/n}\to1\cdot1=1\). For (d) and (e), \(\sqrt[n]{n^8}=(n^{1/n})^8\to1\) and \(\sqrt[n]{n^5}=(n^{1/n})^5\to1\). For (f), since \(n\ge1\), \[n\le6n+3\le9n, \qquad n^{1/n}\le(6n+3)^{1/n}\le9^{1/n}n^{1/n}.\] Both outer terms tend to \(1\), so the squeeze theorem proved above gives the same limit for the middle term. The direct proof of \(n^{1/n}\to1\) in Lecture [lec:4] supplies the two bounding limits.

Example 5.3

Cancellation Before the Sum Law

Find \[\lim_{n\to\infty}\left(\frac{2n^3}{2n^2+3} +\frac{1-5n^2}{5n+1}\right).\]

Show worked solution

The first summand behaves like \(n\), and the second like \(-n\); neither has a finite real limit. The sum law, which assumes both limits exist, does not apply to them separately. Put the terms over a common denominator instead: \[\frac{2n^3(5n+1)+(1-5n^2)(2n^2+3)}{(2n^2+3)(5n+1)} =\frac{2n^3-13n^2+3}{10n^3+2n^2+15n+3}.\] Dividing numerator and denominator by \(n^3\) now gives a convergent quotient with nonzero limiting denominator, so the answer is \(1/5\). The highest degree terms cancel inside the one expression.

Example 5.4

Five Uses of Squeezing

Establish the following limits.

  1. \(\dfrac{\sin n}{n}\to0\).

  2. If \[y_n=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{n^2+k}{n^3+2n},\] then \(y_n\to1\).

  3. If \(0<x<1\), then \(x^n\to0\).

  4. If \(x>0\), then \(x^{1/n}\to1\).

  5. \(n^{1/n}\to1\).

Show worked solution

  1. Since \(-1\le\sin n\le1\), \[-\frac1n\le\frac{\sin n}{n}\le\frac1n.\] Both bounds tend to \(0\).

  2. Each summand satisfies \[\frac{n^2+1}{n^3+2n} \le \frac{n^2+k}{n^3+2n} \le \frac{n^2+n}{n^3+2n}.\] Adding \(n\) terms gives lower and upper bounds that both tend to \(1\).

  3. Write \(x=1/(1+a)\) with \(a>0\). Bernoulli’s inequality gives \((1+a)^n\ge1+na>na\), so \[0<x^n=\frac1{(1+a)^n}<\frac1{na}\to0.\]

  4. Suppose first that \(x>1\) and write \(x^{1/n}=1+d_n\) with \(d_n>0\). Bernoulli’s inequality gives \(x=(1+d_n)^n\ge1+nd_n\), so \[0<d_n\le\frac{x-1}{n}\to0.\] Therefore \(x^{1/n}=1+d_n\to1\). The case \(x=1\) is constant. The case \(0<x<1\) follows by reciprocals.

  5. For \(n\ge2\), write \(n^{1/n}=1+k_n\). By the quadratic estimate proved in Lecture [lec:4], \[n=(1+k_n)^n\ge\binom n2k_n^2,\] so \(0\le k_n^2\le2/(n-1)\). Given \(\varepsilon>0\), choose \(N>1+2/\varepsilon^2\). For all \(n\ge N\), \(0\le k_n<\varepsilon\); hence \(k_n\to0\) and \(n^{1/n}=1+k_n\to1\).

Example 5.5

Dominant Term in an \(n\) th Root

Let \(A_1,\ldots,A_k\ge0\). Compute \[\lim_{n\to\infty}(A_1^n+\cdots+A_k^n)^{1/n}.\]

Show worked solution

Let \(M=\max\{A_1,\ldots,A_k\}\). Then \[M^n\le A_1^n+\cdots+A_k^n\le kM^n.\] Taking \(n\)th roots, \[M\le(A_1^n+\cdots+A_k^n)^{1/n}\le k^{1/n}M.\] Since \(k^{1/n}\to1\), the squeeze theorem gives the limit \(M\).

Proof strategy 5.2

For \(u_n\ge0\) and \(u_n\to v>0\), rationalisation gives \[|\sqrt{u_n}-\sqrt v| =\frac{|u_n-v|}{\sqrt{u_n}+\sqrt v} \le\frac{|u_n-v|}{\sqrt v}.\] Given \(\varepsilon>0\), make \(|u_n-v|<\varepsilon\sqrt v\); this proves \(\sqrt{u_n}\to\sqrt v\) directly. If \(v=0\), use the implication \(0\le u_n<\varepsilon^2\Rightarrow\sqrt{u_n}<\varepsilon\).

For cube roots put \(a_n=\sqrt[3]{u_n}\) and \(b=\sqrt[3]v\). If \(b\ne0\), the identity \[a_n^2+a_nb+b^2=(a_n+b/2)^2+3b^2/4\ge3b^2/4>0\] holds for every \(n\), without assuming that \((a_n)\) converges. Therefore \[|a_n-b|=\frac{|u_n-v|}{a_n^2+a_nb+b^2} \le\frac4{3b^2}|u_n-v|.\] Make \(|u_n-v|<3b^2\varepsilon/4\) to obtain \(|a_n-b|<\varepsilon\). If \(b=0\), use \(|u_n|<\varepsilon^3\Rightarrow|a_n|<\varepsilon\). Thus cube roots preserve these limits as well. Repeated use of the proved product law handles any fixed positive integer power of these roots.

Rationalisation, Roots, and Finite Sums

Example 5.6

Rationalising differences of square roots

Evaluate the limits of \[\begin{aligned} \text{(a)}\quad&\sqrt{2n+3}-\sqrt{n-1},\\ \text{(b)}\quad&\sqrt{n^2+n+1}-\sqrt{n^2-n+1},\\ \text{(c)}\quad&n^2\bigl(n-\sqrt{n^2+1}\bigr). \end{aligned}\] Here a limit of \(+\infty\) or \(-\infty\) describes unbounded growth in a specified direction, rather than convergence to a real number.

Show worked solution

(a) Both roots grow like \(\sqrt n\), but their leading coefficients differ. Since \(\sqrt{2n+3}\geq\sqrt{2n}\) and \(\sqrt{n-1}\leq\sqrt n\), \[\sqrt{2n+3}-\sqrt{n-1}\geq(\sqrt2-1)\sqrt n\longrightarrow+\infty.\] For any prescribed height \(M>0\), the right side exceeds \(M\) when \(n>[M/(\sqrt2-1)]^2\), which makes the conclusion precise.

(b) Directly replacing both roots by \(n\) would produce the ambiguous difference \(n-n\). Multiply by the conjugate first: \[\begin{aligned} \sqrt{n^2+n+1}-\sqrt{n^2-n+1} &=\frac{(n^2+n+1)-(n^2-n+1)}{\sqrt{n^2+n+1}+\sqrt{n^2-n+1}}\\ &=\frac{2}{\sqrt{1+n^{-1}+n^{-2}}+\sqrt{1-n^{-1}+n^{-2}}} \longrightarrow\boxed{1}. \end{aligned}\] The subtraction in the numerator exposes the term \(2n\) that survives the cancellation.

(c) A small difference may become large after multiplication by \(n^2\). Here \[n^2(n-\sqrt{n^2+1}) =\frac{n^2\bigl(n^2-(n^2+1)\bigr)}{n+\sqrt{n^2+1}} =-\frac{n}{1+\sqrt{1+n^{-2}}}\longrightarrow\boxed{-\infty}.\] Indeed \(1+\sqrt{1+n^{-2}}\leq1+\sqrt2\) for \(n\geq1\), so the expression is at most \(-n/(1+\sqrt2)\); it eventually lies below every negative threshold.

Example 5.7

Cube Root Cancellation

Find the limits of \[\text{(a)}\quad\sqrt[3]{n^2-n^3}+n, \qquad \text{(b)}\quad\sqrt[3]{(n+1)^2}-\sqrt[3]{(n-1)^2}.\] In (a), the \(+n\) is outside the cube root.

Show worked solution

The relevant algebraic identity is \[(a+b)(a^2-ab+b^2)=a^3+b^3.\] (a) Put \(a=\sqrt[3]{n^2-n^3}\) and \(b=n\). Although \(a\) and \(b\) separately have magnitudes comparable with \(n\), their sum is much smaller. Since \(a^3+b^3=n^2\), \[a+b=\frac{n^2}{a^2-an+n^2} =\frac{1}{(a/n)^2-(a/n)+1}.\] Now \(a/n=\sqrt[3]{n^{-1}-1}\to-1\). The denominator tends to \(1+1+1=3\) and is always positive. Therefore the limit is \(\boxed{1/3}\).

(b) Put \(a=\sqrt[3]{(n+1)^2}\) and \(b=\sqrt[3]{(n-1)^2}\). Apply \(a^3-b^3=(a-b)(a^2+ab+b^2)\): \[a-b=\frac{(n+1)^2-(n-1)^2}{a^2+ab+b^2} =\frac{4n}{a^2+ab+b^2}.\] Writing \(u_n=a/n^{2/3}=(1+n^{-1})^{2/3}\) and \(v_n=b/n^{2/3}=(1-n^{-1})^{2/3}\) gives \[a-b=\frac{4n^{-1/3}}{u_n^2+u_nv_n+v_n^2}\longrightarrow\boxed{0},\] because \(u_n,v_n\to1\), whereas \(n^{-1/3}\to0\).

Example 5.8

Dominant powers, alternating pairs, and telescoping

Determine the limits of \[\begin{aligned} \text{(a)}\quad&\frac{\sqrt{n^2+1}+\sqrt n}{\sqrt[4]{n^3+n}-\sqrt n},\\[2pt] \text{(b)}\quad&\frac{1-2+3-4+\cdots+(2n-1)-2n}{\sqrt{n^2+1}+\sqrt{4n^2-1}},\\[2pt] \text{(c)}\quad&\frac1{1\cdot2}+\frac1{2\cdot3}+\cdots+\frac1{n(n+1)}. \end{aligned}\]

Show worked solution

(a) The numerator is at least \(n\). For \(n\geq1\), the denominator is positive, since \(n^3+n>n^2\), and \[0<\sqrt[4]{n^3+n}-\sqrt n \leq\sqrt[4]{n^3+n}\leq\sqrt[4]{2n^3}=2^{1/4}n^{3/4}.\] Thus the fraction is at least \(2^{-1/4}n^{1/4}\to+\infty\). Its limit is \(\boxed{+\infty}\). The lower bound is enough; the exact rate is unnecessary.

(b) Group the numerator into adjacent pairs: \[(1-2)+(3-4)+\cdots+((2n-1)-2n)=\underbrace{-1-1-\cdots-1}_{n\text{ terms}}=-n.\] The denominator equals \(n\bigl(\sqrt{1+n^{-2}}+\sqrt{4-n^{-2}}\bigr)\). Hence \[\frac{-n}{\sqrt{n^2+1}+\sqrt{4n^2-1}} =-\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+n^{-2}}+\sqrt{4-n^{-2}}} \longrightarrow\boxed{-\frac13}.\]

(c) Each summand is a difference of neighbouring reciprocals: \[\frac1{k(k+1)}=\frac1k-\frac1{k+1}.\] In the finite sum the interior terms cancel, leaving \[\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac1{k(k+1)}=1-\frac1{n+1}\longrightarrow\boxed{1}.\] This uses only a finite algebraic identity and the basic limit \(1/(n+1)\to0\).

Example 5.9

Normalise the roots before taking limits

Evaluate \[\text{(a)}\quad\frac{\sqrt n}{\sqrt{n+1}+\sqrt n}, \qquad \text{(b)}\quad\frac{\sqrt{n^2+4n}}{\sqrt[3]{n^3-3n^2}}, \qquad \text{(c)}\quad\sqrt[3]{1-n^3}+n.\] In (b), the quotient is defined for all \(n>3\); this suffices for its limit.

Show worked solution

(a) Divide the numerator and denominator by \(\sqrt n\): \[\frac{\sqrt n}{\sqrt{n+1}+\sqrt n} =\frac1{\sqrt{1+n^{-1}}+1}\longrightarrow\boxed{\frac12}.\]

(b) Factor \(n^2\) beneath the square root and \(n^3\) beneath the cube root: \[\frac{\sqrt{n^2+4n}}{\sqrt[3]{n^3-3n^2}} =\frac{n\sqrt{1+4/n}}{n\sqrt[3]{1-3/n}} =\frac{\sqrt{1+4/n}}{\sqrt[3]{1-3/n}} \longrightarrow\boxed{1}.\] The denominator tends to \(1\), so the quotient law applies for all sufficiently large \(n\).

(c) Set \(a=n\) and \(b=\sqrt[3]{1-n^3}\). Then \(a^3+b^3=1\). Rather than substituting \(b\approx-n\) into their sum, use the cube identity: \[n+\sqrt[3]{1-n^3}=\frac1{n^2-n\sqrt[3]{1-n^3}+(\sqrt[3]{1-n^3})^2} =\frac{n^{-2}}{1-r_n+r_n^2}, \quad r_n:=\sqrt[3]{n^{-3}-1}.\] As \(r_n\to-1\), the denominator in the last expression tends to \(3\), and \(n^{-2}\to0\). Thus the limit is \(\boxed{0}\). The identity also shows that the expression is positive for all \(n\).

Example 5.10

Bounded oscillations and two finite geometric sums

Find the limits of \[\begin{aligned} \text{(a)}\quad&\frac{\cos(n^3)}{2n}-\frac{3n}{6n+1},\\[2pt] \text{(b)}\quad&\frac{2n}{2n^2-1}\cos\!\left(\frac{n+1}{2n-1}\right) -\frac{n}{1-2n}\,\frac{n(-1)^n}{n^2+1},\\[2pt] \text{(c)}\quad&\frac{1+\frac12+\frac14+\cdots+\frac1{2^n}} {1+\frac13+\frac19+\cdots+\frac1{3^n}}. \end{aligned}\] The juxtaposed fractions in (b) are multiplied.

Show worked solution

(a) The sequence \(\cos(n^3)\) need not have a limit, but it is bounded by \(1\) in absolute value. Thus \[\left|\frac{\cos(n^3)}{2n}\right|\leq\frac1{2n}\longrightarrow0, \qquad \frac{3n}{6n+1}=\frac3{6+n^{-1}}\longrightarrow\frac12.\] The required limit is \(\boxed{-1/2}\).

(b) The cosine factor has absolute value at most \(1\), so the first term has magnitude at most \(2n/(2n^2-1)\to0\). For \(n\geq1\), \[\left|\frac{n}{1-2n}\,\frac{n(-1)^n}{n^2+1}\right| =\frac{n}{2n-1}\,\frac{n}{n^2+1} \leq\frac1n\longrightarrow0.\] Both terms therefore tend to \(0\), and the answer is \(\boxed{0}\).

(c) Let \(S_n(r)=1+r+\cdots+r^n\) for a fixed \(r\ne1\). Subtracting \(rS_n(r)\) from \(S_n(r)\) makes every interior term cancel: \[(1-r)S_n(r)=1-r^{n+1}.\] Consequently the given ratio is exactly \[\frac{S_n(1/2)}{S_n(1/3)} =\frac{2(1-2^{-(n+1)})}{\frac32(1-3^{-(n+1)})} \longrightarrow\boxed{\frac43}.\] Only the finite sums shown in the question are used; no theory of infinite series is needed.

The Limit of Averages

Suppose it is your seventeenth birthday, and you have invited your friends to a party. Most of them are about your age: some are sixteen, some seventeen, and some eighteen. A few family members are there too. Perhaps there are children aged four or five, and adults aged thirty, forty, or fifty. Now I ask you: roughly what is the average age in the room?

You would expect it to be somewhere near your age. Why? Because there are many friends around seventeen and only a few family members whose ages are much farther away. Those family members do affect the average, but their influence becomes smaller as the number of friends grows.

Let us make that last sentence visible in a calculation. Imagine, just for this calculation, that all \(m\) friends are seventeen and the five family members have ages \(4,5,30,40,50\). Then the average is \[\frac{17m+4+5+30+40+50}{m+5} =17+\frac{44}{m+5}.\] The family members remain in the room; we have not erased their ages. Yet their effect on the average tends to zero as more seventeen-year-old friends arrive. With friends aged sixteen to eighteen, their own average lies between sixteen and eighteen, and the fixed family group has a vanishing effect as the number of friends grows. This explains why “near seventeen” is the natural expectation.

Now return to a sequence \(a_n\to L\). For any small window around \(L\), all terms after some point lie inside that window. The earlier terms are our fixed group of family members: their values may be very far from \(L\), but there are only finitely many of them. The later terms are our growing group of friends, all close to \(L\). They may lie on either side of \(L\); what matters is their distance from it.

So if we average the first \(n\) terms, should that average also approach \(L\)? We expect it to. The difference from a single birthday party is that convergence gives this control in every window, however small. Let us turn that expectation into a proof.

Theorem 5.6

Cesàro’s Theorem for Sequences

If \(a_n\to L\) and \[b_n=\frac{a_1+\cdots+a_n}{n},\] then \(b_n\to L\).

Show proof

Proof. We want to make the error \(|b_n-L|\) smaller than a given \(\varepsilon>0\). Since \(L=(L+\cdots+L)/n\), with \(n\) copies of \(L\), \[b_n-L=\frac{(a_1-L)+\cdots+(a_n-L)}{n}.\] The triangle inequality therefore gives \[|b_n-L|\le\frac1n\sum_{k=1}^n|a_k-L|.\] Thus it is enough to control the average of the individual errors.

Because \(a_k\to L\), choose \(K\in\mathbb{N}\) such that \[|a_k-L|<\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\qquad(k\ge K).\] The terms before \(K\) need not satisfy this estimate. Instead, collect their errors into the single number \[C=|a_1-L|+\cdots+|a_{K-1}-L|.\] When \(K=1\), this is an empty sum and we set \(C=0\). Once \(\varepsilon\) and \(K\) have been chosen, \(C\) is fixed; it does not grow with \(n\).

For every \(n\ge K\), split the sum at that point: \[\begin{aligned} |b_n-L| &\le\frac1n\sum_{k=1}^{K-1}|a_k-L| +\frac1n\sum_{k=K}^n|a_k-L|\\ &\le\frac Cn+\frac{n-K+1}{n}\,\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\\ &\le\frac Cn+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}. \end{aligned}\] The first part is the fixed group’s contribution. The second part is small because every one of its individual errors is small, however many terms we include.

Choose an integer \(N\ge K\) with \(N>2C/\varepsilon\). For every \(n\ge N\), we have \(C/n<\varepsilon/2\) (also when \(C=0\)), so \[|b_n-L|\le\frac Cn+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}<\varepsilon.\] This is exactly the definition of \(b_n\to L\). ◻

Notice how the proof follows the party story. We did not need to make the early terms themselves small. We only needed their total error to be fixed, so that dividing it by a growing number makes its contribution small. For the tail, convergence supplies a uniform bound on every error.

The converse is false. The sum \(\sum_{k=1}^n(-1)^k\) is \(0\) when \(n\) is even and \(-1\) when \(n\) is odd. Its average has absolute value at most \(1/n\), so the averages tend to \(0\), although the terms \((-1)^n\) themselves do not converge. Averaging can smooth out oscillation.

A bounded sequence that does not settle

Example 5.11

Why \((\sin n)\) Does Not Converge

Prove that the sequence \((\sin n)\) is divergent.

Show worked solution

We use the elementary trigonometric identities \(\sin(s+t)=\sin s\cos t+\cos s\sin t\), \(\cos(s+t)=\cos s\cos t-\sin s\sin t\), and \(\sin^2t+\cos^2t=1\), with angles in radians. Since \(0<1<\pi\), we also have \(\sin1>0\). Assume \(\sin n\to a\). The index-shift rule gives \(\sin(n+1)\to a\). From \[\sin(n+1)=\sin n\cos1+\cos n\sin1,\] we obtain \[\cos n=\frac{\sin(n+1)-\sin n\cos1}{\sin1},\] so \((\cos n)\) would also converge; write \(\cos n\to b\). Passing to limits in the sine and cosine addition formulae gives \[\begin{aligned} a&=a\cos1+b\sin1,\\ b&=b\cos1-a\sin1. \end{aligned}\] Writing \(c=\cos1\) and \(s=\sin1\), the equations are \((1-c)a-sb=0\) and \(sa+(1-c)b=0\). Multiply the first equation by \(a\) and the second by \(b\), then add: \[(1-c)a^2-sab+sab+(1-c)b^2=0, \qquad (1-c)(a^2+b^2)=0.\] Since \(0<1<\pi\), we have \(c=\cos1<1\), so \(1-c>0\). Hence \(a^2+b^2=0\). Both squares are nonnegative, and therefore \(a=b=0\). But \[\sin^2n+\cos^2n=1\] for every \(n\), so the left side would tend to \(a^2+b^2=0\), a contradiction.
