Limits at Infinity, Sequences, and Arithmetic

Read this lecture online, then continue to the next chapter. Figures and exercises are being adapted separately for the web edition.

Limits at Infinity and Infinite Limits

There are two different changes we may make to the definition. The input can go arbitrarily far out on the real line, or the output can become arbitrarily large in magnitude. The symbols involved must tell us which change is intended.

Definition 10.1

A Finite Limit at Infinity

For a domain \(A\) unbounded above, \(\lim_{x\to+\infty}f(x)=L\in\mathbb{R}\) means \[\forall\varepsilon>0\quad\exists M>0\quad x\in A,\ x>M\Longrightarrow |f(x)-L|<\varepsilon.\] For a domain unbounded below, \(\lim_{x\to-\infty}f(x)=L\) means the same statement with \(x<-M\) in place of \(x>M\).

Definition 10.2

An Infinite Limit at a Finite Point

Let \(c\) be an accumulation point of \(A\). We write \(\lim_{x\to c}f(x)=+\infty\) if \[\forall B>0\quad\exists\delta>0\quad x\in A,\ 0<|x-c|<\delta\Longrightarrow f(x)>B.\] The assertion \(\lim_{x\to c}f(x)=-\infty\) replaces the final inequality by \(f(x)<-B\).

Definition 10.3

Infinite Outputs at Infinity

Assume that the domain \(A\) is unbounded above. Then \(\lim_{x\to+\infty}f(x)=+\infty\) means \[\forall B>0\quad\exists M>0\quad x\in A,\ x>M\Longrightarrow f(x)>B.\] For an input tending to \(-\infty\), assume instead that \(A\) is unbounded below. For the other combinations, replace \(x>M\) by \(x<-M\) when the input tends to \(-\infty\), and replace \(f(x)>B\) by \(f(x)<-B\) when the output tends to \(-\infty\).

Warning 10.1

The symbol \(+\infty\) is not a real number which a function eventually reaches. It abbreviates the statement that the outputs eventually exceed every prescribed positive bound. In a finite limit at infinity, \(\varepsilon\) controls output accuracy and \(M\) controls how far the input must travel. In an infinite limit, a large output target \(B\) replaces the small error \(\varepsilon\).

An Infinite Limit and Failure of a Limit Are Different Statements

Motivation 10.1

An important question came up in class: “If the answer is infinity, does that just mean that the limit does not exist?” We must separate two kinds of behaviour. An output can eventually exceed every prescribed height; that is a definite, predictable statement. Or the outputs can fail to settle towards any one finite value or either signed infinity. Those are different situations.

Remark 10.1

In the definition of a finite real limit, the proposed value belongs to \(\mathbb{R}\). Thus \(f(x)\to+\infty\) is not convergence to a real number. When we also allow the extended values \(+\infty\) and \(-\infty\), we can correctly say that the function has the infinite limit \(+\infty\). Some books reserve “the limit exists” for finite limits. In these notes we will specify finite limit, infinite limit, or no finite or signed infinite limit, so that the wording cannot hide the mathematical behaviour.

Function, as \(x\to0\) What the nearby outputs do Precise conclusion
\(1/x^2\) Exceed every positive target on both sides, once \(x\) is close enough to \(0\). Infinite limit \(+\infty\); no finite real limit.
\(\sin(1/x)\) Keep returning to different heights arbitrarily close to \(0\), while staying between \(-1\) and \(1\). No finite or signed infinite limit.
\(1/x\) Become large positive for positive inputs and large negative for negative inputs. No single finite or signed infinite limit at \(0\).

An infinite limit gives a guarantee for every height

Consider \(f(x)=1/x^2\) on \(\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}\). Given any height \(B>0\), choose \[\delta=\frac1{\sqrt B}.\] Then every input satisfying \(0<|x|<\delta\) has \(x^2<1/B\), and hence \(1/x^2>B\). This proves \[\lim_{x\to0}\frac1{x^2}=+\infty.\] Notice what we have proved: a larger output target can always be met by restricting the input further. We have not merely observed some large values. The same argument works on both sides of zero because squaring removes the sign. It cannot also have a finite limit \(L\): choosing \(B>|L|+1\) makes every sufficiently nearby output more than \(1\) away from \(L\).

Oscillation gives a different obstruction

For \(g(x)=\sin(1/x)\), the bound \(|g(x)|\le1\) immediately rules out both \(+\infty\) and \(-\infty\). It also has no finite limit. Here is a direct use of the negated definition from Lecture 9, without yet using any theorem about sequences.

Fix a proposed real value \(L\) and an arbitrary radius \(\delta>0\). Choose a positive integer \(n\) large enough that \(1/(2\pi n)<\delta\). The two admissible inputs \[u=\frac1{2\pi n},\qquad v=\frac1{2\pi n+\pi/2}\] both lie in \((0,\delta)\), but \(g(u)=0\) and \(g(v)=1\). At least one of the distances \(|0-L|\) and \(|1-L|\) is at least \(1/2\): otherwise the triangle inequality would give \[1=|1-0|\le|1-L|+|L-0|<1.\] Choose the corresponding input \(u\) or \(v\). Thus the fixed output tolerance \(\varepsilon_0=1/2\) defeats every radius, for every proposed finite \(L\). The function does not have a finite limit, and its boundedness rules out either infinite limit as well.

Finally, unboundedness by itself does not give a signed infinite limit. For \(h(x)=1/x\), arbitrarily small positive inputs produce positive outputs and arbitrarily small negative inputs produce negative outputs. The function therefore cannot eventually stay above every positive target, or below every negative target. Nor can it have a finite limit: for any proposed \(L\) and radius \(\delta>0\), choose \[0<x<\min\left\{\delta,\frac1{|L|+1}\right\}.\] Then \(|1/x-L|\ge1/x-|L|>1\), so the finite-limit condition fails with the fixed tolerance \(1\).

Warning 10.2

The assertion \(f(x)\to+\infty\) requires all sufficiently close admissible inputs to produce values larger than the prescribed target. A few large values are not enough. Likewise, “no finite limit” alone does not distinguish predictable growth to \(+\infty\) from oscillation or opposing signs. Always state the behaviour that has actually been proved.

What changes when the input tends to infinity?

Approaching a finite input \(a\) means entering an increasingly small neighbourhood of \(a\). Approaching \(+\infty\) means travelling beyond every fixed input threshold. We therefore replace the small input strip by a right-hand tail. The permitted output interval around a finite \(L\) is still \[V=(L-\varepsilon,L+\varepsilon).\] We now seek a large enough \(M\) such that \[\boxed{\quad f\bigl(A\cap(M,\infty)\bigr)\subseteq V.\quad}\] The neighbourhood picture has the same purpose—all the selected inputs must give outputs in the chosen band—but the selected input region is now an entire tail. We do not subtract a real number called infinity or write a distance \(|x-\infty|\).

For a concrete example, take \(f(x)=2+1/x\) for \(x>0\). The proposed limit is \(2\), and \[|f(x)-2|=\frac1x.\] For every \(\varepsilon>0\), choosing \(M=1/\varepsilon\) gives \[x>M\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\frac1x<\varepsilon.\] As before, one choice must work for all inputs in the selected region, not merely a few large sample values.

Example 10.1

A rational function at positive infinity

Prove directly that \[\lim_{x\to+\infty}\frac{5x+1}{3x+9}=\frac53.\]

Explanation 10.1

Subtract the proposed limit before estimating: \[\left|\frac{5x+1}{3x+9}-\frac53\right| =\left|\frac{3(5x+1)-5(3x+9)}{3(3x+9)}\right| =\frac{14}{|3x+9|}.\] For \(x>0\), this is less than \(14/(3x)\). Given \(\varepsilon>0\), choose \[M=\max\left\{1,\frac{14}{3\varepsilon}\right\}.\] Then every \(x>M\) gives output error less than \(\varepsilon\). This is exactly the definition of the stated limit.

\(\varepsilon=0.5\), \(M=28/3\)

\(\varepsilon=0.2\), \(M=70/3\)

\(\varepsilon=0.05\), \(M=280/3\)

Here the input is not confined to an interval around a finite centre: it is taken in the entire orange tail \(x>M\), continuing beyond the right edge of the drawing. The output must stay in the blue window around \(5/3\) throughout that tail. The choices \(M=14/(3\varepsilon)\) are safe because the error is less than \(14/(3x)\) for \(x>0\). A smaller output tolerance asks us to go farther out along the same input line; it does not ask us to substitute an “infinite number” for \(x\).

Example 10.2

Another rational limit, with an explicit threshold

Prove directly that \(\lim_{x\to+\infty}(2x-1)/(3x+2)=2/3\).

Explanation 10.2

For \(x>0\), \[\left|\frac{2x-1}{3x+2}-\frac23\right| =\frac7{3(3x+2)}<\frac7{9x}.\] Given \(\varepsilon>0\), choose \(M=\max\{1,7/(9\varepsilon)\}\). If \(x>M\), the displayed expression is smaller than \(\varepsilon\), proving the limit.

Example 10.3

An output that becomes arbitrarily large

Prove that \(\lim_{x\to1}1/(1-x)^2=+\infty\).

Explanation 10.3

Let a target \(B>0\) be given and choose \(\delta=1/\sqrt B\). If \(0<|x-1|<\delta\), then \((1-x)^2<1/B\), so \[\frac1{(1-x)^2}>B.\] Both signs of \(x-1\) give the same conclusion because the denominator is squared. We have met every output target, which proves the infinite limit.

\(B=1\), \(\delta=1\)

\(B=4\), \(\delta=1/2\)

\(B=9\), \(\delta=1/3\)

These pictures concern \(1/(1-x)^2\) at \(x=1\). The target is now to put the output above a prescribed height \(B\), rather than inside a small interval around a real number. Every point of the graph with \(0<|x-1|<\delta=1/\sqrt B\) lies above the blue dashed line. The arrows show that both branches continue above the top of the page; the graph has not stopped. There is no neighbourhood of the form \((\infty-\varepsilon,\infty+\varepsilon)\) in this argument.

Example 10.4

Logarithmic and exponential growth from their definitions

For \(a>1\), prove \[\lim_{x\to+\infty}\log_a x=+\infty, \qquad\lim_{x\to+\infty}a^x=+\infty.\]

Explanation 10.4

We use the familiar fact that \(a^x\) is strictly increasing for \(a>1\), with increasing inverse \(\log_a x\).

For the first assertion, fix \(B>0\) and choose \(M=a^B\). Whenever \(x>M\), applying the increasing logarithm gives \(\log_a x>B\).

For the second assertion, choose \(M=\max\{1,\log_a B\}\). If \(x>M\), strict increase gives \[a^x>a^{\log_a B}=B.\] These choices prove the two assertions directly from the large-target definition. No differentiation is involved.

Example 10.5

An inverse trigonometric limit

Prove directly that \(\lim_{x\to+\infty}\arctan x=\pi/2\).

Explanation 10.5

The inverse tangent takes values in \((-\pi/2,\pi/2)\) and is strictly increasing. Given \(\varepsilon>0\), set \[\eta=\min\left\{\frac{\varepsilon}{2},\frac\pi4\right\}, \qquad M=\cot\eta.\] Then \(0<\eta<\varepsilon\) and \(M=\tan(\pi/2-\eta)>0\). If \(x>M\), applying the increasing inverse tangent gives \[\frac\pi2-\eta<\arctan x<\frac\pi2.\] It follows that \(|\arctan x-\pi/2|<\eta<\varepsilon\). Thus the value \(\pi/2\) is approached even though the inverse tangent never equals it.

\(\varepsilon=0.5\), \(M\approx3.916\)

\(\varepsilon=0.2\), \(M\approx9.967\)

\(\varepsilon=0.1\), \(M\approx19.983\)

The axes stay fixed while the blue window about \(\pi/2\) narrows. Here the exact thresholds are \(M=\cot(\varepsilon/2)\); the displayed decimals are rounded for reading the pictures. Each orange tail continues beyond the page, and every output on that tail lies in its blue window, as the inequalities in the proof guarantee. The curve never reaches \(\pi/2\), but reaching the limiting value is not part of the definition.

Example 10.6

A bounded numerator over a large input

Prove directly that \(\lim_{x\to+\infty}\sin x/x=0\).

Explanation 10.6

For \(x>0\), \(|\sin x/x|\le1/x\). Given \(\varepsilon>0\), choose \(M=\max\{1,1/\varepsilon\}\). Every \(x>M\) then gives \(|\sin x/x|<\varepsilon\). This direct error estimate proves the assertion. The same reasoning with \(|x|\) proves a limit of zero as \(x\to-\infty\) as well.

Returning to a Finite Input Point

Motivation 10.2

Before we begin, let us return to the two windows from Lecture 9. Someone gives us an output window \(V=(L-\varepsilon,L+\varepsilon)\). We must find an input window \(U=(a-\delta,a+\delta)\) such that every admissible input in \(U\), apart from the centre \(a\), is sent into \(V\). The whole relevant piece of the graph must stay inside the output band.

\[f\bigl((U\setminus\{a\})\cap A\bigr)\subseteq V.\]

[Figure being adapted for the online edition.]

The arrow describes the image of the whole admissible input window; it does not assert that \(f(a)=L\).

Pause at three questions. Who chooses \(\varepsilon\) first? Must the same \(\delta\) work for every admissible \(x\)? Why is the centre \(a\) removed? The answers are: the output accuracy is prescribed first; yes, one radius must work for all those inputs; and the limit concerns nearby values, independently of the value at \(a\).

Why Bring Sequences Back?

Direct estimates can require several restrictions on \(\delta\), and finding a useful estimate is sometimes difficult. A table or an algebraic identity may suggest a candidate \(L\), but the definition asks us to verify that candidate for every requested accuracy. The definition itself is not a mechanical procedure for discovering \(L\).

We already know a great deal about sequences. If we feed a function a sequence of inputs approaching \(a\), we obtain a sequence of outputs. Can we use the sequence results to understand the function limit? The answer is yes, provided that we consider every admissible input sequence. This is the bridge we now prove.

The Sequential Criterion

Motivation 10.3

How can our new definition connect with the sequence limits we already understand? Choose any sequence of admissible inputs \(x_n\to c\), with \(x_n\ne c\). If every nearby input gives a nearby output, then this particular sequence must give \(f(x_n)\to L\). The more interesting question is the converse: if every possible input sequence behaves correctly, does that force the function limit? Yes. A failure of the definition would let us build one sequence recording that failure.

Theorem 10.1

Sequential Characterisation of a Function Limit

Let \(f:A\to\mathbb{R}\) and let \(c\) be an accumulation point of \(A\). Then \[\lim_{x\to c}f(x)=L\] if and only if, for every sequence \((x_n)\) in \(A\setminus\{c\}\) with \(x_n\to c\), one has \(f(x_n)\to L\). This is also called Heine’s criterion.

Show proof

Proof. First suppose the function limit is \(L\). Fix an admissible sequence \(x_n\to c\) and an error \(\varepsilon>0\). The function definition supplies \(\delta>0\). Since \(x_n\to c\), there exists \(N\) such that \(n\ge N\) implies \(|x_n-c|<\delta\). Every \(x_n\) differs from \(c\), so the definition gives \(|f(x_n)-L|<\varepsilon\) for \(n\ge N\). This proves \(f(x_n)\to L\).

For the converse, suppose every admissible input sequence gives output limit \(L\), but the function does not have limit \(L\). Negating the definition gives one fixed \(\varepsilon_0>0\) such that, for every \(\delta>0\), there is some \(x\in A\) with \[0<|x-c|<\delta,\qquad |f(x)-L|\ge\varepsilon_0.\] For each positive integer \(n\), choose such a point \(x_n\) using \(\delta=1/n\). Then \[0<|x_n-c|<\frac1n, \qquad |f(x_n)-L|\ge\varepsilon_0\quad\hbox{for every }n.\] The inputs converge to \(c\), but the outputs cannot converge to \(L\): they never enter the \(\varepsilon_0\)-neighbourhood of \(L\). This contradicts the assumption and proves the converse. ◻

Warning 10.3

For a proof of a function limit, the input sequence must be arbitrary. Checking \(c+1/n\), or even several cleverly chosen sequences, is not enough. For a disproof, two input sequences with different output limits are enough, since a function limit would have to work along both. The input sequences need not be monotone or have distinct terms; they must stay in the domain, avoid \(c\), and converge to \(c\).

Example 10.7

A rational function by arbitrary sequences

Prove with Heine’s criterion that \[\lim_{x\to2}\frac{3x+1}{5x+4}=\frac12.\]

Explanation 10.7

Let \((x_n)\) be any sequence in \(\mathbb{R}\setminus\{-4/5,2\}\) with \(x_n\to2\). The sequence limit laws already established give \[3x_n+1\to7,\qquad5x_n+4\to14.\] The denominator limit is nonzero. The quotient law for sequences therefore gives \[\frac{3x_n+1}{5x_n+4}\longrightarrow\frac7{14}=\frac12.\] Nothing in this argument depended on a special choice of the input sequence. Heine’s criterion now gives the stated function limit.

Example 10.8

Oscillation close to one

Show that \(\lim_{x\to1}\sin\bigl(1/(x-1)\bigr)\) does not exist.

Explanation 10.8

We want two approaches to \(1\) which make the sine take visibly different values. Choose \[x_n=1+\frac1{n\pi},\qquad y_n=1+\frac2{(4n+1)\pi}.\] Both sequences avoid \(1\) and tend to \(1\). But \[\sin\frac1{x_n-1}=\sin(n\pi)=0,\] \[\sin\frac1{y_n-1} =\sin\left(2n\pi+\frac\pi2\right)=1.\] The first output sequence has limit \(0\), the second limit \(1\). Hence the function has no limit at \(1\).

Example 10.9

The same oscillation near zero

Show that \(\lim_{x\to0}\sin(1/x)\) does not exist.

Explanation 10.9

Take \(x_n=1/(2n\pi+\pi/2)\) and \(y_n=1/(2n\pi+3\pi/2)\). They both approach zero through positive domain points, while the output values are constantly \(1\) and \(-1\). Thus even restricting attention to positive inputs does not produce a limit.

Example 10.10

Approaching zero with opposite signs

Show that \(\lim_{x\to0}2^{1/x}\) does not exist.

Explanation 10.10

Take \(x_n=-1/n\) and \(y_n=1/n\). Both approach zero, but \[2^{1/x_n}=2^{-n}\to0, \qquad 2^{1/y_n}=2^n\to+\infty.\] The sequence facts follow, for instance, from \(2^n\ge n+1\) and \(0<2^{-n}\le1/(n+1)\). No finite number can be the output limit along both approaches. Nor does the function tend to \(+\infty\) at zero, since its values along the negative inputs tend to zero. The positive outputs also rule out a limit of \(-\infty\).

The sequential criterion at infinity

The finite-limit criterion has the following precise counterpart: \[\lim_{x\to+\infty}f(x)=L \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad f(x_n)\to L\text{ for every domain sequence }x_n\to+\infty.\] For the forward direction, a sequence tending to \(+\infty\) eventually exceeds any threshold \(M\) from the definition. For the converse, if the definition fails, there is one \(\varepsilon_0>0\) such that for every \(M>0\) some domain point \(x>M\) has \(|f(x)-L|\ge\varepsilon_0\). Choose such a point \(x_n>n\) for every \(n\). Then \(x_n\to+\infty\), but the output sequence does not tend to \(L\), contradicting the sequential condition. The proof at \(-\infty\) uses \(x_n<-n\).

Example 10.11

A function that keeps oscillating far out

Prove that \(\lim_{x\to+\infty}\sin x\) does not exist.

Explanation 10.11

Choose \(x_n=n\pi\) and \(y_n=2n\pi+\pi/2\). Both input sequences tend to \(+\infty\), but \[\sin x_n=0\to0,\qquad \sin y_n=1\to1.\] The sequential criterion at infinity rules out a common finite limit. The bound \(|\sin x|\le1\) also rules out either infinite limit. Compare this with the direct proof in Lecture 9 that \(\sin x/x\to0\) as \(x\to+\infty\): division by the large input makes the oscillation small enough to give a limit.

Why the Arithmetic Rules Work

Motivation 10.4

We have already used the arithmetic rules for sequences. We now want the same rules for functions, but let us see the error estimates themselves. If two outputs are close to their proposed limits, how close are their sum, product, and quotient? The proofs below answer that question directly from the \(\varepsilon\)–\(\delta\) definition.

Throughout this section, \(f\) and \(g\) are defined on a common set \(A\subseteq\mathbb{R}\), \(c\) is an accumulation point of \(A\), and all statements “near \(c\)” concern points \(x\in A\) with \(x\ne c\).

Theorem 10.2

A Finite Limit Gives a Bound Nearby

If \(f(x)\to L\in\mathbb{R}\) as \(x\to c\), there is a number \(\delta_0>0\) such that \[0<|x-c|<\delta_0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad |f(x)|<|L|+1.\]

Show proof

Proof. In the definition of the limit choose the allowed output error to be \(1\). Then \(|f(x)-L|<1\) in a sufficiently small deleted neighbourhood. The triangle inequality gives \(|f(x)|\le |f(x)-L|+|L|<1+|L|\). The value \(f(c)\), if present, can be included by enlarging the bound. ◻

Theorem 10.3

A Nonzero Limit Keeps the Denominator Away from Zero

If \(g(x)\to M\ne0\), there is a number \(\delta_0>0\) such that \[0<|x-c|<\delta_0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad |g(x)|>\frac{|M|}{2}>0.\]

Show proof

Proof. Choose the output error \(|M|/2\). The reverse triangle inequality gives \[|g(x)|\ge |M|-|g(x)-M|>|M|-\frac{|M|}{2}=\frac{|M|}{2}.\] Thus the reciprocal and quotient are defined on a sufficiently small deleted neighbourhood. This is the reason for the condition \(M\ne0\) in the quotient law. ◻

Theorem 10.4

Arithmetic of Finite Function Limits

Suppose \(f(x)\to L\) and \(g(x)\to M\) as \(x\to c\). For every real constant \(k\), \[\begin{aligned} \lim_{x\to c}kf(x)&=kL, & \lim_{x\to c}(f(x)+g(x))&=L+M,\\ \lim_{x\to c}(f(x)-g(x))&=L-M, & \lim_{x\to c}f(x)g(x)&=LM. \end{aligned}\] If \(M\ne0\), then also \[\lim_{x\to c}\frac1{g(x)}=\frac1M, \qquad \lim_{x\to c}\frac{f(x)}{g(x)}=\frac LM.\]

Show proof

Proof. In every part, fix an arbitrary \(\varepsilon>0\). Each tolerance used below produces a positive radius by the definition of the corresponding limit. Taking the minimum of finitely many positive radii lets all the estimates hold at once.

The constant and identity limits were proved directly in Example [ex:constant-and-identity-limits]. We now prove the arithmetic rules.

Scalar multiples. If \(k=0\), there is no error to control. If \(k\ne0\), choose \(\delta\) so that \(|f(x)-L|<\varepsilon/|k|\). Then \[|kf(x)-kL|=|k|\,|f(x)-L|<\varepsilon.\]

Sum and difference. Choose radii \(\delta_f,\delta_g\) that make respectively \(|f(x)-L|<\varepsilon/2\) and \(|g(x)-M|<\varepsilon/2\). With \(\delta=\min\{\delta_f,\delta_g\}\), \[|(f(x)+g(x))-(L+M)| \le |f(x)-L|+|g(x)-M|<\varepsilon.\] Exactly the same estimate applies to the difference because \[|(f(x)-g(x))-(L-M)| \le |f(x)-L|+|g(x)-M|.\]

Product. The useful algebraic step is to add and subtract \(f(x)M\): \[f(x)g(x)-LM=f(x)(g(x)-M)+M(f(x)-L).\] Put \(B=|L|+1>0\). By the preceding boundedness theorem, some radius \(\delta_0\) makes \(|f(x)|<B\). Choose \(\delta_f,\delta_g\) so that \[|f(x)-L|<\frac{\varepsilon}{2(|M|+1)}, \qquad |g(x)-M|<\frac{\varepsilon}{2B},\] respectively. Set \(\delta=\min\{\delta_0,\delta_f,\delta_g\}\). For every admissible \(x\), \[\begin{aligned} |f(x)g(x)-LM| &\le |f(x)|\,|g(x)-M|+|M|\,|f(x)-L|\\ &<\frac{\varepsilon}{2} +\frac{|M|\varepsilon}{2(|M|+1)} <\varepsilon. \end{aligned}\] The temporary bound on \(f\) removes the difficulty caused by the factor \(f(x)\).

Reciprocal. Assume \(M\ne0\). Choose a radius that makes \[|g(x)-M|<\min\left\{\frac{|M|}{2},\frac{\varepsilon|M|^2}{2}\right\}.\] The first inequality ensures \(|g(x)|>|M|/2\). Consequently, \[\left|\frac1{g(x)}-\frac1M\right| =\frac{|g(x)-M|}{|g(x)|\,|M|} <\frac{2|g(x)-M|}{|M|^2}<\varepsilon.\]

Quotient. It also follows from the product and reciprocal laws, but a direct error estimate is instructive. Choose \(\delta_0\) giving \(|g(x)|>|M|/2\), and radii \(\delta_f,\delta_g\) giving \[|f(x)-L|<\frac{\varepsilon|M|}{4},\qquad |g(x)-M|<\frac{\varepsilon|M|^2}{4(|L|+1)}.\] With the minimum of these three radii, \[\begin{aligned} \left|\frac{f(x)}{g(x)}-\frac LM\right| &=\frac{|M(f(x)-L)-L(g(x)-M)|}{|M|\,|g(x)|}\\ &\le\frac{2}{|M|}|f(x)-L| +\frac{2|L|}{|M|^2}|g(x)-M|\\ &<\frac{\varepsilon}{2} +\frac{|L|\varepsilon}{2(|L|+1)}<\varepsilon. \end{aligned}\] This completes all the direct proofs. ◻

Remark 10.2

For an input tending to \(+\infty\), replace each small-radius condition by a large-threshold condition \(x>R\). Take the maximum of the finitely many thresholds. For \(x\to-\infty\), use \(x<-R\). The output estimates are unchanged, so all the arithmetic rules hold for finite limits at infinity as well.

Example 10.12

Substitution and Cancellation

Evaluate \[\lim_{x\to2}(3x^2-5x+1),\qquad \lim_{x\to1}\frac{x^2-1}{x-1}.\]

Explanation 10.12

Since \(x\to2\), repeated multiplication gives \(x^2\to4\). Scalar multiples and sums then give \(3\cdot4-5\cdot2+1=3\) for the first limit.

For the second expression, numerator and denominator both tend to \(0\). The quotient law therefore does not apply in its displayed form. For every \(x\ne1\), \[\frac{x^2-1}{x-1}=\frac{(x-1)(x+1)}{x-1}=x+1.\] A limit ignores the value at the centre, so the required limit equals \(\lim_{x\to1}(x+1)=2\).

Warning 10.4

The expression \(0/0\) is not a value and does not determine a limit. For example, as \(x\to0\), the quotients \(x/x\), \(x^2/x\), and \(x/x^2\) have respectively limit \(1\), limit \(0\), and no finite two-sided limit. The algebraic rule for quotients requires the denominator’s limit to be nonzero.
