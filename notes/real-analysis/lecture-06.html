Monotone Convergence and Subsequences

Read this lecture online, then continue to the next chapter. Figures and exercises are being adapted separately for the web edition.

Can We Prove Convergence Before Finding the Limit?

In many of our earlier examples, we first guessed where the sequence was going. We guessed a number \(L\), and then tried to make \(|x_n-L|\) small. The limit laws also helped us calculate a limit once we recognised the pieces. But suppose a sequence is given by a complicated recurrence. We can calculate its first few terms, yet we cannot see its exact destination. Must we know that destination before we can prove the sequence converges?

That is the question for this lecture. We shall learn how to establish the existence of a limit without first calculating it. In this lecture the terms move steadily in one direction and cannot escape beyond a fixed bound. Later we shall select subsequences to understand the behaviour of a sequence more closely. Lecture [lec:7] will introduce a second method: comparing late terms with one another without specifying a destination.

Let us start with the first situation. Imagine a sequence whose terms keep moving to the right on the number line. If they are also trapped below a fixed ceiling, where can they go? The terms do not have to reach the ceiling we first choose; that ceiling might be much too high. What matters is the least ceiling above all the terms. The completeness of the real line guarantees that this boundary is itself a real number.

Definition 6.1

Monotone Sequence

A sequence \((x_n)\) is increasing if \(x_n\le x_{n+1}\) for all \(n\), decreasing if \(x_n\ge x_{n+1}\) for all \(n\), and monotone if it is increasing or decreasing.

Here “increasing” allows equality; it is also called nondecreasing. Similarly, “decreasing” means nonincreasing. If every comparison is strict, we say strictly increasing or strictly decreasing. For example, \((1-1/n)\) is increasing and bounded above by \(1\), while \((1/n)\) is decreasing and bounded below by \(0\). The sequence \((n)\) is increasing but unbounded, so monotonicity alone cannot guarantee a finite limit.

The boundary that belongs to the real line

For \(S=(0,1)\), the numbers \(1\), \(2\), and \(100\) are all upper bounds. Only \(1\) is the least upper bound. It is not a maximum, because \(1\notin S\). For \([0,1]\), the supremum is still \(1\), and this time it is also a maximum.

Definition 6.2

Supremum and infimum

An upper bound of \(S\) is a number \(u\) such that \(s\le u\) for every \(s\in S\). The least such upper bound is \(\sup S\). A lower bound satisfies \(l\le s\) for every \(s\in S\), and the greatest lower bound is \(\inf S\). A maximum or minimum must actually belong to the set.

Theorem 6.1

Completeness axiom of \(\mathbb{R}\)

Every nonempty set of real numbers that is bounded above has a real supremum.

This is the fundamental property we accept about the real number system. It is an axiom here, not a consequence of a graph that appears unbroken. All the completeness results in the lectures will be deduced from it. The analogous statement for infima follows by applying it to \(-S=\{-s:s\in S\}\).

For example, the positive rational numbers whose squares are less than \(2\) have supremum \(\sqrt2\) in \(\mathbb{R}\), but no supremum in \(\mathbb{Q}\). Being able to find rational numbers arbitrarily near a boundary is different from having the boundary in the number system.

A brief review: measuring with a stick

We met the Archimedean property in Lecture [lec:1]. Let us recall its meaning through a picture. The ancient Greek formulation concerned geometric magnitudes, long before the modern construction of the real number system. The following stick experiment is a way to express that idea in our own language.1

Suppose I give you a stick. You do not know its length. Beside it I place a unit segment, whose length we call \(1\). Can you be sure that sufficiently many copies of this unit segment, laid end to end, will extend beyond the stick? Yes. We may not know in advance how many copies we need, but some finite number will be enough. If the stick has length \(b>0\), then \(n\) copies of the unit segment have total length \(n\), so the statement is \(n>b\).

Now turn the experiment around. Keep the unit segment fixed, and repeatedly lay copies of a very short stick end to end. If its length is \(a>0\), then \(n\) copies have length \(na\), and eventually \(na>1\). The stick may be very short, but it has positive length. More generally, repeated copies of any positive length \(a\) eventually exceed any prescribed length \(b>0\).

The drawings illustrate particular choices. The number of copies depends on the two lengths being compared.

Theorem 6.2

Archimedean property: review

For every \(a,b>0\), there exists \(n\in\mathbb{N}\) such that \(na>b\). In particular, for every real \(A\), some \(n\in\mathbb{N}\) satisfies \(n>A\); for every \(\varepsilon>0\), some \(n\in\mathbb{N}\) satisfies \(1/n<\varepsilon\).

Show proof

Proof. Recall the short completeness argument. If \(\mathbb{N}\) were bounded above, let \(s=\sup\mathbb{N}\). Since \(s-1<s\), the number \(s-1\) is not an upper bound. Thus some \(n\in\mathbb{N}\) satisfies \(n>s-1\), so \(n+1>s\), a contradiction. Hence natural numbers exceed every real bound. Apply this to \(b/a\) to get \(n>b/a\), and multiply by \(a>0\). Taking \(a=\varepsilon\) and \(b=1\) gives \(n\varepsilon>1\), equivalently \(1/n<\varepsilon\). ◻

Remark 6.1

In number theory and arithmetic geometry we also study non-Archimedean distances, such as the \(p\)-adic metric. Here is a small glimpse. Fix a prime \(p\). For a nonzero integer \(m\), write \(m=p^ku\), where \(p\) does not divide \(u\), and define \(|m|_p=p^{-k}\); put \(|0|_p=0\). Thus \(|m|_p\le1\) for every integer \(m\). For example, \(|125|_5=1/125\), although its usual size is \(125\). Adding \(1\) to itself any number of times never produces \(p\)-adic size greater than \(1\).

For rationals, extend the definition by \(|r/s|_p=|r|_p/|s|_p\) and set \(d_p(x,y)=|x-y|_p\). This metric satisfies the stronger triangle inequality \[d_p(x,z)\le\max\{d_p(x,y),d_p(y,z)\}.\] Such a metric is called an ultrametric. The difference lies in how size and distance are measured: we are not claiming that the usual order property of \(\mathbb{R}\) has failed. No \(p\)-adic theory is needed in this course.2

The Monotone Convergence Theorem

A real number \(M\) is the supremum of a nonempty set \(S\subseteq\mathbb{R}\) when

  1. \(s\le M\) for all \(s\in S\);

  2. for every \(\varepsilon>0\), there exists \(s\in S\) with \(M-\varepsilon<s\).

The second condition says that no smaller number is still an upper bound.

Theorem 6.3

Monotone Convergence Theorem

A monotone sequence of real numbers converges if and only if it is bounded. More precisely:

  1. a bounded increasing sequence converges to \(\sup\{x_n:n\in\mathbb{N}\}\);

  2. a bounded decreasing sequence converges to \(\inf\{x_n:n\in\mathbb{N}\}\).

Show proof

Proof. We prove the increasing case. Let \[M=\sup\{x_n:n\in\mathbb{N}\}.\] Given \(\varepsilon>0\), the number \(M-\varepsilon\) is not an upper bound. Hence there exists \(n_0\) such that \[M-\varepsilon<x_{n_0}\le M.\] Because the sequence is increasing, for every \(n\ge n_0\), \[M-\varepsilon<x_{n_0}\le x_n\le M<M+\varepsilon.\] Therefore \(\abs{x_n-M}<\varepsilon\), so \(x_n\to M\).

For the decreasing case, the set of values is nonempty and bounded below, so \[m=\inf\{x_n:n\in\mathbb{N}\}\] is a real number. Given \(\varepsilon>0\), \(m+\varepsilon\) is not a lower bound: if it were, \(m\) would not be the greatest lower bound. Hence some \(n_0\) satisfies \(m\le x_{n_0}<m+\varepsilon\). For every \(n\ge n_0\), decreasingness gives \[m\le x_n\le x_{n_0}<m+\varepsilon,\] so \(|x_n-m|<\varepsilon\). This proves \(x_n\to m\).

These arguments prove that a bounded monotone sequence converges. Conversely, every convergent sequence is bounded, as proved in Lecture [lec:5]; this proves the other implication. For an increasing sequence, a bound above is enough, since \(x_1\) already bounds it below. For a decreasing sequence, a bound below is enough, since \(x_1\) already bounds it above. ◻

Example 6.1

A Nested-Radical Sequence

Let \[x_1=\sqrt2, \qquad x_{n+1}=\sqrt{2+x_n}.\] Show that \((x_n)\) converges and find its limit.

Show worked solution

We first find a region that the terms cannot leave. The first term satisfies \(0<x_1=\sqrt2<2\). If \(0<x_n<2\), then \(2+x_n>0\) and \[0<x_{n+1}=\sqrt{2+x_n}<\sqrt4=2.\] Thus induction proves the bound for every \(n\). Since both \(x_{n+1}\) and \(x_n\) are positive, comparing their squares preserves the inequality. Therefore \[x_{n+1}\ge x_n \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad 2+x_n\ge x_n^2 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad (2-x_n)(x_n+1)\ge0,\] which is true for \(0<x_n\le2\). Thus the sequence is increasing and bounded above, so it converges. Write \(x_n\to\lambda\). By the index-shift rule, \(x_{n+1}\) has the same limit. Taking limits in \(x_{n+1}^2=2+x_n\) using the algebra of sequence limits gives \[\lambda^2=2+\lambda.\] Hence \(\lambda^2-\lambda-2=0\), so \(\lambda=2\) or \(\lambda=-1\). By order preservation, the positive terms give \(\lambda\ge0\), which excludes \(-1\). Hence \(\lambda=2\).

Example 6.2

An Affine Recurrence

Let \[x_1=8, \qquad x_{n+1}=\frac12x_n+2.\] Show that the sequence converges and find its limit.

Show worked solution

We look for a lower barrier that is preserved by the recurrence. The number \(4\) suggests itself because \(4=\tfrac12\cdot4+2\). The first term satisfies \(x_1=8\ge4\). If \(x_n\ge4\), then \[x_{n+1}=\frac12x_n+2\ge4,\] so induction gives \(x_n\ge4\) for all \(n\). Moreover, \[x_{n+1}-x_n=2-\frac12x_n\le0.\] Thus the sequence decreases and is bounded below by \(4\). It converges. Passing to the limit in the recurrence gives \[\lambda=\frac12\lambda+2,\] so \(\lambda=4\).

Recursive Sequences: Establish Convergence First

A recurrence may suggest a candidate limit by the equation \(L=F(L)\). That equation only says what the limit would have to satisfy if the sequence converged and the passage to the limit were justified. It does not itself prove convergence.

Example 6.3

Newton’s Square-Root Iteration

Let \(a>0\), \(x_1>0\), and \(x_{n+1}=\tfrac12(x_n+a/x_n)\). Prove that \(x_n\to\sqrt a\).

Show worked solution

The recurrence is well-defined for every \(n\): \(x_1>0\), and whenever \(x_n>0\), both \(x_n\) and \(a/x_n\) are positive, so \(x_{n+1}>0\). Put \(s=\sqrt a\). We want to know on which side of \(s\) the iterates lie. Since \(a=s^2\), \[x_{n+1}-s =\frac{x_n^2+s^2-2sx_n}{2x_n} =\frac{(x_n-s)^2}{2x_n}.\] The right side is nonnegative. Thus \(x_n\ge s\) for every \(n\ge2\), even when \(x_1<s\). For \(n\ge2\), \[x_{n+1}-x_n=\frac{a-x_n^2}{2x_n}\le0.\] The sequence \((x_{n+1})_{n\ge1}\) is decreasing and bounded below by \(s\), so the monotone convergence theorem gives its limit. The index-shift rule gives the same limit \(L\) for \((x_n)\), and order preservation gives \(L\ge s>0\). Taking limits in the recurrence is now legitimate: \(L=(L+a/L)/2\), so \(L^2=a\) and \(L=s\).

The same identity describes the speed of convergence. If \(e_n=x_n-s\) for \(n\ge2\), then \(0\le e_{n+1}\le e_n^2/(2s)\). Once the error is small, the next error is bounded by a constant times its square. For \(a=2\) and \(x_1=1\), the first terms are \(1,3/2,17/12,577/408\). These are rational approximations to an irrational limit.

Subsequences and Divergence

An ordered list, and a selection that keeps its order

Before we select a subsequence, let us be precise about what we are preserving. In an ordered list, each entry has a place. We can ask which of two distinct positions comes first, even when the entries at those positions have equal values.

A strict total order is a relation expressing “comes before” with three properties: no object comes before itself; if one object comes before a second and the second before a third, the first comes before the third; and, for any two distinct objects, exactly one comes before the other. An ordered subset keeps the order inherited from the original collection. We select objects, but we do not rearrange the selected objects.

For the list \(x_1,x_2,x_3,\ldots\), let us use the classroom notation \[x_1\succ_{\mathrm{pos}}x_2\succ_{\mathrm{pos}}x_3 \succ_{\mathrm{pos}}\cdots\] to say that the first occurrence comes before the second, the second before the third, and so on. The subscript “pos” reminds us that this describes position in the list. It does not say that \(x_1>x_2>x_3\), and it is not the numerical notion of majorization. Thus a sequence may have this order of positions without being monotone.

There is a small point here that matters. A sequence is an indexed family, or an ordered list, not merely the set of its values. For example, \[1,0,1,0,1,0,\ldots\] has infinitely many entries, although its set of values is only \(\{0,1\}\). The first \(1\) and the third entry \(1\) have the same value but occupy different positions. Formally, we may keep track of each occurrence by the pair \((n,x_n)\) and define \[(i,x_i)\succ_{\mathrm{pos}}(j,x_j) \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad i<j.\] These tagged occurrences, rather than the bare numerical values, form the strictly ordered collection. The shorter notation above refers to these occurrences.

Now select infinitely many positions, keeping their inherited order: \(n_1<n_2<n_3<\cdots\). Reading the entries at those positions gives a new sequence. This is the precise meaning of calling a subsequence an “ordered subset” of the original list: it is an infinite selection of occurrences, read in their original order. In the alternating example, selecting positions \(1,3,5,\ldots\) gives \(1,1,1,\ldots\); selecting positions \(2,4,6,\ldots\) gives \(0,0,0,\ldots\).

Definition 6.3

Subsequence

Let \(n_1<n_2<n_3<\cdots\) be positive integers. Then \[x_{n_1},x_{n_2},x_{n_3},\ldots\] is a subsequence of \((x_n)\) and is denoted by \((x_{n_k})\).

For \((1/n)\), choosing \(n_k=k^2\) gives the subsequence \[1,\frac14,\frac19,\frac1{16},\ldots.\] The selected indices must be strictly increasing: we cannot return to an earlier position or select the same position twice. Equal values are allowed if they occur at different positions. For example, every subsequence of the constant sequence \(1,1,1,\ldots\) still has repeated values.

We always select infinitely many positions. We may omit finitely many or infinitely many of the original terms: choosing \(n_k=2k\) omits all odd positions, and choosing \(n_k=k^2\) omits all nonsquare positions. What we preserve is the order of the selected positions.

Intuition 6.1

Suppose there is a train going to Delhi. It has many coaches, and you decide that you will pay attention only to some of them: perhaps the second coach, then the fourth, then the sixth. Where are those coaches going? They are going to Delhi as well. You have changed which coaches you are watching, but the journey of the train has not changed.

Now choose the coaches in some other way. Ignore several in between, then look at another one farther along. However you choose them, as long as you are looking at coaches on that same journey, they still have the same destination. We are only selecting which coaches to observe; we are not physically detaching them and sending them somewhere else.

This is the picture behind a subsequence. The whole sequence has a destination \(L\). We keep some terms, preserve their order, and continue selecting terms farther and farther along. Once the original sequence stays inside a small window around \(L\), every selected term from that part of the sequence stays inside the same window. So the selected sequence must go to \(L\) as well.

The teal coaches are the entries we watch; all coaches remain on the same train. Their labels record position in the list, not numerical size or distance from \(L\). The arrows in the lower row record the order in which we read the selected entries.

Theorem 6.4

Subsequences Preserve Limits

If \(x_n\to L\), then every subsequence \((x_{n_k})\) also converges to \(L\).

Show proof

Proof. First observe why \(n_k\ge k\) for every \(k\). The positive integer \(n_1\) is at least \(1\). If \(n_k\ge k\), then strict increase of integer indices gives \(n_{k+1}\ge n_k+1\ge k+1\). Induction proves the claim. Thus the selected indices must eventually pass any fixed original index.

Let \(\varepsilon>0\). Since \(x_n\to L\), there is \(N\in\mathbb{N}\) such that \[n\ge N\quad\Longrightarrow\quad |x_n-L|<\varepsilon.\] For the subsequence, choose \(K=N\). If \(k\ge K\), then \(n_k\ge k\ge N\), and hence \[|x_{n_k}-L|<\varepsilon.\] This is precisely the definition of \(x_{n_k}\to L\). Notice the two roles: \(k\) is the position in the selected sequence, while \(n_k\) is its position in the original sequence. ◻

Method 6.1

A sequence diverges if

  1. it is unbounded; or

  2. it has two convergent subsequences with different limits.

For instance, \((-1)^n\) has the even subsequence constantly equal to \(1\) and the odd subsequence constantly equal to \(-1\). The sequence \[1,\frac12,3,\frac14,5,\frac16,\ldots\] is unbounded and therefore divergent.

Nested intervals: closing in on a single point

Imagine repeatedly keeping a closed subinterval of the previous interval. The left endpoints can only move right and the right endpoints can only move left. If the lengths shrink to zero, the two boundaries must identify the same real number.

Theorem 6.5

Nested Intervals: Cantor Intersection Property

Suppose \(I_n=[a_n,b_n]\) are nonempty closed bounded intervals, \(I_{n+1}\subseteq I_n\), and \(b_n-a_n\to0\). Then there is exactly one real number \(L\) belonging to every \(I_n\).

Show proof

Proof. The set of left endpoints is nonempty and bounded above by \(b_1\). Put \(L=\sup\{a_n:n\ge1\}\). For a fixed \(n\), every left endpoint is at most \(b_n\): if \(k\ge n\), nesting gives \(a_k\le b_n\), and if \(k<n\), then \(a_k\le a_n\le b_n\). Hence \(a_n\le L\le b_n\). This proves existence. If \(L\) and \(M\) are both in every interval, then \(|L-M|\le b_n-a_n\) for all \(n\). Letting \(n\to\infty\) gives \(L=M\). ◻

Warning 6.1

Closedness matters: the nested intervals \((0,1/n)\) have no common point. Shrinking length gives uniqueness: the constant sequence of intervals \([0,1]\) has many common points. The theorem does not say every bounded sequence converges.

Cantor’s intersection property: finding a number by narrowing its home

Motivation 6.1

Suppose I tell you where a number lies, but I do not give its exact value. First I say that it lies between \(1.4\) and \(1.5\). Then I improve the information: between \(1.41\) and \(1.42\), then between \(1.414\) and \(1.415\), and then between \(1.4142\) and \(1.4143\). The room in which the number can hide becomes smaller and smaller.

Each of these closed intervals sits inside the previous one. The nested interval property, also called the Cantor intersection property, tells us that they have a common point in \(\mathbb{R}\). When their lengths tend to zero, there is exactly one common point. In the general form, a decreasing sequence of nonempty compact sets has a nonempty intersection; shrinking diameter gives uniqueness. Here we use its interval form.

All the displayed endpoints are rational. Yet intervals chosen this way, and continued by the decimal brackets for the positive square root of \(2\), isolate an irrational point. This is a useful way to picture the gap in \(\mathbb{Q}\): the rational approximations improve, but their intended destination need not be a rational number.

This leads to Cantor’s construction of the real numbers. His 1872 construction used rational Cauchy sequences: reliable rational approximation processes, with two processes identified when their difference tends to zero.3 The shrinking-interval picture helps us understand the same completion idea. After defining Cauchy sequences in Lecture [lec:7], we shall make this connection precise in Cantor’s construction from rational approximations.

Warning 6.2

The intersection theorem just proved assumes that we are already working in \(\mathbb{R}\). A construction of \(\mathbb{R}\) must begin with rational arithmetic and define the missing numbers themselves. It cannot assume a real limit already exists. Also, nonempty intersection and a single common point are different conclusions: the intervals \([0,1]\) repeated forever have many common points.

The Bolzano–Weierstrass Theorem

Theorem 6.6

Bolzano–Weierstrass

Every bounded sequence of real numbers has a convergent subsequence.

Show proof

Proof. Suppose \(m\le x_n\le M\) for every \(n\). If \(m=M\), the sequence is constant, and the conclusion is immediate. Suppose \(m<M\). Throughout this proof, “infinitely many terms in an interval” means infinitely many indices \(n\) for which \(x_n\) belongs to the interval; the corresponding values may repeat. Start with \(I_0=[m,M]\). Divide it into two closed halves. At least one half contains infinitely many terms of the sequence; call that half \(I_1\) and choose one term \(x_{n_1}\in I_1\). Indeed, if both halves contained only finitely many indices, their union would contain only finitely many, whereas every sequence term belongs to \(I_0\). The shared midpoint does not affect this argument.

Bisect \(I_1\). Choose a half \(I_2\) containing infinitely many sequence terms and then choose \(n_2>n_1\) with \(x_{n_2}\in I_2\). Such a choice is possible because only finitely many indices are at most \(n_1\), while infinitely many indices have terms in \(I_2\). At each stage the same reasoning permits a new, larger index. Continue. We obtain \[I_1\supseteq I_2\supseteq I_3\supseteq\cdots, \qquad x_{n_k}\in I_k,\] and \[\text{length}(I_k)=\frac{M-m}{2^k}\to0.\] Write \(I_k=[a_k,b_k]\). The nested-interval theorem proved just above gives a unique \(L\) in every \(I_k\). Since \(x_{n_k}\) and \(L\) both belong to \(I_k\), \[|x_{n_k}-L|\le b_k-a_k=\frac{M-m}{2^k}\longrightarrow0.\] Thus \(x_{n_k}\to L\). The existence of the common point \(L\) is exactly where completeness enters the argument. ◻

Theorem 6.7

A Useful Converse Test

Let \((x_n)\) be bounded. If every convergent subsequence of \((x_n)\) has the same limit \(L\), then \(x_n\to L\).

Show proof

Proof. Suppose \(x_n\not\to L\). The negation of convergence supplies \(\varepsilon_0>0\) such that, after every index \(N\), some \(n\ge N\) satisfies \(|x_n-L|\ge\varepsilon_0\). Choose \(n_1\) with this property. Having chosen \(n_k\), apply the same statement with \(N=n_k+1\) to choose \(n_{k+1}>n_k\). We have produced a subsequence with \[|x_{n_k}-L|\ge\varepsilon_0\qquad\text{for every }k.\] It is bounded because the original sequence is bounded. By Bolzano–Weierstrass, it has a convergent subsequence \((x_{n_{k_j}})\). This is also a subsequence of the original sequence, so the hypothesis says that its limit is \(L\). But convergence to \(L\) would make \(|x_{n_{k_j}}-L|<\varepsilon_0\) for all sufficiently large \(j\), contradicting the displayed inequality. Therefore \(x_n\to L\). ◻
