From a Recursive Description to a Formula

💡 Motivation

In the previous lecture, recurrence relations appeared because we were counting. That remains our point of view.

Sometimes the recurrence itself is already enough: it lets us compute the desired numbers, prove inequalities, or compare two combinatorial families. Sometimes, however, a closed formula is useful. In this lecture I shall introduce only a small collection of solving methods that repeatedly occur in counting problems.

We shall therefore keep asking: What did the recurrence count, and what does the final formula tell us?\boxed{\text{What did the recurrence count, and what does the final formula tell us?}}

We shall not try to develop a general theory of difference equations. Our toolbox will be deliberately small: iteration, telescoping, second-order characteristic equations, simple forcing terms, and small systems created by keeping track of more than one combinatorial state.

Reading Recurrences from a State Graph

A state graph is a compact way of recording which symbol may follow which. Each vertex represents the current state. We draw a directed edge from state uu to state vv when vv is allowed immediately after uu. A loop means that the same state may be repeated.

To read a recurrence, fix a final state and add the counts of all states from which we can enter it. This is simply the Addition Principle written on a graph.

Our convention in the diagrams

A two-headed edge represents two opposite directed edges. If no edge is drawn, the transition is forbidden.

Example 8.1

Words over {0,1,2}\{0,1,2\} with Neighbour Difference at Most One

Let xnx_n be the number of length-nn words over {0,1,2}\{0,1,2\} such that any two neighbouring symbols differ by at most 11. Construct recurrences from the transition graph and reduce them to one recurrence for xnx_n.

Solution.
Tools to recall.

Theorem 1.2 (Addition Principle); Definition 7.2 (Initial Conditions)

Let unu_n be the number of valid length-nn words ending in 00. By symmetry, the number ending in 22 is also unu_n. Let vnv_n be the number ending in 11.

To end in 00, the preceding symbol may be 00 or 11. Therefore un=un−1+vn−1.\boxed{u_n=u_{n-1}+v_{n-1}}. \tag{1} To end in 11, the preceding symbol may be 0,1,0,1, or 22. From (1) and (2), vn=un+un−1.v_n=u_n+u_{n-1}. Hence un+1=un+vn=2un+un−1.u_{n+1}=u_n+v_n=2u_n+u_{n-1}. The same second-order recurrence is satisfied by every linear combination of the two state sequences, and in particular by xnx_n. Therefore xn+1=2xn+xn−1.\boxed{x_{n+1}=2x_n+x_{n-1}}. The natural initial conditions are x0=1x_0=1 for the empty word and x1=3x_1=3 for the one-letter words 0,1,20,1,2. Thus x2=7x_2=7, agreeing with a direct check.

Solution.

Recurrences with Two Varying Parameters

So far most of our sequences have had one index: a0,a1,a2,….a_0,a_1,a_2,\ldots. In many counting problems, however, one number does not record enough information. We must remember both the total size of the object and a second statistic. We then obtain a triangular array a(n,r),a(n,r), rather than a single sequence.

Typical examples are: c(n,r)=permutations of [n] having exactly r cycles,S(n,r)=partitions of [n] into exactly r blocks,A(n,r)=permutations of [n] having exactly r descents,p(n,r)=integer partitions of n into exactly r parts.\begin{array}{ccl} c(n,r)&=&\text{permutations of \([n]\) having exactly \(r\) cycles},\\[1mm] S(n,r)&=&\text{partitions of \([n]\) into exactly \(r\) blocks},\\[1mm] A(n,r)&=&\text{permutations of \([n]\) having exactly \(r\) descents},\\[1mm] p(n,r)&=&\text{integer partitions of \(n\) into exactly \(r\) parts}. \end{array}

Why I introduced this in class

The important new feature is that both indices vary. When we pass from size nn to size n+1n+1, the second parameter may stay the same or change by one. Therefore the recurrence usually connects entries in two neighbouring rows and two neighbouring columns.

Permutations as Products of Cycles

In Lecture 2 we viewed a permutation as a bijection σ:[n]→[n].\sigma:[n]\longrightarrow[n]. For the recurrence we are about to study, cycle notation is the natural language.

Start with an element ii and repeatedly apply σ\sigma: i,σ(i),σ2(i),σ3(i),….i,\ \sigma(i),\ \sigma^2(i),\ \sigma^3(i),\ldots. Because [n][n] is finite, some value must repeat. Since σ\sigma is one-to-one, the first repetition returns to the starting element. Thus every element lies in a cycle.

For example, suppose σ(1)=4,σ(4)=1,σ(2)=3,σ(3)=5,σ(5)=2.\sigma(1)=4,\quad \sigma(4)=1, \qquad \sigma(2)=3,\quad \sigma(3)=5,\quad \sigma(5)=2. Then σ=(14)(235).\boxed{\sigma=(1\ 4)(2\ 3\ 5)}. The order of disjoint cycles does not matter, and a cycle may be rotated: (235)=(352)=(523).(2\ 3\ 5)=(3\ 5\ 2)=(5\ 2\ 3).

Terminology from graph theory and permutation theory

A one-cycle is a fixed point. A two-cycle is a transposition. A permutation whose cycles all have length 11 or 22 is an involution. A permutation with no one-cycles is a derangement, connecting this discussion with the derangement recurrence from Lecture 7.

Unsigned Stirling Numbers of the First Kind

Definition 8.1

Unsigned Stirling Number of the First Kind

Let c(n,r)c(n,r) denote the number of permutations of [n][n] having exactly rr disjoint cycles. These are the unsigned Stirling numbers of the first kind.

The boundary values are c(n,1)=(n−1)!,c(n,n)=1,c(n,1)=(n-1)!,\qquad c(n,n)=1, because a one-cycle permutation is a cyclic ordering of all nn elements, while the identity is the only permutation having nn cycles.

Theorem 8.1

Stirling Recurrence of the First Kind

For 1≤r≤n+11\le r\le n+1, c(n+1,r)=c(n,r−1)+nc(n,r).\boxed{c(n+1,r)=c(n,r-1)+n\,c(n,r)}.

Proof.

Focus on the new element n+1n+1.

Case 1: n+1n+1 forms a cycle by itself.

Then the other nn elements must form exactly r−1r-1 cycles. This contributes c(n,r−1).c(n,r-1).

Case 2: n+1n+1 belongs to an existing cycle.

First choose a permutation of [n][n] having exactly rr cycles. In cycle notation, insert n+1n+1 immediately after one of the nn existing elements. There are nn possible insertion places, and the number of cycles does not change. Hence this case contributes nc(n,r).nc(n,r).

The construction is reversible: remove n+1n+1 from its cycle to recover the unique permutation of [n][n] and the unique place after which it had been inserted. Thus the two cases are disjoint, complete, and counted exactly once.

Stirling Numbers of the Second Kind

We first met the Stirling numbers of the second kind in Lecture 5, where they counted distributions of distinct objects into unlabelled nonempty boxes. Their interpretation through set partitions and equivalence relations is developed further in Appendix B.

Let S(n,r)S(n,r) denote the number of partitions of [n][n] into exactly rr nonempty unlabelled blocks.

Example 8.4

Place the elements of [k][k] in increasing order on a vertical line and the elements of [n][n] on a parallel vertical line. A relation is represented by joining related pairs. Theorem 2.4 (Bijection Principle)

Because there are no isolated points, the first point on the left and the first point on the right must be joined. Now exactly one of the following three cases occurs.

Case 1: the first left point is also joined to the second right point.

Then the first right point cannot be joined to any later left point, because that would create a crossing. Delete the first right point. What remains is an arbitrary object counted by f(k,n−1).f(k,n-1). The deletion is reversible by adding a new first right point joined only to the first left point.

Case 2: the second left point is joined to the first right point.

By the symmetric argument, deleting the first left point gives f(k−1,n)f(k-1,n) possibilities.

Case 3: neither of the two extra boundary edges in Cases 1 and 2 occurs.

The first left and first right points are then joined only to each other. Delete both. This gives f(k−1,n−1)f(k-1,n-1) possibilities.

The noncrossing condition makes the three cases disjoint and ensures that no fourth case is possible. Hence f(k,n)=f(k,n−1)+f(k−1,n)+f(k−1,n−1).\boxed{f(k,n)=f(k,n-1)+f(k-1,n)+f(k-1,n-1)}. Method 1: Iteration

Example 8.5

Tower of Hanoi

A tower of nn discs of distinct sizes is stacked on one of three pegs, with larger discs below smaller discs. One move transfers exactly one disc, and no disc may be placed on a smaller disc. Let hnh_n be the minimum number of moves needed to transfer the complete tower to another peg. Prove hn=2hn−1+1h_n=2h_{n-1}+1 and solve the recurrence.

1. Start with the complete tower on AA.

2. Move the smaller tower from AA to BB.

3. Move the largest disc from AA to CC.

4. Move the smaller tower from BB to CC.

Solution.

There is a direct procedure:

  1. move the top n−1n-1 discs to the auxiliary peg, requiring hn−1h_{n-1} moves;

  2. move the largest disc once;

  3. move the n−1n-1 smaller discs onto the largest disc, requiring another hn−1h_{n-1} moves.

Thus hn≤2hn−1+1.h_n\le 2h_{n-1}+1.

Conversely, in any successful procedure the largest disc must move. Before it can move, all n−1n-1 smaller discs must be transferred to the third peg, requiring at least hn−1h_{n-1} moves. After the largest disc moves, those smaller discs must be transferred onto it, requiring at least another hn−1h_{n-1} moves. Therefore hn≥2hn−1+1.h_n\ge 2h_{n-1}+1. Combining the two inequalities, hn=2hn−1+1,h1=1.\boxed{h_n=2h_{n-1}+1}, \qquad h_1=1.

Now substitute backwards: hn=2hn−1+1=2(2hn−2+1)+1=22hn−2+2+1=⋯=2n−1h1+2n−2+⋯+2+1.\begin{aligned} h_n &=2h_{n-1}+1\\ &=2(2h_{n-2}+1)+1\\ &=2^2h_{n-2}+2+1\\ &=\cdots\\ &=2^{n-1}h_1+2^{n-2}+\cdots+2+1. \end{aligned} Since h1=1h_1=1, hn=2n−1+2n−2+⋯+2+1=2n−1.h_n=2^{n-1}+2^{n-2}+\cdots+2+1=2^n-1. A Third Proof for the Triangular-Grid Parallelograms

The same triangular-grid count has already appeared twice in Part I:

  • the Lecture 3 bijection proof, where one orientation is encoded by four chosen points on an extended base;

  • the Lecture 4 layered proof, which uses binomial identities and the hockey-stick identity.

We now give a third proof, using a recurrence.

Example 8.8

Parallelograms by Recurrence

Let ana_n be the total number of parallelograms in the nn-th triangular subdivision, counted over all three orientations. Prove an=an−1+3(n+23)a_n=a_{n-1}+3\binom{n+2}{3} and hence recover an=3(n+34).a_n=3\binom{n+3}{4}.

Solution.

The (n−1)(n-1)-st subdivision sits naturally inside the nn-th subdivision. Therefore every old parallelogram contributes to an−1a_{n-1}. It remains to count the new parallelograms that use at least one vertex on the newly added base layer.

The base contains n+2n+2 grid nodes. The number of nodes not on the base is 1+2+⋯+(n+1)=(n+1)(n+2)2.1+2+\cdots+(n+1)=\frac{(n+1)(n+2)}2. A new parallelogram is determined by choosing its acute vertex uu on the base and the opposite acute vertex vv off the base, except that uu and vv may not lie on a single grid line. For each of the n+2n+2 choices of uu, exactly n+1n+1 off-base nodes are excluded in this way. Hence the number of new parallelograms is (n+2)⋅(n+1)(n+2)2−(n+2)(n+1)=n(n+1)(n+2)2=3(n+23).\begin{aligned} &(n+2)\cdot\frac{(n+1)(n+2)}2-(n+2)(n+1)\\ &\qquad=\frac{n(n+1)(n+2)}2 =3\binom{n+2}{3}. \end{aligned} Therefore an=an−1+3(n+23).\boxed{a_n=a_{n-1}+3\binom{n+2}{3}}. Since a1=3a_1=3, backward summation gives an=3+3∑j=2n(j+23)=3∑j=1n(j+23)=3(n+34),\begin{aligned} a_n &=3+3\sum_{j=2}^{n}\binom{j+2}{3}\\ &=3\sum_{j=1}^{n}\binom{j+2}{3}\\ &=3\binom{n+3}{4}, \end{aligned} where the last step is the hockey-stick identity. Thus an=3(n+34).\boxed{a_n=3\binom{n+3}{4}}.

What to notice.

The three proofs count the same objects but expose different structures: a bijection, a layered binomial sum, and a recurrence obtained by adding one new layer.

Return to the composition recurrence from Lecture 7: pn=pn−1+pn−2+⋯+p0.p_n=p_{n-1}+p_{n-2}+\cdots+p_0. For n−1n-1, pn−1=pn−2+⋯+p0.p_{n-1}=p_{n-2}+\cdots+p_0. Subtract: pn−pn−1=pn−1.p_n-p_{n-1}=p_{n-1}. Thus pn=2pn−1.p_n=2p_{n-1}. Since p1=1p_1=1, pn=2n−1.\boxed{p_n=2^{n-1}}.

When the Characteristic Method Does Not Apply

⚠ Common mistake

The characteristic-root method is not a universal recurrence solver.

It applies directly to linear recurrences with constant coefficients, such as an=5an−1−6an−2.a_n=5a_{n-1}-6a_{n-2}.

It does not directly solve: an=an−12+1,a_n=a_{n-1}^2+1, or Cn=∑k=0n−1CkCn−1−k,C_n=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}C_kC_{n-1-k}, or recurrences with strongly varying coefficients such as an=nan−1+1.a_n=na_{n-1}+1.

Key Lessons from Lecture 8

Remark

The main methods are:

  1. First derive the recurrence from the counting problem; solving comes second.

  2. Use iteration when a first-order recurrence expands cleanly.

  3. Use telescoping when an−an−1a_n-a_{n-1} appears.

  4. Use the characteristic equation mainly for simple second-order constant-coefficient recurrences that arise from strings, tilings, and similar objects.

  5. Use a shift for simple constant forcing terms.

  6. Keep two or more states when one sequence forgets information that the next step needs.

  7. For a two-parameter array a(n,r)a(n,r), record both the recurrence and all boundary values, then compute the table row by row.

  8. In Stirling-type recurrences, focus on the new element and decide whether it creates a new cycle or block, or enters an existing one.

  9. We do not need a closed formula every time. A recurrence can itself be a very satisfactory solution to a counting problem.
