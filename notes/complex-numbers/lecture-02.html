💡 The problem to resolve

We know how to rotate and dilate about OO by multiplying polar points. What if the prescribed centre is some other point PP? The geometric plan is to bring PP to OO, perform the known scale and turn, and then put the whole picture back. We need to understand the motion that carries one point to another without turning or changing any length: translation.

The distinction between the two centres matters. Multiplying the position of AA by an origin-centred instruction changes its distance and direction from OO. A rotation about PP must instead change the arrow PAPA, while leaving PP fixed. In the following drawing the two arrows beginning at PP have the turn and length ratio prescribed by the instruction; their positions measured from OO are not the data to multiply.

Polar coordinates make rotation and dilation simple. Cartesian coordinates make translation simple. We want one arithmetic in which we can perform all three without repeatedly changing the kind of calculation. The chosen origin, rightward unit ray and anticlockwise orientation fix the points that will be called 00, 11 and ii; with these choices, geometry will supply the operations.

2.1  From a position to a displacement

The point A=(x,y)A=(x,y) is a position. The arrow from the origin to AA is a displacement: move xx units horizontally and yy units vertically. The same displacement can begin at a different point. If it begins at P=(u,v)P=(u,v), it ends at (u+x,v+y)(u+x,v+y).

This distinction explains why a point can represent a translation. The point supplies the displacement measured from the origin; the translation applies that displacement everywhere. For points A=(x,y)A=(x,y) and B=(u,v)B=(u,v), define A+B=(x+u,y+v).\boxed{A+B=(x+u,y+v).} To construct A+BA+B, start at AA and copy the arrow OBOB. The endpoint is the sum. Starting at BB and copying OAOA gives the same endpoint: these are the two routes around a parallelogram. An arrow may be slid without changing the displacement it represents: its horizontal and vertical changes stay the same. In particular, sliding OBOB until its tail is at AA gives the arrow from AA to A+BA+B. This is the parallelogram law familiar from vectors, developed here directly from the geometry of points. Its diagonal is O(A+B)O(A+B); the two routes O→A→A+BO\to A\to A+B and O→B→A+BO\to B\to A+B reach the same point.

Example 2.1 · Slide one arrow over the other

Let A=(1,2)A=(1,2) and B=(4,5)B=(4,5). The arrow OBOB moves four units right and five units up. Slide that arrow so it begins at AA: its tip reaches (1,2)+(4,5)=(1+4,2+5)=(5,7).(1,2)+(4,5)=(1+4,2+5)=\boxed{(5,7)}. The equality of these two routes is the geometric meaning of A+B=B+AA+B=B+A.

Writing TB(X)=X+BT_B(X)=X+B for the translation supplied by OBOB, two successive translations obey TC(TB(X))=(X+B)+C=X+(B+C)=TB+C(X).T_C(T_B(X))=(X+B)+C=X+(B+C)=T_{B+C}(X). The inverse translation is T−BT_{-B}: translating by BB and then by the opposite arrow −B-B brings every point back. Thus the addition of points records the composition of translations, just as the polar multiplication of Lecture 1 records the composition of scale and turn instructions about OO.

2.1.1  The additive laws

The zero point (0,0)(0,0) is an additive identity because (x,y)+(0,0)=(x,y).(x,y)+(0,0)=(x,y). The additive inverse of (x,y)(x,y) is (−x,−y)(-x,-y), since (x,y)+(−x,−y)=(0,0).(x,y)+(-x,-y)=(0,0). Geometrically the inverse displacement has the same length and the opposite direction.

Commutativity follows coordinate by coordinate: (x,y)+(u,v)=(x+u,y+v)=(u+x,v+y)=(u,v)+(x,y).(x,y)+(u,v)=(x+u,y+v)=(u+x,v+y)=(u,v)+(x,y). For associativity, let C=(s,t)C=(s,t). 2.1.2  Subtraction gives the displacement between two points

Define B−A=B+(−A)B-A=B+(-A). If A=(x,y)A=(x,y) and B=(u,v)B=(u,v), then B−A=(u−x,v−y).B-A=(u-x,v-y). Starting at AA and performing this displacement ends at BB, because A+(B−A)=BA+(B-A)=B. They are related, but they are not the same kind of object. For the points A=(1,2)A=(1,2) and B=(4,5)B=(4,5) just used, the arrow from AA to BB is B−A=(4−1,5−2)=(3,3).B-A=(4-1,5-2)=(3,3). 2.2  The three-stage recipe for an arbitrary centre

Our multiplication rotates and dilates about the origin. Suppose the required centre is a point PP different from the origin. Let AA be the point to be moved, and let U=[k,θ]U=[k,\theta] describe the desired scale and turn. When we “bring PP to the origin,” we translate every point by the same displacement −P-P. Otherwise the relative arrow PAPA would change and we would be solving a different problem. Translation changes the position of an arrow without changing the arrow itself.

  1. Bring the centre to the origin. Subtract PP from every point. The centre becomes P−P=0P-P=0, and AA becomes A−PA-P.

  2. Perform the turn and scale. Multiply the displacement A−PA-P by UU. Its image is U(A−P)U(A-P).

  3. Restore the centre's original position. Add PP back. The final point is P+U(A−P)P+U(A-P).

Thus the whole construction is A↦P+U(A−P).\boxed{A\longmapsto P+U(A-P).} At this stage, however, the expression hides a practical inconvenience. Subtraction and addition are easiest in Cartesian coordinates, while our multiplication has been defined in polar coordinates. To evaluate the formula, we would have to convert the displacement to polar form, multiply there, and convert back. We want the multiplication rule directly in Cartesian coordinates.

Example 2.3 · A quarter-turn and doubling about a centre away from OO

A quarter-turn takes (1,1)(1,1) to (−1,1)(-1,1); doubling the resulting arrow gives U(A−P)=(−2,2).U(A-P)=(-2,2). Finally restore the original centre: A′=P+U(A−P)=(2,1)+(−2,2)=(0,3).A'=P+U(A-P)=(2,1)+(-2,2)=\boxed{(0,3)}. The original arrow PA=(1,1)PA=(1,1) has length 2\sqrt2. The final arrow PA′=(−2,2)PA'=(-2,2) has length 222\sqrt2 and is its anticlockwise quarter-turn. Meanwhile PP stays fixed because P+U(P−P)=P+UO=PP+U(P-P)=P+UO=P. The result is a rotation and dilation about PP.

In a final algebraic form, we shall write the same recipe as z↦p+u(z−p)z\mapsto p+u(z-p). It is useful to read it from the inside out: z−pz-p is the arrow from the chosen centre to zz, multiplication by uu changes that arrow, and addition of pp places its tail back at the chosen centre.

2.3  Constructing the Argand plane from two coordinate languages

The construction so far has not introduced a second physical plane. The same point can be described by its Cartesian pair (x,y)(x,y) or by the polar data [r,θ][r,\theta]. A rectangular grid lets us move xx units horizontally and yy units vertically. A ray and a distance from OO let us turn through an angle and multiply a length. For r>0r>0 the two descriptions are related by x=rcosθ,y=rsinθ.x=r\cos\theta,\qquad y=r\sin\theta. At OO we have (x,y)=(0,0)(x,y)=(0,0) and r=0r=0, but no particular direction angle. Thus the origin needs a separate convention whenever a polar angle is used.

We now equip this one set of points with both operations: translation supplies coordinate addition, while origin-centred spiral similarity supplies polar multiplication. The latter must be translated into Cartesian coordinates before the two rules can be used together without changing notation. The resulting plane, with its arithmetic, is called the complex plane or Argand plane. Its geometry is the reason for its algebraic rules.

2.4  The angle-addition formulas from geometry

We will need the formulas for the cosine and sine of a sum. They can themselves be read from a rotation.

A unit arrow making angle α\alpha with the horizontal has components (cosα,sinα)(\cos\alpha,\sin\alpha). Its anticlockwise perpendicular unit arrow has components (−sinα,cosα)(-\sin\alpha,\cos\alpha): a quarter-turn sends a rightward component upward and an upward component leftward.

Now draw a unit arrow making an additional angle β\beta from the first arrow. Resolve it along the first arrow and its perpendicular. The signed components are cosβ\cos\beta and sinβ\sin\beta. Scaling each unit arrow and adding the resulting displacements give (cos(α+β),sin(α+β))=cosβ(cosα,sinα)+sinβ(−sinα,cosα).\begin{aligned} (\cos(\alpha+\beta),\sin(\alpha+\beta)) &=\cos\beta(\cos\alpha,\sin\alpha)\\ &\quad+\sin\beta(-\sin\alpha,\cos\alpha). \end{aligned} Here real multiplication of a displacement means ordinary scaling; the displayed sum is the componentwise addition already defined. Comparing horizontal and vertical components gives cos(α+β)=cosαcosβ−sinαsinβ,sin(α+β)=sinαcosβ+cosαsinβ.\boxed{\begin{aligned} \cos(\alpha+\beta)&=\cos\alpha\cos\beta-\sin\alpha\sin\beta,\\ \sin(\alpha+\beta)&=\sin\alpha\cos\beta+\cos\alpha\sin\beta. \end{aligned}} This resolution argument works with signed components in every quadrant. It is a geometric calculation, independent of the complex multiplication rule we are about to derive.

2.5  Deriving multiplication in Cartesian coordinates

Let the same two nonzero points have the descriptions P=(x1,y1)=[r1,θ1],x1=r1cosθ1,y1=r1sinθ1,Q=(x2,y2)=[r2,θ2],x2=r2cosθ2,y2=r2sinθ2.\begin{aligned} P&=(x_1,y_1)=[r_1,\theta_1],&x_1&=r_1\cos\theta_1,&y_1&=r_1\sin\theta_1,\\ Q&=(x_2,y_2)=[r_2,\theta_2],&x_2&=r_2\cos\theta_2,&y_2&=r_2\sin\theta_2. \end{aligned} Their product has polar data [r1r2,θ1+θ2][r_1r_2,\theta_1+\theta_2]. Its horizontal coordinate is x=r1r2cos(θ1+θ2)=r1r2(cosθ1cosθ2−sinθ1sinθ2)=(r1cosθ1)(r2cosθ2)−(r1sinθ1)(r2sinθ2)=x1x2−y1y2.\begin{aligned} x&=r_1r_2\cos(\theta_1+\theta_2)\\ &=r_1r_2(\cos\theta_1\cos\theta_2-\sin\theta_1\sin\theta_2)\\ &=(r_1\cos\theta_1)(r_2\cos\theta_2) -(r_1\sin\theta_1)(r_2\sin\theta_2)\\ &=x_1x_2-y_1y_2. \end{aligned} Its vertical coordinate is y=r1r2sin(θ1+θ2)=r1r2(sinθ1cosθ2+cosθ1sinθ2)=(r1sinθ1)(r2cosθ2)+(r1cosθ1)(r2sinθ2)=y1x2+x1y2.\begin{aligned} y&=r_1r_2\sin(\theta_1+\theta_2)\\ &=r_1r_2(\sin\theta_1\cos\theta_2+\cos\theta_1\sin\theta_2)\\ &=(r_1\sin\theta_1)(r_2\cos\theta_2) +(r_1\cos\theta_1)(r_2\sin\theta_2)\\ &=y_1x_2+x_1y_2. \end{aligned} We have obtained the rule (x1,y1)(x2,y2)=(x1x2−y1y2,x1y2+y1x2).\boxed{(x_1,y_1)(x_2,y_2) =(x_1x_2-y_1y_2,\ x_1y_2+y_1x_2).} The rule also gives (0,0)(0,0) when either factor is (0,0)(0,0), agreeing with our separate definition of the zero product. This is the same multiplication as before, expressed in another coordinate system.

2.6  Why addition and multiplication belong together

We already know the separate additive and multiplicative laws. The crucial remaining law is distributivity. Let P=(x,y)P=(x,y), Q=(u,v)Q=(u,v) and R=(s,t)R=(s,t). Then P(Q+R)=(x,y)(u+s,v+t)=(x(u+s)−y(v+t),x(v+t)+y(u+s))=(xu+xs−yv−yt,xv+xt+yu+ys)=(xu−yv,xv+yu)+(xs−yt,xt+ys)=PQ+PR.\begin{aligned} P(Q+R)&=(x,y)(u+s,v+t)\\ &=(x(u+s)-y(v+t),\ x(v+t)+y(u+s))\\ &=(xu+xs-yv-yt,\ xv+xt+yu+ys)\\ &=(xu-yv,xv+yu)+(xs-yt,xt+ys)\\ &=PQ+PR. \end{aligned} Commutativity of multiplication also gives (Q+R)P=QP+RP(Q+R)P=QP+RP.

There is a geometric reason for the calculation. Multiplication by a fixed nonzero point rotates and dilates a parallelogram about the origin. Its two side displacements and its diagonal are all transformed together. The image diagonal is the sum of the image sides. Multiplication by zero collapses all of them to the origin, so the same equality holds there as well.

We now have a number system: addition and multiplication are closed, associative and commutative; they have identities 0,10,1; every point has an additive inverse; every nonzero point has a multiplicative inverse; and multiplication distributes over addition. A system with these properties is called a field. We will use the properties, rather than require familiarity with the terminology.

2.7  The point ii and the expression x+iyx+iy

We built the point set from ordered pairs of real numbers. Definition 2.1 · Complex numbers

A complex number is a point of this plane equipped with the addition and multiplication we have constructed. The set of all such numbers is denoted by ℂ\mathbb C. If z=x+iyz=x+iy, then xx is its real part and yy its imaginary part, written Rez=x\operatorname{Re}z=x and Imz=y\operatorname{Im}z=y.

The imaginary part is the real number yy, not the expression iyiy. Two complex numbers are equal exactly when both their real parts and their imaginary parts are equal.

2.7.1  Cartesian plane and complex plane: what is the difference?

There is no new collection of geometric points. The point (2,3)(2,3) in the Cartesian plane is the point 2+3i2+3i in the complex plane. Cartesian and polar coordinates are two ways of locating it. Calling the plane complex means that we have supplied it with a particular arithmetic, especially the multiplication that encodes turning and scaling.

The ordinary Cartesian plane can already describe displacements and vector addition. It can also describe transformations by pairs of coordinate formulas. The additional structure here is that a single point can be multiplied with another point, producing a rotation and a dilation. This multiplication makes expressions such as p+u(z−p)p+u(z-p) meaningful and economical.

(1,3)↔[2,60∘]↔1+i3.(1,\sqrt3) \quad\longleftrightarrow\quad [2,60^\circ] \quad\longleftrightarrow\quad 1+i\sqrt3. The Cartesian description gives horizontal and vertical components. The polar description gives distance and direction. The complex notation lets us calculate with both addition and multiplication in one system.

2.7.2  Ordinary algebra now has a geometric foundation

2.8  All three operations in one plane

For u=k(cosθ+isinθ)u=k(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta) with k>0k>0, multiplication by uu turns through θ\theta and scales by kk. Addition of tt translates by the displacement tt. Expanding the same formula gives z′=uz+(1−u)pz'=uz+(1-u)p. Conversely, a transformation z′=uz+v,u≠0,z'=uz+v,\qquad u\ne0, is a rotation and dilation about some centre when u≠1u\ne1. 2.8.1  The rotation matrix is the same rule in another notation

For scale 11, multiplication by cosθ+isinθ\cos\theta+i\sin\theta gives x′=xcosθ−ysinθ,y′=xsinθ+ycosθ.x'=x\cos\theta-y\sin\theta,\qquad y'=x\sin\theta+y\cos\theta. A matrix is a rectangular array used here to store the coefficients of these two linear equations. The notation (x′y′)=(cosθ−sinθsinθcosθ)(xy)\begin{pmatrix}x'\\y'\end{pmatrix} =\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta&-\sin\theta\\\sin\theta&\cos\theta\end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}x\\y\end{pmatrix} means exactly the preceding two equations: multiply across each row and add. No extra geometric operation has been introduced. Complex multiplication packages the same information into a single number.

2.9  Modulus, argument and conjugation

The modulus of z=x+iyz=x+iy is its distance from the origin: |z|=x2+y2.\left|z\right|=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}. For z≠0z\ne0, any direction of its ray is called an argument of zz. Thus z=|z|(cosθ+isinθ).z=\left|z\right|(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta). We will keep the trigonometric expression cosθ+isinθ\cos\theta+i\sin\theta written in full. The modulus is unique; an argument can be changed by complete turns.

If one particular argument is wanted, we choose the interval −180∘<θ≤180∘or equivalently−π<θ≤π-180^\circ<\theta\le180^\circ\qquad\text{or equivalently}\qquad-\pi<\theta\le\pi and write Argz\operatorname{Arg}z. Thus the negative real axis has the representative 180∘180^\circ, not −180∘-180^\circ. Every nonzero ray is represented exactly once; the origin still has no argument. When adding angles, we first perform the actual turns and only then choose a representative in this interval.

Reflection in the real axis sends (x,y)(x,y) to (x,−y)(x,-y). Its complex coordinate is called the conjugate: z‾=x−iy.\bar z=x-iy. Then z+z‾=2x,z−z‾=2iy,zz‾=(x+iy)(x−iy)=x2+y2=|z|2.\begin{aligned} z+\bar z&=2x,&z-\bar z&=2iy,\\ z\bar z&=(x+iy)(x-iy)=x^2+y^2=\left|z\right|^2. \end{aligned} Consequently Rez=z+z‾2,Imz=z−z‾2i.\operatorname{Re}z=\frac{z+\bar z}{2},\qquad\operatorname{Im}z=\frac{z-\bar z}{2i}. The second expression is real even though it uses ii in its denominator.

In polar form, r(cosθ+isinθ)¯=r(cosθ−isinθ)=r(cos(−θ)+isin(−θ)).\overline{r(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)} =r(\cos\theta-i\sin\theta) =r\bigl(\cos(-\theta)+i\sin(-\theta)\bigr). Reflection therefore reverses a turn. Direct expansion also gives z+w¯=z‾+w‾,zw¯=z‾w‾.\overline{z+w}=\bar z+\bar w,\qquad\overline{zw}=\bar z\bar w. For example, the real part of zwzw is xu−yvxu-yv and its imaginary part is xv+yuxv+yu; conjugating changes only the latter's sign, which is also the result of multiplying (x−iy)(u−iv)(x-iy)(u-iv).

2.10  Division: undoing a spiral similarity

If w≠0w\ne0, the equation wq=zwq=z has the unique solution q=w−1zq=w^{-1}z, denoted by z/wz/w. Uniqueness follows by multiplying the equation by w−1w^{-1}. Geometrically, division by ww undoes its scale and turn.

Since ww‾=|w|2>0w\bar w=\left|w\right|^2>0, w−1=w‾|w|2,zw=zw‾|w|2.w^{-1}=\frac{\bar w}{\left|w\right|^2},\qquad \boxed{\frac zw=\frac{z\bar w}{\left|w\right|^2}.} Multiplying numerator and denominator by the conjugate makes the denominator real. This is a consequence of reflection and the modulus, not an isolated trick. To read the inverse as a point, let w=s(cosϕ+isinϕ)w=s(\cos\phi+i\sin\phi) with s>0s>0. The conjugate w‾=s(cosϕ−isinϕ)=s(cos(−ϕ)+isin(−ϕ))\bar w=s(\cos\phi-i\sin\phi) =s\bigl(\cos(-\phi)+i\sin(-\phi)\bigr) is the reflected arrow of the same length. Multiplying it by 1/s21/s^2 leaves its direction unchanged and makes its length 1/s1/s. Thus w−1=1s(cosϕ−isinϕ).w^{-1}=\frac1s(\cos\phi-i\sin\phi).

Example 2.7 · A quotient calculated completely

Find (3+4i)/(1−2i)(3+4i)/(1-2i).

Solution

3+4i1−2i=(3+4i)(1+2i)(1−2i)(1+2i)=3+6i+4i+8i21+4=−5+10i5=−1+2i.\begin{aligned} \frac{3+4i}{1-2i} &=\frac{(3+4i)(1+2i)}{(1-2i)(1+2i)}\\ &=\frac{3+6i+4i+8i^2}{1+4}\\ &=\frac{-5+10i}{5}=-1+2i. \end{aligned} Check by multiplying back: (1−2i)(−1+2i)=−1+4i−4i2=3+4i(1-2i)(-1+2i)=-1+4i-4i^2=3+4i.

If z=r(cosθ+isinθ)z=r(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta) and w=s(cosϕ+isinϕ)w=s(\cos\phi+i\sin\phi), where r,s>0r,s>0, then zw=rs(cos(θ+ϕ)+isin(θ+ϕ)),zw=rs\bigl(\cos(\theta+\phi)+i\sin(\theta+\phi)\bigr), and zw=rs(cos(θ−ϕ)+isin(θ−ϕ)).\frac zw=\frac rs\bigl(\cos(\theta-\phi)+i\sin(\theta-\phi)\bigr). Example 2.8 · Why principal arguments need an adjustment

Let z=cos150∘+isin150∘,w=cos100∘+isin100∘.z=\cos150^\circ+i\sin150^\circ, \qquad w=\cos100^\circ+i\sin100^\circ. Their product has direction 250∘250^\circ. 2.11  Reading a quotient as geometry

Let A,B,PA,B,P be points with A≠PA\ne P. The quotient q=b−pa−pq=\frac{b-p}{a-p} is the multiplier carrying the arrow PAPA to the arrow PBPB, because q(a−p)=b−pq(a-p)=b-p. Its modulus is PB/PAPB/PA. If B≠PB\ne P, its direction is the turn from PAPA to PBPB; when B=PB=P, the multiplier is 00 and has no direction. This interpretation gives several basic criteria. We can first slide both arrows so their tails meet at 00: they become a−pa-p and b−pb-p. Next multiply both by (a−p)−1(a-p)^{-1}. The first arrow now ends at 11 and the second ends at qq. The new diagram displays the scale and directed turn of the original pair without the position PP distracting us.

Example 2.9 · Normalising two arrows with a common tail

For p=1+ip=1+i, a=3+2ia=3+2i and b=2+4ib=2+4i, compute the multiplier carrying PAPA to PBPB.

Solution

The arrows are a−p=2+ia-p=2+i and b−p=1+3ib-p=1+3i. Multiplying the first by 1+i1+i gives (1+i)(2+i)=1+3i=b−p.(1+i)(2+i)=1+3i=b-p. Since a−p≠0a-p\ne0, the multiplier is uniquely q=(b−p)/(a−p)=1+iq=(b-p)/(a-p)=1+i. Its modulus is 2\sqrt2 and its direction is 45∘45^\circ anticlockwise. Dividing the two lengths independently checks the scale: PB/PA=10/5=2PB/PA=\sqrt{10}/\sqrt5=\sqrt2.

  • If qq is positive real, the two arrows have the same direction. If qq is negative real, they have opposite directions. Thus three distinct points P,A,BP,A,B are collinear exactly when qq is real.

  • If q=itq=it for a nonzero real tt, its turn is a quarter-turn in one of the two directions. Thus PAPA and PBPB are perpendicular exactly when qq is nonzero and purely imaginary.

  • If |q|=1\left|q\right|=1, the two arrows have equal length.

  • If q=iq=i or q=−iq=-i, the arrows are perpendicular and have equal length simultaneously.

For two separate nonzero segments ABAB and CDCD, replace the arrows by b−ab-a and d−cd-c. The quotient (d−c)/(b−a)(d-c)/(b-a) is real precisely when the lines are parallel, and nonzero purely imaginary precisely when they are perpendicular. No angle shorthand is needed.

Example 2.10 · An equilateral triangle from one quotient

Suppose a≠ba\ne b and c−ab−a=12+32i.\frac{c-a}{b-a}=\frac12+\frac{\sqrt3}{2}i. The right-hand side has modulus 11 and direction 60∘60^\circ. Therefore AC=ABAC=AB and the turn from ABAB to ACAC is 60∘60^\circ anticlockwise. The triangle is isosceles with vertex angle 60∘60^\circ, so its other two angles are also 60∘60^\circ. 2.12  Finding the similarity and its centre

Suppose a direct similarity sends AA to CC and BB to DD, where a≠ba\ne b and c≠dc\ne d. Its rule has the form z′=uz+vz'=uz+v. The two image conditions give c=ua+v,d=ub+v.c=ua+v,\qquad d=ub+v. Subtracting eliminates the translation: d−c=u(b−a),u=d−cb−a.d-c=u(b-a),\qquad u=\frac{d-c}{b-a}. Then v=c−uav=c-ua. Thus both the scale-and-turn factor and the translation are determined. If u≠1u\ne1, the centre is p=v/(1−u)p=v/(1-u); if u=1u=1, the transformation is a translation or the identity.

2.13  Distances, sums and a parallelogram

Because b−ab-a is the arrow from AA to BB, its modulus is ABAB. In particular, |z−a|=R\left|z-a\right|=R is the circle with centre aa and radius R>0R>0. The equation |z−a|=|z−b|\left|z-a\right|=\left|z-b\right| says that a point is equally far from A,BA,B; when A≠BA\ne B, its locus is the perpendicular bisector of ABAB.

Example 2.12 · A perpendicular bisector calculated

Describe |z−2|=|z+2|\left|z-2\right|=\left|z+2\right|.

Solution

Write z=x+iyz=x+iy and square both nonnegative distances: (x−2)2+y2=(x+2)2+y2.(x-2)^2+y^2=(x+2)^2+y^2. Expanding and cancelling gives −4x=4x-4x=4x, hence x=0x=0. The locus is the imaginary axis, the perpendicular bisector of the segment from −2-2 to 22.

For any z,wz,w, |z+w|2=(z+w)(z‾+w‾)=|z|2+|w|2+zw‾+z‾w=|z|2+|w|2+2Re(zw‾).\begin{aligned} \left|z+w\right|^2&=(z+w)(\bar z+\bar w)\\ &=\left|z\right|^2+\left|w\right|^2+z\bar w+\bar z w\\ &=\left|z\right|^2+\left|w\right|^2+2\operatorname{Re}(z\bar w). \end{aligned} If z,w≠0z,w\ne0 and the smaller angle between their arrows is θ\theta, then Re(zw‾)=|z||w|cosθ\operatorname{Re}(z\bar w)=\left|z\right|\left|w\right|\cos\theta. Replacing ww by −w-w gives |z−w|2=|z|2+|w|2−2|z||w|cosθ.\left|z-w\right|^2=\left|z\right|^2+\left|w\right|^2-2\left|z\right|\left|w\right|\cos\theta. These are the cosine-rule calculations for the two diagonals of the addition parallelogram. If either arrow is zero, both equalities hold directly, without assigning an angle to the zero arrow.

Example 2.13 · Two diagonal lengths

Suppose |z|=2\left|z\right|=2, |w|=3\left|w\right|=3, and the angle between their arrows is 60∘60^\circ. Find |z+w|/|z−w|\left|z+w\right|/\left|z-w\right|.

Solution

Since cos60∘=1/2\cos60^\circ=1/2, |z+w|2=22+32+2⋅2⋅3⋅12=4+9+6=19,|z−w|2=22+32−2⋅2⋅3⋅12=4+9−6=7.\begin{aligned} \left|z+w\right|^2&=2^2+3^2+2\cdot2\cdot3\cdot\frac12=4+9+6=19,\\ \left|z-w\right|^2&=2^2+3^2-2\cdot2\cdot3\cdot\frac12=4+9-6=7. \end{aligned} Both lengths are nonnegative, so the required ratio is 19/7\sqrt{19}/\sqrt7.

Adding the two squared-length formulas makes the cross terms cancel: |z+w|2+|z−w|2=2|z|2+2|w|2.\boxed{\left|z+w\right|^2+\left|z-w\right|^2=2\left|z\right|^2+2\left|w\right|^2.} This is the parallelogram identity: the sum of the squared diagonal lengths equals the sum of the squared side lengths.

2.13.1  The triangle inequality

Travelling from 00 to zz and then to z+wz+w cannot be shorter than travelling directly from 00 to z+wz+w. Algebra gives the same result. Since the real part of a complex number is at most its modulus, Re(zw‾)≤|zw‾|=|z||w|.\operatorname{Re}(z\bar w)\le\left|z\bar w\right|=\left|z\right|\left|w\right|. Therefore |z+w|2≤|z|2+|w|2+2|z||w|=(|z|+|w|)2.\left|z+w\right|^2\le\left|z\right|^2+\left|w\right|^2+2\left|z\right|\left|w\right|=(\left|z\right|+\left|w\right|)^2. Taking nonnegative square roots proves |z+w|≤|z|+|w|.\boxed{\left|z+w\right|\le\left|z\right|+\left|w\right|.} For nonzero z,wz,w, equality holds exactly when zw‾z\bar w is positive real, which says that the two displacements point in the same direction. If one is zero, equality also holds. Applying the inequality to z=(z−w)+wz=(z-w)+w and then interchanging z,wz,w gives ||z|−|w||≤|z−w|.\bigl|\left|z\right|-\left|w\right|\bigr|\le\left|z-w\right|.

2.14  A worked synthesis: reflection in a tilted line

Let a line pass through pp and make angle θ\theta with the real axis. We already know how to reflect in the real axis: conjugate. To use that fact, straighten the given line, reflect, and undo the straightening: u=cosθ+isinθ,u‾=cosθ−isinθ.u=\cos\theta+i\sin\theta, \qquad \bar u=\cos\theta-i\sin\theta. Multiplication by u‾\bar u turns clockwise through θ\theta, while multiplication by uu reverses that turn. The five stages are z↦z−ptranslate the line through the origin,↦u‾(z−p)rotate it onto the real axis,↦u‾(z−p)¯reflect,↦uu‾(z−p)¯rotate back,↦p+uu‾(z−p)¯translate back.\begin{aligned} z&\longmapsto z-p&&\text{translate the line through the origin},\\ &\longmapsto\bar u(z-p)&&\text{rotate it onto the real axis},\\ &\longmapsto\overline{\bar u(z-p)}&&\text{reflect},\\ &\longmapsto u\,\overline{\bar u(z-p)}&&\text{rotate back},\\ &\longmapsto p+u\,\overline{\bar u(z-p)}&&\text{translate back}. \end{aligned} Since u‾¯=u\overline{\bar u}=u, the two rotation factors combine to u2=(cosθ+isinθ)2=cos(2θ)+isin(2θ).u^2=(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)^2 =\cos(2\theta)+i\sin(2\theta). The resulting rule is z′=p+(cos(2θ)+isin(2θ))z−p¯.\boxed{z'=p+\bigl(\cos(2\theta)+i\sin(2\theta)\bigr)\overline{z-p}.} The doubling of the line angle has a visual explanation. A ray at angle θ+δ\theta+\delta is δ\delta above the reflecting line. Its mirror is δ\delta below the line, at angle θ−δ\theta-\delta. Conjugation changes θ+δ\theta+\delta to −(θ+δ)-(\theta+\delta); the final factor cos(2θ)+isin(2θ)\cos(2\theta)+i\sin(2\theta) restores the reflected direction 2θ−(θ+δ)=θ−δ2\theta-(\theta+\delta)=\theta-\delta.

There is also a version that needs no measurement of the line angle. If the reflecting line passes through distinct points A,BA,B, use the nonzero arrow b−ab-a to make that line the real axis. The five steps become z↦z−ab−a↦(z−ab−a)¯↦z′=a+(b−a)(z−ab−a)¯.\boxed{\quad z\ \longmapsto\ \frac{z-a}{b-a}\ \longmapsto\ \overline{\left(\frac{z-a}{b-a}\right)}\ \longmapsto\ z'=a+(b-a)\overline{\left(\frac{z-a}{b-a}\right)}.\quad} The first expression both translates AA to 00 and sends BB to 11. Conjugation is then an ordinary reflection in the horizontal axis. The last expression reverses the change of coordinates. The midpoint (z+z′)/2(z+z')/2 is the perpendicular foot on ABAB: reflected points are equally distant from that line and their joining segment meets it at a right angle.

Example 2.14 · Reflecting without finding an angle

💡 The geometric idea to retain

The point ii is a quarter-turn. Addition translates, multiplication rotates and dilates, and the expression p+u(z−p)p+u(z-p) performs these actions about any centre. The algebraic laws follow from the geometry and the coordinate calculations connecting it to the real numbers.
