Preface

The arithmetic of rotations, dilations and translations.

A rotation changes a direction. A dilation changes a distance. A translation changes a position. These are geometric actions that we can draw and perform before we have any reason to speak of an imaginary number. The purpose of this course is to find an arithmetic in which all three actions can be expressed simply.

We begin with spiral similarity: a turn and a change of scale about the same centre. Polar coordinates make this transformation almost effortless to describe. Distances multiply and angles add. A multiplication of points is already taking shape. Translation then suggests addition. The difficulty is that the first operation is simplest in polar coordinates and the second in Cartesian coordinates. Resolving that difficulty leads to the multiplication rule for ordered pairs, to the point ii, and finally to the expression x+iyx+iy.

In this development, i2=−1i^2=-1 is not an unexplained rule. It records two successive quarter-turns. The distributive law is not something to be accepted merely because the notation resembles ordinary algebra; it must be established. A quotient records the scale and turn that carry one displacement to another. Roots of unity describe equally spaced points on a circle. Complex equations describe square centres, equilateral triangles and the Euler line.

This book contains five lectures. Each lecture has a continuous development, worked problems and exercises. The written calculations and supporting proofs are deliberately fuller than a blackboard outline: a reader should be able to reconstruct the reasoning between successive lines. Lecture 3 develops de Moivre's theorem from the meaning of multiplication and finishes with rotation matrices. Lecture 4 moves from angles to roots of unity and integer-coefficient rigidity. Lecture 5 begins with triangle areas and unequal divisions of a segment. It ends by using barycentric coordinates to explain why an affine function on a triangle attains its extreme values at vertices.

Each of the five lectures has a homework set with a worked solution beneath each problem. Appendix A closes with five further problems for independent study.

Only arithmetic with real numbers and elementary algebra are assumed at the start. The polynomial gcd calculation is explained when it is needed, and Eisenstein's irreducibility criterion is stated before its application. The geometric facts needed along the way are introduced in their usual elementary form. Degrees are used at the beginning; radians are introduced through arc length before they are used in formulas. Clockwise and anticlockwise turns are described explicitly. In Lecture 4 we introduce equality modulo a half-turn from its definition, distinguishing the direction of an arrow from the direction of its supporting line. Fractional and real powers are treated with a chosen angle; rational approximation uses continuity, not density alone. The distinction between a location and a displacement, and between an ordered pair and its complex-coordinate notation, will be kept visible throughout.

Appendix A returns to the Euler-line theorem from Lecture 3. The lecture proves it by complex coordinates; the appendix begins instead with a diameter of the circumcircle and develops a synthetic proof from parallel lines, a parallelogram and similar triangles. The two proofs make the central theme visible from opposite directions: geometry gives the algebra its meaning, and algebra reveals the geometry with unusual economy.
