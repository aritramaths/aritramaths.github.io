☠ The next question

A complex quotient already measures a change of scale and a turn. Can the same quotient tell us whether three points lie on a line, whether two lines meet at a right angle, or whether four points lie on a circle? We begin by deciding exactly what is meant by an angle. The distinction between a turn of an arrow and an angle between whole lines is essential.

4.1  An angle is a quotient of two displacements

Let A,B,CA,B,C be three distinct points, with complex coordinates a,b,ca,b,c. The angle at AA is made by the two rays beginning at AA: the ray towards BB and the ray towards CC. The relevant arrows are therefore AB→=b−a,AC→=c−a,\overrightarrow{AB}=b-a, \qquad \overrightarrow{AC}=c-a, not the position vectors bb and cc measured from an unrelated origin.

Translate the picture by −a-a. The point AA becomes the origin; the other two points acquire coordinates b−ab-a and c−ac-a. Translation changes neither the lengths of the arrows nor the angle between them. Now there is a unique complex multiplier that takes the first nonzero arrow to the second: q(b−a)=c−a,q=c−ab−a.q(b-a)=c-a, \qquad q=\frac{c-a}{b-a}. The modulus of qq tells us the change of length, and its direction tells us the turn: |q|=ACAB.\boxed{\left|q\right|=\frac{AC}{AB}}. If q=ρ(cosθ+isinθ)q=\rho(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta), multiplication by qq turns ABAB through θ\theta and then changes its length by the factor ρ\rho. Thus the angle calculation is not a new rule added to complex numbers. It is the original meaning of multiplication, applied to displacements instead of positions.

Definition 4.1 · Directed ray angle

The directed angle from ABAB to ACAC records a turn from the ray ABAB to the ray ACAC. Anticlockwise turns are positive; clockwise turns are negative. Two numerical turns describe the same final rays if their difference is a whole number of complete turns, that is, an integer multiple of 360∘360^\circ or 2π2\pi radians.

We retain the principal-argument convention of Lecture 2: −π<Argz≤π(z≠0).-\pi<\operatorname{Arg}z\le\pi\qquad(z\ne0). Consequently Arg((c−a)/(b−a))\operatorname{Arg}((c-a)/(b-a)) is one convenient representative of the directed ray angle. Other representatives differ from it by 2kπ2k\pi, with kk an integer. The ordinary interior angle of a triangle is different: it is the number between 00 and π\pi obtained by taking the smaller angle between the rays. For example, a principal directed angle of −45∘-45^\circ corresponds to an ordinary angle of 45∘45^\circ, not an ordinary angle of 315∘315^\circ.

Example 4.1 · The same vertex, two orders of the rays

Take a=4+3i,b=4+7i,c=8+7i.a=4+3i,\qquad b=4+7i,\qquad c=8+7i. The arrow ABAB is 4i4i, pointing vertically upward. The arrow ACAC is 4+4i4+4i, pointing northeast. To turn from ABAB to ACAC we turn clockwise through 45∘45^\circ. The algebra records exactly this: c−ab−a=4+4i4i=1−i,Arg(1−i)=−45∘.\frac{c-a}{b-a} =\frac{4+4i}{4i}=1-i, \qquad \operatorname{Arg}(1-i)=-45^\circ. Reversing the order of the rays replaces the quotient by its reciprocal: b−ac−a=4i4+4i=1+i2,Arg1+i2=45∘.\frac{b-a}{c-a} =\frac{4i}{4+4i} =\frac{1+i}{2}, \qquad \operatorname{Arg}\frac{1+i}{2}=45^\circ. The ordinary angle BACBAC is 45∘45^\circ in both descriptions. The directed angles have opposite signs because their starting and finishing rays have been exchanged.

4.2  From rays to whole lines: why a half-turn is forgotten

A ray has a starting point and a chosen direction. A whole line has no preferred direction. The arrows uu and −u-u point in opposite directions, but they lie along the same line. Replacing a direction arrow by its negative turns it through 180∘180^\circ without changing the line it describes.

For this reason, a condition involving whole lines should not depend on whether a direction arrow was chosen one way or the other. We deliberately identify numerical angles that differ by a whole number of half-turns.

Definition 4.2 · Equality of angles modulo a half-turn

For real angle measures α,β\alpha,\beta, the notation α≡β(mod180∘)\alpha\equiv\beta\pmod{180^\circ} means that α−β=k⋅180∘\alpha-\beta=k\cdot180^\circ for some integer kk. In radians the same statement is written α≡β(modπ),meaning α−β=kπ.\alpha\equiv\beta\pmod\pi, \qquad\text{meaning }\alpha-\beta=k\pi. This is an equality of angle classes, not necessarily an equality of the numerical representatives that have been written down.

For example, 20∘≡200∘≡−160∘(mod180∘),20^\circ\equiv200^\circ\equiv-160^\circ\pmod{180^\circ}, because the differences are integer multiples of 180∘180^\circ. On the other hand, 20∘20^\circ and −20∘-20^\circ are not equal modulo 180∘180^\circ: their difference is 40∘40^\circ, not a multiple of 180∘180^\circ.

⚠ What is simplified, and what is not

Working modulo π\pi removes the need to choose a positive direction along each line. It does not say that every clockwise angle equals the corresponding anticlockwise angle. In general θ\theta and −θ-\theta are different even modulo π\pi. Nor does this convention change a physical rotation: rotation by π\pi sends a nonzero arrow to its negative, although it returns its supporting line to itself.

4.2.1  The 105∘105^\circ and 75∘75^\circ distinction

This example is worth writing three different ways, because each calculation answers a different question.

  1. Compare the two directed angles. An anticlockwise turn of 105∘105^\circ is represented by +105∘+105^\circ; a clockwise turn of 75∘75^\circ is represented by −75∘-75^\circ. Their difference is 105∘−(−75∘)=180∘≡0(mod180∘).105^\circ-(-75^\circ)=180^\circ\equiv0\pmod{180^\circ}. Thus 105∘≡−75∘(mod180∘)105^\circ\equiv-75^\circ\pmod{180^\circ}. The expression that becomes zero is their difference.

  2. Perform the two rotations successively. An anticlockwise turn of 105∘105^\circ, followed by a clockwise turn of 75∘75^\circ, gives the signed total 105∘+(−75∘)=30∘.105^\circ+(-75^\circ)=30^\circ. This is a genuine 30∘30^\circ rotation. It is not zero modulo 180∘180^\circ.

  3. Add two ordinary interior angles. If the ordinary opposite angles of a cyclic quadrilateral are 105∘105^\circ and 75∘75^\circ, their sum is 105∘+75∘=180∘≡0(mod180∘).105^\circ+75^\circ=180^\circ\equiv0\pmod{180^\circ}. Neither interior angle was negative in this last calculation. Signs arise only when a direction of turning has been specified.

Before calculating, decide whether the task is to compare two angles, to compose two turns, or to add two ordinary angles. Mixing these operations is the source of the apparent contradiction.

4.2.2  Adding directed line angles

Let ℓ1,ℓ2,ℓ3\ell_1,\ell_2,\ell_3 be lines. Choose nonzero complex direction arrows u1,u2,u3u_1,u_2,u_3 along them. The directed line angle from ℓ1\ell_1 to ℓ2\ell_2 is the class of Arg(u2/u1)\operatorname{Arg}(u_2/u_1) modulo π\pi. Changing either arrow to its negative changes a representative by π\pi, so the class is independent of those choices.

The quotient identity u3u1=u3u2u2u1\frac{u_3}{u_1}=\frac{u_3}{u_2}\frac{u_2}{u_1} expresses angle addition: ∠(ℓ1,ℓ3)≡∠(ℓ1,ℓ2)+∠(ℓ2,ℓ3)(modπ).\angle(\ell_1,\ell_3) \equiv\angle(\ell_1,\ell_2)+\angle(\ell_2,\ell_3)\pmod\pi. Also ∠(ℓ2,ℓ1)≡−∠(ℓ1,ℓ2)(modπ).\angle(\ell_2,\ell_1)\equiv-\angle(\ell_1,\ell_2)\pmod\pi. The congruence sign reminds us that a principal argument might jump from one numerical representative to another, while the angle class remains unchanged.

4.3  Three collinear points

Proposition 4.3 · Collinearity criterion

For three distinct points A,B,CA,B,C, A,B,C are collinear⇔c−ab−a∈ℝ.\boxed{A,B,C\text{ are collinear}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \frac{c-a}{b-a}\in\mathbb R.} The quotient is automatically nonzero under the distinctness assumption.

Proof

By the angle rule, the argument of q=(c−a)/(b−a)q=(c-a)/(b-a) measures the turn from ABAB to ACAC. The three points are collinear exactly when these rays point in the same direction or in opposite directions. Hence A,B,C are collinear⇔Argq∈{0,π}.A,B,C\text{ are collinear} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \operatorname{Arg}q\in\{0,\pi\}. An argument of 00 means that qq is positive real; an argument of π\pi means that qq is negative real. Thus the last condition is exactly q∈ℝ\{0}q\in\mathbb R\setminus\{0\}, proving both directions.

The angle version says that the line angle is 00 modulo π\pi. Both a turn of 00 and a turn of π\pi give the same supporting line, explaining why both positive and negative real quotients are allowed.

Example 4.2 · A collinear triple not on a coordinate axis

Let a=1+ia=1+i, b=3+2ib=3+2i and c=7+4ic=7+4i. Then c−ab−a=6+3i2+i=3.\frac{c-a}{b-a}=\frac{6+3i}{2+i}=3. Thus AC=3ABAC=3AB, with both arrows pointing in the same direction. In particular A,B,CA,B,C are collinear and BB lies between AA and CC. If instead d=−3−id=-3-i, then d−ab−a=−4−2i2+i=−2.\frac{d-a}{b-a}=\frac{-4-2i}{2+i}=-2. The point DD is on the same line, two copies of ABAB away from AA in the opposite direction.

4.4  Two perpendicular lines

Proposition 4.4 · Perpendicularity criterion

Suppose A≠BA\ne B and C≠DC\ne D. Then AB⊥CD⇔b−ad−c∈iℝ\{0}.\boxed{AB\perp CD\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \frac{b-a}{d-c}\in i\mathbb R\setminus\{0\}.} Equivalently, AB⊥CD⇔Re((b−a)(d−c)¯)=0.\boxed{AB\perp CD\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \operatorname{Re}\bigl((b-a)\overline{(d-c)}\bigr)=0.} The lines may share an endpoint; only the two direction arrows must be nonzero.

Proof

By the angle rule, the argument of q=(b−a)/(d−c)q=(b-a)/(d-c) measures the turn from the direction arrow CDCD to the direction arrow ABAB. Their lines are perpendicular exactly when this turn is a quarter-turn in either direction. With our principal-argument convention, this says AB⊥CD⇔Argq∈{−π2,π2}.AB\perp CD \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \operatorname{Arg}q\in\left\{-\frac\pi2,\frac\pi2\right\}. These are precisely the arguments of the nonzero purely imaginary numbers: q=−i|q|q=-i\left|q\right| or q=i|q|q=i\left|q\right|. This proves the first equivalence. A clockwise quarter-turn may also be written as an anticlockwise turn of 3π/23\pi/2; it gives the same finishing ray.

For the second form, multiply the quotient by the conjugate of its denominator: b−ad−c=(b−a)(d−c)¯|d−c|2.\frac{b-a}{d-c} =\frac{(b-a)\overline{(d-c)}}{\left|d-c\right|^2}. The denominator is positive real. Therefore qq has real part zero exactly when (b−a)(d−c)¯(b-a)\overline{(d-c)} has real part zero. This is the second criterion.

Example 4.3 · Checking two full lines

Consider a=2−i,b=−1+2i,c=−2−i,d=1+2i.a=2-i,\quad b=-1+2i,\quad c=-2-i,\quad d=1+2i. Using the opposite orientation on both segments is harmless: a−bc−d=3−3i−3−3i=(3−3i)(−3+3i)18=i.\frac{a-b}{c-d} =\frac{3-3i}{-3-3i} =\frac{(3-3i)(-3+3i)}{18} =i. Thus the lines ABAB and CDCD are perpendicular. Notice that the quotient also has modulus 11, so these particular segments have equal lengths. Perpendicularity alone would require only a nonzero purely imaginary quotient, not necessarily ii or −i-i.

4.4.1  Where the dot product appears

Write u=x+iyu=x+iy and v=s+itv=s+it. Direct expansion gives uv‾=(x+iy)(s−it)=(xs+yt)+i(ys−xt).u\bar v=(x+iy)(s-it)=(xs+yt)+i(ys-xt). Hence Re(uv‾)=xs+yt\operatorname{Re}(u\bar v)=xs+yt, the familiar dot product of (x,y)(x,y) and (s,t)(s,t). We have not imported a new perpendicularity rule: the geometric quarter-turn criterion has generated the algebraic dot-product criterion.

For a triangle ABCABC, take u=b−au=b-a and v=c−av=c-a. Its angle at AA is right exactly when Re((b−a)(c−a)¯)=0.\operatorname{Re}\bigl((b-a)\overline{(c-a)}\bigr)=0. For instance, u=3+4iu=3+4i and v=4−3iv=4-3i satisfy uv‾=(3+4i)(4+3i)=25i.u\bar v=(3+4i)(4+3i)=25i. The real part is zero, and indeed v=−iuv=-iu is a clockwise quarter-turn of uu.

4.5  Cyclic quadrilaterals and directed angles

Definition 4.5 · Concyclic points and a cyclic quadrilateral

Points are concyclic if one circle passes through all of them. A cyclic quadrilateral has four distinct vertices on a circle, listed in their order around that circle.

4.5.1  The circle-angle fact we shall use

Fix two points A,CA,C on a circle. Points B,DB,D on the same side of the chord ACAC see equal ordinary angles. On opposite sides, the two ordinary angles add to 180∘180^\circ. This is the circle-angle theorem from Lecture 1: joining the centre to the vertices makes isosceles triangles, and shows that an angle at the circumference is half the arc it faces.

For example, if the two opposite interior angles are 105∘105^\circ and 75∘75^\circ, then 105∘+75∘=180∘.105^\circ+75^\circ=180^\circ. But the directed turns from BABA to BCBC and from DADA to DCDC have opposite signs. In the drawing below they are −105∘-105^\circ and +75∘+75^\circ. Their difference is −180∘-180^\circ, so they are equal modulo a half-turn.

We use the following standard geometric criterion, including its converse: if A,B,CA,B,C are not collinear, then A,B,C,D are concyclic⇔∠ABC≡∠ADC(modπ).\boxed{A,B,C,D\text{ are concyclic} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \angle ABC\equiv\angle ADC\pmod\pi.} In ordinary language, the angles are equal when B,DB,D are on the same side of ACAC, and supplementary when they are on opposite sides. The familiar test that a convex quadrilateral is cyclic if and only if its opposite angles add to 180∘180^\circ is the opposite-side form of this criterion. This elementary circle fact is the geometry used in the proof below.

4.6  The cross ratio: a test for one circle or one line

Definition 4.6 · Cross ratio in the chosen order

For four distinct points A,B,C,DA,B,C,D with coordinates a,b,c,da,b,c,d, put K=c−ba−bc−da−d=(c−b)(a−d)(a−b)(c−d).K=\frac{\dfrac{c-b}{a-b}}{\dfrac{c-d}{a-d}} =\frac{(c-b)(a-d)}{(a-b)(c-d)}. This is the cross ratio in the displayed order. We always display its formula because changing the order can change its value.

Theorem 4.7 · Concyclicity or collinearity criterion

Four distinct points A,B,C,DA,B,C,D lie on one circle or on one line if and only if K=(c−b)(a−d)(a−b)(c−d)∈ℝ.\boxed{K=\frac{(c-b)(a-d)}{(a-b)(c-d)}\in\mathbb R.} If A,B,CA,B,C are not collinear, the conclusion is one circle. If A,B,CA,B,C are collinear, the conclusion is one line.

Proof

Read each quotient as an angle. At BB the two arrows are a−ba-b and c−bc-b, while at DD they are a−da-d and c−dc-d. By the angle rule proved at the beginning of this lecture, Argc−ba−b≡∠ABC,Argc−da−d≡∠ADC(modπ).\operatorname{Arg}\frac{c-b}{a-b}\equiv\angle ABC, \qquad \operatorname{Arg}\frac{c-d}{a-d}\equiv\angle ADC \pmod\pi. Dividing the two quotients subtracts their angles. Therefore ArgK≡∠ABC−∠ADC(modπ).\operatorname{Arg}K\equiv\angle ABC-\angle ADC\pmod\pi. A nonzero complex number is real exactly when its argument is an integer multiple of π\pi. Hence K∈ℝ⇔∠ABC≡∠ADC(modπ).\boxed{K\in\mathbb R \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \angle ABC\equiv\angle ADC\pmod\pi.} If A,B,CA,B,C are not collinear, the circle-angle criterion just stated says exactly that DD lies on their circle. This proves both directions of the circle case.

If A,B,CA,B,C are collinear, (c−b)/(a−b)(c-b)/(a-b) is a nonzero real number. Its quotient by (c−d)/(a−d)(c-d)/(a-d) is real exactly when (c−d)/(a−d)(c-d)/(a-d) is real. By the collinearity criterion, this means that A,D,CA,D,C are collinear. Thus all four points lie on one line, as required.

⚠ Do not forget the line case

Four distinct real numbers also give a real cross ratio. To conclude that the points lie on a circle, check that one triple is not collinear. The real cross ratio then excludes every possibility except the circle.

Example 4.4 · The four fourth roots of unity

Take a=1a=1, b=ib=i, c=−1c=-1 and d=−id=-i. Then K=(−1−i)(1+i)(1−i)(−1+i)=−2i2i=−1.\begin{aligned} K &=\frac{(-1-i)(1+i)}{(1-i)(-1+i)} =\frac{-2i}{2i}=-1. \end{aligned} The cross ratio is real. Also c−ab−a=−2i−1=1+i\frac{c-a}{b-a}=\frac{-2}{i-1}=1+i is not real. Hence the four points are concyclic rather than collinear. Their known picture is the unit square on the unit circle, so the algebraic test agrees with the geometry.

Example 4.5 · A real cross ratio on a line

For a=0a=0, b=1b=1, c=2c=2, d=3d=3, K=(2−1)(0−3)(0−1)(2−3)=−3.K=\frac{(2-1)(0-3)}{(0-1)(2-3)}=-3. The number is real and negative, just as it can be for a cyclic quadrilateral. But here every point is real, so the conclusion is collinearity, not concyclicity. The sign of the cross ratio alone does not distinguish a circle from a line.

4.6.1  The order of four points on their circle

The real-cross-ratio test answers whether four points share a circle or line. Sometimes a problem asks more: the vertices must occur in a specified order around the circle, so that the quadrilateral does not cross itself. For four distinct concyclic points, in the order used in our definition, A,B,C,D occur successively around the circle⇔K<0.\boxed{A,B,C,D\text{ occur successively around the circle} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad K<0.} Either direction around the circle is allowed in this statement.

Here the sign also has a direct angle explanation. The two quotients defining KK have positive moduli, so the sign of KK is determined by the difference of their directed angles.

If B,DB,D lie on opposite sides of ACAC, write their ordinary angles as α\alpha and 180∘−α180^\circ-\alpha. The corresponding directed turns have opposite signs. Their difference is therefore +180∘+180^\circ or −180∘-180^\circ, and KK is negative. This is precisely the case in which A,B,C,DA,B,C,D follow the boundary of their circle.

If B,DB,D lie on the same side of ACAC, their directed angles are equal as numerical representatives, not just modulo a half-turn. Their difference is 00, and KK is positive. The specified list then does not give the boundary order. Thus the sign test is simply another reading of the same circle-angle picture.

Example 4.6 · When successive powers make a cyclic quadrilateral

Find all complex numbers zz such that the four points with coordinates z,z2,z3,z4z,\ z^2,\ z^3,\ z^4 are the successive vertices of a cyclic quadrilateral, in this order.

Solution

The four points must first be distinct. Thus z≠0z\ne0, and no cancellation below may be made at a value for which two of the displayed powers coincide. Their cross ratio simplifies as follows: K=(z3−z2)(z−z4)(z−z2)(z3−z4)=z2(z−1)[−z(z−1)(1+z+z2)][−z(z−1)][−z3(z−1)]=−1+z+z2z=−1−z−1z.\begin{aligned} K &=\frac{(z^3-z^2)(z-z^4)}{(z-z^2)(z^3-z^4)}\\ &=\frac{z^2(z-1)\,[-z(z-1)(1+z+z^2)]} {[-z(z-1)]\,[-z^3(z-1)]}\\ &=-\frac{1+z+z^2}{z} =-1-z-\frac1z. \end{aligned} For KK to be real we must have z+1/zz+1/z real. Equate this expression with its conjugate: z+1z=z‾+1z‾.z+\frac1z=\bar z+\frac1{\bar z}. Since z≠0z\ne0, multiplication and rearrangement give (z−z‾)(1−1|z|2)=0,or(z−z‾)(|z|2−1)=0.(z-\bar z)\left(1-\frac1{\left|z\right|^2}\right)=0, \qquad\text{or}\qquad (z-\bar z)(\left|z\right|^2-1)=0. There are two possibilities: zz is real, or |z|=1\left|z\right|=1. The real case puts all four powers on one line, so cannot give a cyclic quadrilateral with four distinct vertices. We therefore require z=cost+isint,0<t<2π,z=\cos t+i\sin t,\qquad 0<t<2\pi, with the exceptional angles that cause repeated vertices still to be excluded.

On the unit circle 1/z=z‾1/z=\bar z, so K=−1−2cost.K=-1-2\cos t. The specified circular order requires K<0K<0, and hence cost>−1/2\cos t>-1/2. In the selected full-turn interval this is exactly 0<t<2π3or4π3<t<2π.0<t<\frac{2\pi}{3} \qquad\text{or}\qquad \frac{4\pi}{3}<t<2\pi. These intervals already exclude every possible coincidence. Indeed, equality of two of the four powers would give zm=1z^m=1 for m=1,2m=1,2 or 33. In the chosen full-turn interval these forbidden angles are 00, π\pi, 2π/32\pi/3 and 4π/34\pi/3, none of which belongs to the displayed intervals. Conversely, for every angle in either interval the powers are distinct, they lie on the unit circle, and their cross ratio is negative. Therefore they have exactly the required boundary order.

The complete answer is z=cost+isint,t∈(0,2π3)∪(4π3,2π).\boxed{z=\cos t+i\sin t,\quad t\in\left(0,\frac{2\pi}{3}\right) \cup\left(\frac{4\pi}{3},2\pi\right).}

💡 The three geometric tests in one place

For nonzero direction arrows, a real quotient means the same supporting line and a purely imaginary quotient means perpendicular supporting lines. For four distinct points, a real cross ratio means one circle or one line; a noncollinearity check selects the circle case. In each test the algebra is comparing turns, and working modulo π\pi makes the comparison independent of arbitrary choices of direction along the lines.

4.7  Reversing a power: all the roots, not just one

Multiplication gave us a way to repeat a turn. Taking an nnth root asks us to reverse that repetition. There is a new feature: several different turns can lead to the same final direction after they have each been repeated nn times.

For instance, both a turn through 30∘30^\circ and a turn through 210∘210^\circ, when doubled, finish in the direction 60∘60^\circ. Their doubled angles differ by 360∘360^\circ. This is why a complex square root cannot usually mean a single point unless a convention for choosing a root has been supplied.

Definition 4.8 · nnth root of a complex number

Let nn be a positive integer. An nnth root of w∈ℂw\in\mathbb C is a complex number zz satisfying zn=wz^n=w.

The equation zn=0z^n=0 has only the solution z=0z=0, because |z|n=0\left|z\right|^n=0 forces |z|=0\left|z\right|=0. The equation z1=wz^1=w has only the solution z=wz=w. We now treat the genuinely new case: w≠0w\ne0 and n≥2n\ge2.

Choose a polar description w=r(cosθ+isinθ),r>0.w=r(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta),\qquad r>0. We may choose 0≤θ<2π0\le\theta<2\pi for convenience. The root equation concerns the point ww, however, not a particular choice of its angle.

Theorem 4.9 · The complete nnth-root formula

The nonzero number w=r(cosθ+isinθ)w=r(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta) has exactly nn distinct nnth roots. They are zk=r1/n(cosθ+2kπn+isinθ+2kπn),k=0,1,…,n−1.\boxed{z_k=r^{1/n}\left(\cos\frac{\theta+2k\pi}{n} +i\sin\frac{\theta+2k\pi}{n}\right), \quad k=0,1,\ldots,n-1.} Here r1/nr^{1/n} denotes the unique positive real nnth root of rr.

Proof

We must check three things: the displayed numbers are roots, they are different, and no root is missing.

Every displayed number works. By de Moivre's theorem, zkn=r(cos(θ+2kπ)+isin(θ+2kπ))=r(cosθ+isinθ)=w.\begin{aligned} z_k^n &=r\bigl(\cos(\theta+2k\pi)+i\sin(\theta+2k\pi)\bigr)\\ &=r(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)=w. \end{aligned} The addition of 2kπ2k\pi describes kk complete extra turns and does not change the final point.

The nn displayed roots are distinct. Suppose zj=zkz_j=z_k, where 0≤j,k≤n−10\le j,k\le n-1. Their moduli are equal already, so equality of their directions says that their angles differ by a complete number of turns: θ+2jπn−θ+2kπn=2ℓπfor some integer ℓ.\frac{\theta+2j\pi}{n}-\frac{\theta+2k\pi}{n}=2\ell\pi \quad\text{for some integer }\ell. 4.8  Roots of unity: the arithmetic of equal steps

The simplest nonzero target is 11. Its roots encode the rotations that return to the starting point after nn repetitions.

Definition 4.10 · Roots of unity

For an integer qq, therefore, εq=1⇔n divides q.\boxed{\varepsilon^q=1\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad n\text{ divides }q.} Indeed, εq\varepsilon^q has angle 2qπ/n2q\pi/n, and this is a whole number of turns exactly when q/nq/n is an integer. 4.9  Primitive roots and the first return to the starting point

The point εk\varepsilon^k may make more than one vertex-step at a time. It need not visit every vertex before returning to 11. This brings an elementary number-theoretic question into the geometry.

Definition 4.11 · Order and primitive root

If uu is a root of unity, its order is the smallest positive integer mm for which um=1u^m=1. An nnth root of unity is primitive if its order is exactly nn.

We recall the integer facts needed for the calculation. The greatest common divisor of integers k,nk,n, with n>0n>0, is the largest positive integer dividing both; it is written gcd(k,n)\gcd(k,n). The Euclidean algorithm repeatedly divides with remainder until a remainder is zero. The last nonzero remainder is the greatest common divisor. Substituting the equations backwards expresses that gcd as an integer combination of the original numbers.

For example, 12=2⋅5+2,5=2⋅2+1,12=2\cdot5+2,\qquad 5=2\cdot2+1, so 1=5−2(12−2⋅5)=5⋅5−2⋅12.1=5-2(12-2\cdot5)=5\cdot5-2\cdot12. Thus gcd(5,12)=1\gcd(5,12)=1, and the displayed identity records why no common divisor larger than 11 can occur. In general the same algorithm gives integers s,ts,t with sk+tn=gcd(k,n).sk+tn=\gcd(k,n). This is the integer Bézout identity. In particular, if gcd(k,n)=1\gcd(k,n)=1 and nn divides kmkm, then nn divides mm: multiply sk+tn=1sk+tn=1 by mm and observe that both terms on the left are divisible by nn.

Proposition 4.12 · The order of a vertex-step

Let ε=cos(2π/n)+isin(2π/n)\varepsilon=\cos(2\pi/n)+i\sin(2\pi/n) and let k∈ℤk\in\mathbb Z. Then order(εk)=ngcd(k,n).\boxed{\operatorname{order}(\varepsilon^k)=\frac{n}{\gcd(k,n)}.} Consequently εk\varepsilon^k is primitive if and only if gcd(k,n)=1\gcd(k,n)=1.

Proof

Put d=gcd(k,n)d=\gcd(k,n) and write k=dk′,n=dn′,gcd(k′,n′)=1.k=dk',\qquad n=dn',\qquad \gcd(k',n')=1. The equation (εk)m=1(\varepsilon^k)^m=1 is equivalent to n∣kmn\mid km. Cancelling dd turns this into n′∣k′m.n'\mid k'm. Since k′k' and n′n' have gcd 11, the Bézout argument just given implies n′∣mn'\mid m. Conversely, if m=n′m=n', then km=dk′n′=k′nkm=dk'n'=k'n is divisible by nn, so (εk)n′=1(\varepsilon^k)^{n'}=1.

Thus the possible positive return times are precisely the positive multiples of n′n'. The first is n′=n/dn'=n/d, as required. It equals nn exactly when d=1d=1.

4.10  Sums of roots and what they count

For n≥2n\ge2, the nn arrows from the origin to all the nnth roots of unity balance: 1+ε+ε2+⋯+εn−1=0.1+\varepsilon+\varepsilon^2+\cdots+\varepsilon^{n-1}=0. Proposition 4.13 · The roots-of-unity filter

4.11  Why the polynomial gcd stays the same

We need one polynomial fact before the last result of the lecture. The notation ℚ[X]\mathbb Q[X] means polynomials with rational coefficients; ℂ[X]\mathbb C[X] allows complex coefficients. A polynomial with rational coefficients belongs to both sets.

A polynomial is monic if its leading coefficient is 11. We choose a monic gcd so that multiplying by an arbitrary nonzero constant does not give a different answer.

Definition 4.14 · Monic polynomial gcd

The monic gcd of two nonzero polynomials is their monic common divisor of greatest degree. Every common divisor divides it.

Theorem 4.15 · The gcd does not change when the coefficient field is enlarged

If f,gf,g have rational coefficients, their monic gcd is the same whether it is calculated in ℚ[X]\mathbb Q[X] or in ℂ[X]\mathbb C[X]. The same statement holds for any two fields K⊂LK\subset L and polynomials in K[X]K[X].

Proof

Use the Euclidean algorithm, just as for integers. Divide, then divide again: f=q1g+r1,g=q2r1+r2,….f=q_1g+r_1,\qquad g=q_2r_1+r_2,\qquad\ldots. At each step the remainder has smaller degree, so the process stops. The last nonzero remainder, divided by its leading coefficient, is the monic gcd.

Why does this calculation not change over ℂ\mathbb C? Polynomial division only adds, subtracts, multiplies and divides the coefficients already present. Starting with rational coefficients, every quotient and remainder above still has rational coefficients. The same division steps are therefore valid in ℂ[X]\mathbb C[X] and give the same last nonzero remainder.

For clarity, the reason the last remainder is a gcd is the identity f=qg+r⇒common divisors of f,g=common divisors of g,r.f=qg+r \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \text{common divisors of }f,g =\text{common divisors of }g,r. Indeed, a divisor of f,gf,g divides r=f−qgr=f-qg, and a divisor of g,rg,r divides f=qg+rf=qg+r. Repeating this observation through the algorithm proves the claim in either coefficient field.

Example 4.14 · The gcd stays the same, although it may split

For f=X2+1f=X^2+1 and g=2X2+2g=2X^2+2, division gives g=2fg=2f. Thus their monic gcd is X2+1X^2+1, both over ℚ\mathbb Q and over ℂ\mathbb C. Over ℂ\mathbb C we may also write X2+1=(X−i)(X+i).X^2+1=(X-i)(X+i). The gcd has not changed; it has merely been written as a product of smaller factors.

💡 The consequence we need

If two rational-coefficient polynomials share a complex root α\alpha, then X−αX-\alpha divides both over ℂ\mathbb C. Their gcd over ℂ\mathbb C is therefore not constant. Since the gcd is unchanged, their gcd over ℚ\mathbb Q is not constant either.

This does not mean that X−αX-\alpha itself has rational coefficients. It means that some nonconstant polynomial with rational coefficients divides both.

4.12  The prime-polygon relation

Let pp be prime and put ε=cos2πp+isin2πp,Φp(X)=1+X+⋯+Xp−1.\varepsilon=\cos\frac{2\pi}{p}+i\sin\frac{2\pi}{p}, \qquad \Phi_p(X)=1+X+\cdots+X^{p-1}. The geometric-sum formula gives Φp(ε)=0\Phi_p(\varepsilon)=0.

Definition 4.16 · Irreducibility over a field

A nonconstant polynomial is irreducible over ℚ\mathbb Q if it cannot be written as a product of two lower-degree nonconstant polynomials with rational coefficients. The same definition applies with another coefficient field in place of ℚ\mathbb Q.

We use the following standard polynomial criterion. Stating it explicitly makes the irreducibility test easy to check.

Lemma 4.17 · Eisenstein's criterion, monic form

Let FF be a monic polynomial with integer coefficients. If a prime pp divides every coefficient except the leading one, and p2p^2 does not divide the constant coefficient, then FF is irreducible over ℚ\mathbb Q.

Proposition 4.18 · Irreducibility of the prime-root polynomial

For prime pp, the polynomial Φp(X)=1+X+⋯+Xp−1\Phi_p(X)=1+X+\cdots+X^{p-1} is irreducible over ℚ\mathbb Q.

Proof

The useful trick is to replace XX by X+1X+1. Since XΦp(X+1)=(X+1)p−1,X\Phi_p(X+1)=(X+1)^p-1, the binomial theorem gives Φp(X+1)=∑j=1p(pj)Xj−1.\Phi_p(X+1)=\sum_{j=1}^{p}\binom pjX^{j-1}. Its leading coefficient is 11, and its constant coefficient is pp. Every other coefficient is divisible by pp: for 1≤j<p1\le j<p, j!(pj)=p(p−1)⋯(p−j+1),j!\binom pj=p(p-1)\cdots(p-j+1), where pp divides the right-hand side but does not divide j!j!. Hence p∣(pj)p\mid\binom pj.

Eisenstein's criterion now applies: pp divides all nonleading coefficients, but p2p^2 does not divide the constant coefficient pp. Thus Φp(X+1)\Phi_p(X+1) is irreducible.

Finally, a factorisation Φp(X)=A(X)B(X)\Phi_p(X)=A(X)B(X) would give Φp(X+1)=A(X+1)B(X+1)\Phi_p(X+1)=A(X+1)B(X+1) with the same factor degrees. It cannot exist. Therefore Φp(X)\Phi_p(X) is irreducible as well. For p=2p=2, the shifted polynomial is simply X+2X+2.

Theorem 4.19 · Integer-coefficient rigidity for a prime root

Let pp be prime, let ε=cos(2π/p)+isin(2π/p)\varepsilon=\cos(2\pi/p)+i\sin(2\pi/p), and let a0,…,ap−1a_0,\ldots,a_{p-1} be integers. Then a0+a1ε+⋯+ap−1εp−1=0⇔a0=a1=⋯=ap−1.\boxed{a_0+a_1\varepsilon+\cdots+a_{p-1}\varepsilon^{p-1}=0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad a_0=a_1=\cdots=a_{p-1}.} Zero coefficients are allowed. The statement also holds for rational coefficients.

Proof

If all coefficients equal aa, the sum is a(1+ε+⋯+εp−1)=0.a(1+\varepsilon+\cdots+\varepsilon^{p-1})=0.

Conversely, write f(X)=a0+a1X+⋯+ap−1Xp−1,g(X)=1+X+⋯+Xp−1.f(X)=a_0+a_1X+\cdots+a_{p-1}X^{p-1}, \qquad g(X)=1+X+\cdots+X^{p-1}. If f=0f=0, all coefficients are already equal to zero. Otherwise f(ε)=g(ε)=0f(\varepsilon)=g(\varepsilon)=0. Thus X−εX-\varepsilon is a common factor over ℂ\mathbb C, so the gcd is nonconstant. By the preceding theorem the gcd over ℚ\mathbb Q is also nonconstant.

This gcd divides gg. Since gg is irreducible over ℚ\mathbb Q, its only monic nonconstant divisor is gg itself. Therefore g∣fg\mid f. But degf≤p−1=degg,\deg f\le p-1=\deg g, so f=cgf=cg for a constant cc. Comparing coefficients gives a0=a1=⋯=ap−1=c,a_0=a_1=\cdots=a_{p-1}=c, as required.

Example 4.15 · Why the lemma is useful in combinatorics

Suppose a collection of objects has integer labels. Let aja_j count the labels with remainder jj after division by a prime pp. If ∑objectsεlabel=0,\sum_{\text{objects}}\varepsilon^{\text{label}}=0, group the terms according to those remainders: a0+a1ε+⋯+ap−1εp−1=0.a_0+a_1\varepsilon+\cdots+a_{p-1}\varepsilon^{p-1}=0. The theorem gives a0=⋯=ap−1a_0=\cdots=a_{p-1}. Thus all pp remainder classes have the same size. For example, with p=5p=5 and 3535 objects, each class contains 77 objects. A complex identity has proved an exact counting statement.

⚠ The prime assumption matters

For the fourth root ii, 1+0i+1i2+0i3=0,1+0i+1i^2+0i^3=0, but 1,0,1,01,0,1,0 are not all equal. Here 1+X+X2+X3=(1+X)(1+X2)1+X+X^2+X^3=(1+X)(1+X^2) is reducible. This is precisely where the prime case differs.

💡 The geometric idea to retain

Angles turn quotients into geometry; roots of unity turn regular polygons into algebra. For a prime polygon, a rational linear relation among its vertices can vanish only when all its coefficients are equal.

4.13  Further reading: similar triangles

This section can be read independently after the discussion of roots and roots of unity.

The original spiral-similarity viewpoint also gives a concise comparison of two triangles. Let ABCABC and PQRPQR be nondegenerate triangles, with the indicated correspondence A↔P,B↔Q,C↔R.A\longleftrightarrow P,\quad B\longleftrightarrow Q,\quad C\longleftrightarrow R. Move the first vertex of each triangle to 00 and its second vertex to 11. The normalized coordinates of their third vertices are c−ab−a,r−pq−p.\frac{c-a}{b-a},\qquad \frac{r-p}{q-p}. These numbers retain both the shape and the choice of side of the first edge on which the third vertex lies.

Proposition 4.20 · Similarity with and without reflection

The two triangles are similar with the same orientation if and only if c−ab−a=r−pq−p.\boxed{\frac{c-a}{b-a}=\frac{r-p}{q-p}.} They are similar with opposite orientations if and only if c−ab−a=(r−pq−p)¯.\boxed{\frac{c-a}{b-a}=\overline{\left(\frac{r-p}{q-p}\right)}.}

Proof

A direct similarity has the form z↦λz+μz\mapsto\lambda z+\mu, with λ≠0\lambda\ne0. If it sends AA to PP and BB to QQ, subtraction determines λ=q−pb−a,μ=p−λa.\lambda=\frac{q-p}{b-a},\qquad \mu=p-\lambda a. It sends CC to RR exactly when r−p=λ(c−a)=q−pb−a(c−a),r-p=\lambda(c-a) =\frac{q-p}{b-a}(c-a), which is the first displayed quotient equality. Conversely, that equality makes the explicitly constructed direct similarity send all three corresponding vertices to one another.

Complex conjugation reflects the plane in the real axis and reverses orientation. Reflecting PQRPQR gives the coordinates p‾,q‾,r‾\bar p,\bar q,\bar r. Applying the already proved direct-similarity criterion to ABCABC and this reflected triangle gives c−ab−a=r‾−p‾q‾−p‾=(r−pq−p)¯,\frac{c-a}{b-a} =\frac{\bar r-\bar p}{\bar q-\bar p} =\overline{\left(\frac{r-p}{q-p}\right)}, as required.

Example 4.16 · A shared centroid from three similar constructions

On the three successive sides AB,BC,CAAB,BC,CA construct triangles ADBADB, BECBEC, CFACFA which are similar with the same orientation and with the displayed vertex correspondences. Then d−ab−a=e−bc−b=f−ca−c=t\frac{d-a}{b-a}=\frac{e-b}{c-b}=\frac{f-c}{a-c}=t for a fixed complex number tt. Solve for the three new vertices: d=a+t(b−a),e=b+t(c−b),f=c+t(a−c).d=a+t(b-a),\quad e=b+t(c-b),\quad f=c+t(a-c). Adding the equations gives d+e+f=a+b+c+t((b−a)+(c−b)+(a−c))=a+b+c.\begin{aligned} d+e+f &=a+b+c+t\bigl((b-a)+(c-b)+(a-c)\bigr)\\ &=a+b+c. \end{aligned} The displacement sum around a closed triangle is zero. Divide by 33 to conclude d+e+f3=a+b+c3.\boxed{\frac{d+e+f}{3}=\frac{a+b+c}{3}}. Thus DEFDEF and ABCABC have the same centroid. The cancellation has a geometric reason: the same scale-and-turn instruction was applied to the three arrows of a closed route. If the constructed triple D,E,FD,E,F happens to be collinear or coincident, its point-average (d+e+f)/3(d+e+f)/3 is still defined and is still the centroid of ABCABC. The calculation does not require DEFDEF to be a nondegenerate triangle.
