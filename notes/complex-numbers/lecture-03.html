💡 The purpose of a complex calculation

Before expanding an expression, decide what it should say geometrically. A difference represents an arrow; a quotient represents a turn and a scale; an average represents a midpoint or a balance point. The calculation should preserve that meaning from beginning to end.

3.1  A chord of the unit circle becomes a mirror

Let AA and BB be distinct points on the unit circle centred at 00. Their complex coordinates satisfy |a|=|b|=1\left|a\right|=\left|b\right|=1, so aa‾=bb‾=1,a‾=1a,b‾=1b.a\bar a=b\bar b=1,\qquad \bar a=\frac1a,\quad\bar b=\frac1b. We learned in Lecture 2 to straighten the line ABAB by sending aa to 00 and bb to 11, conjugate, and then undo that change. For any point ZZ, the coordinate of its reflection Z′Z' is therefore z′=a+(b−a)(z−ab−a)¯=a+b−ab‾−a‾(z‾−a‾).z'=a+(b-a)\overline{\left(\frac{z-a}{b-a}\right)} =a+\frac{b-a}{\bar b-\bar a}(\bar z-\bar a). The circle assumption simplifies the coefficient: since b‾−a‾=1b−1a=a−bab=−b−aab,\bar b-\bar a=\frac1b-\frac1a=\frac{a-b}{ab} =-\frac{b-a}{ab}, we obtain (b−a)/(b‾−a‾)=−ab(b-a)/(\bar b-\bar a)=-ab. Substitute a‾=1/a\bar a=1/a and distribute: z′=a+b−abz‾.\boxed{z'=a+b-ab\bar z.} This formula describes reflection in the entire line ABAB, even if ZZ is outside the circle. Its perpendicular foot on the line is f=z+z′2=z+a+b−abz‾2.\boxed{f=\frac{z+z'}2=\frac{z+a+b-ab\bar z}{2}.} The first box is a reflection, an operation supplied by geometry; the second is an average, an operation supplied by translation. Together they calculate a perpendicular projection.

Example 3.1 · The unit-circle chord reflection

Take a=cos(−60∘)+isin(−60∘)a=\cos(-60^\circ)+i\sin(-60^\circ), b=cos60∘+isin60∘b=\cos60^\circ+i\sin60^\circ, and z=1z=1. Calculate the reflection and the foot without guessing them from the picture.

Solution

The two endpoints are a=12−32ia=\tfrac12-\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}i and b=12+32ib=\tfrac12+\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}i. Consequently a+b=1a+b=1 and ab=|a|2=1ab=\left|a\right|^2=1. Since z‾=1\bar z=1, z′=a+b−abz‾=1−1=0,f=z+z′2=12.z'=a+b-ab\bar z=1-1=0,\qquad f=\frac{z+z'}2=\frac12. This agrees with the line x=1/2x=1/2 and the horizontal perpendicular from z=1z=1.

3.2  Midpoints, perpendicular arrows and orientation

If MM is the midpoint of ABAB, the arrow from AA to MM is half the arrow from AA to BB. Therefore m=a+12(b−a)=a+b2.m=a+\frac12(b-a)=\frac{a+b}{2}. The formula is an average of positions, but its justification is a statement about displacements. More generally, a+t(b−a)a+t(b-a) moves a fraction tt of the way from AA to BB when 0≤t≤10\le t\le1.

For nonzero arrows u,vu,v, perpendicularity means that u/vu/v is purely imaginary. Since uv=uv‾|v|2,\frac uv=\frac{u\bar v}{\left|v\right|^2}, and the denominator is positive real, this is equivalent to Re(uv‾)=0.\boxed{\operatorname{Re}(u\bar v)=0.} If u=x+iyu=x+iy and v=s+itv=s+it, expanding shows that Re(uv‾)=xs+yt\operatorname{Re}(u\bar v)=xs+yt. Thus the familiar real coordinate test for perpendicularity is contained in complex multiplication.

We must also decide on orientation before constructing figures externally. If the vertices of a convex polygon are listed anticlockwise, its interior lies on the left as we traverse each side; its exterior is on the right. In clockwise order, the interior is on the right and the exterior on the left. Multiplication of a side arrow by ii points to its left, while multiplication by −i-i points to its right.

3.3  A square centre obtained in two ways

Let A,BA,B be the endpoints of a side of a square constructed on the left of the direction A→BA\to B. Let its centre be SS, with coordinate ss.

3.3.1  First calculation: rotate about the unknown centre

The radii SA,SBSA,SB have equal length, and the turn from SASA to SBSB is 90∘90^\circ anticlockwise. Hence b−s=i(a−s).b-s=i(a-s). Now solve for the unknown point: b−s=ia−is,(i−1)s=ia−b,s=ia−bi−1.\begin{aligned} b-s&=ia-is,\\ (i-1)s&=ia-b,\\ s&=\frac{ia-b}{i-1}. \end{aligned} Multiply numerator and denominator by i+1i+1: s=(ia−b)(i+1)(i−1)(i+1)=i2a+ia−ib−bi2−1=−a+ia−ib−b−2=a+b+i(b−a)2.\begin{aligned} s&=\frac{(ia-b)(i+1)}{(i-1)(i+1)}\\ &=\frac{i^2a+ia-ib-b}{i^2-1}\\ &=\frac{-a+ia-ib-b}{-2}\\ &=\boxed{\frac{a+b+i(b-a)}{2}.} \end{aligned} The final expression is the midpoint of ABAB plus one half of the leftward perpendicular side arrow. Both terms are visible in the square.

3.3.2  Second calculation: construct the opposite vertex

Rotate AA clockwise by 90∘90^\circ about BB. The image is the opposite vertex MM: m=b−i(a−b)=b+i(b−a).m=b-i(a-b)=b+i(b-a). The centre is the midpoint of the diagonal AMAM, so s=a+m2=a+b+i(b−a)2.s=\frac{a+m}{2}=\frac{a+b+i(b-a)}2. This recovers the same formula from a different geometric construction. For a square on the right of A→BA\to B, the perpendicular displacement changes sign: sleft=a+b+i(b−a)2,sright=a+b−i(b−a)2.\boxed{s_{\mathrm{left}}=\frac{a+b+i(b-a)}2,\qquad s_{\mathrm{right}}=\frac{a+b-i(b-a)}2.}

Example 3.2 · Two possible square centres

For a=1+ia=1+i and b=3+3ib=3+3i, the midpoint is 2+2i2+2i and i(b−a)/2=i(1+i)=−1+ii(b-a)/2=i(1+i)=-1+i. The left centre is 1+3i1+3i, and the right centre is 3+i3+i. Their midpoint is again 2+2i2+2i, and they are reflected across line ABAB.

3.4  Four external squares on a quadrilateral

Theorem 3.1

Construct squares externally on the four sides of a convex quadrilateral. The segments joining the centres of opposite squares have equal lengths and are perpendicular.

Proof

List the vertices A,B,C,DA,B,C,D in clockwise order. Then every exterior square is on the left of its directed side. If their centres are S1,S2,S3,S4S_1,S_2,S_3,S_4, the formula just proved gives 2s1=a+b+i(b−a)=(1−i)a+(1+i)b,2s2=b+c+i(c−b)=(1−i)b+(1+i)c,2s3=c+d+i(d−c)=(1−i)c+(1+i)d,2s4=d+a+i(a−d)=(1−i)d+(1+i)a.\begin{aligned} 2s_1&=a+b+i(b-a)=(1-i)a+(1+i)b,\\ 2s_2&=b+c+i(c-b)=(1-i)b+(1+i)c,\\ 2s_3&=c+d+i(d-c)=(1-i)c+(1+i)d,\\ 2s_4&=d+a+i(a-d)=(1-i)d+(1+i)a. \end{aligned} Put X=a−cX=a-c and Y=b−dY=b-d. Subtract opposite centres: 2(s1−s3)=(1−i)(a−c)+(1+i)(b−d)=(1−i)X+(1+i)Y,2(s2−s4)=(1−i)(b−d)+(1+i)(c−a)=−(1+i)X+(1−i)Y.\begin{aligned} 2(s_1-s_3)&=(1-i)(a-c)+(1+i)(b-d)\\ &=(1-i)X+(1+i)Y,\\ 2(s_2-s_4)&=(1-i)(b-d)+(1+i)(c-a)\\ &=-(1+i)X+(1-i)Y. \end{aligned} Multiply the second expression by ii and simplify each coefficient: 2i(s2−s4)=−i(1+i)X+i(1−i)Y=(1−i)X+(1+i)Y=2(s1−s3).\begin{aligned} 2i(s_2-s_4)&=-i(1+i)X+i(1-i)Y\\ &=(1-i)X+(1+i)Y\\ &=2(s_1-s_3). \end{aligned} Therefore s1−s3=i(s2−s4).\boxed{s_1-s_3=i(s_2-s_4).} The arrow from S3S_3 to S1S_1 is an anticlockwise quarter-turn of the arrow from S4S_4 to S2S_2. A quarter-turn preserves length and makes the directions perpendicular, giving both required conclusions.

The two arrows do not vanish. Indeed, s1=s3s_1=s_3 would give Y=iXY=iX. The diagonals of a convex quadrilateral meet at an interior point TT. For clockwise order, the ray TBTB is reached from TATA by a clockwise turn strictly between 0∘0^\circ and 180∘180^\circ. The arrow C→AC\to A has the direction TATA, and D→BD\to B has the direction TBTB. Thus YY cannot be a 90∘90^\circ anticlockwise turn of XX. This rules out the zero case.

The proof illustrates a useful choice of target. Proving a quotient is purely imaginary would establish perpendicularity, but not yet equal length. Proving directly that one displacement is ii times the other establishes both at once and avoids dividing by an expression whose nonzero value would first need justification.

3.5  A 15∘15^\circ–30∘30^\circ–45∘45^\circ construction

Let ABCABC be an anticlockwise triangle. Construct triangles externally on its sides as follows: ∠PBC=45∘,∠BCP=30∘;∠CAQ=45∘,∠QCA=30∘;\angle PBC=45^\circ,\quad\angle BCP=30^\circ; \qquad \angle CAQ=45^\circ,\quad\angle QCA=30^\circ; ∠RAB=∠ABR=15∘.\angle RAB=\angle ABR=15^\circ. We will prove RP=RQRP=RQ and RP⊥RQRP\perp RQ. The geometry will first produce formulas for p,q,rp,q,r, after which a quarter-turn identity will finish the proof.

3.5.1  Find pp by introducing the perpendicular foot

Drop the perpendicular from PP to BCBC, meeting it at MM. Both base angles are acute, so MM lies between BB and CC. The right triangle BMPBMP has a 45∘45^\circ angle, hence BM=MPBM=MP. In the right triangle CMPCMP, tan30∘=MPMC=13,MC=3MP.\tan30^\circ=\frac{MP}{MC}=\frac1{\sqrt3},\qquad MC=\sqrt3\,MP. Therefore BC=(1+3)MPBC=(1+\sqrt3)MP. This is the metric information in the drawing.

Now obtain PP in two ways about MM. The arrow MBMB points left; rotating it anticlockwise through 90∘90^\circ points down to PP, with the same length. Thus p=m+i(b−m)=ib+(1−i)m,m=p−ib1−i.p=m+i(b-m)=ib+(1-i)m, \qquad m=\frac{p-ib}{1-i}. The arrow MCMC points right; rotate it clockwise through 90∘90^\circ and contract it by 1/31/\sqrt3. This also gives MPMP: p=m−i3(c−m),m=3p+ic3+i.p=m-\frac{i}{\sqrt3}(c-m), \qquad m=\frac{\sqrt3p+ic}{\sqrt3+i}. Equate the two expressions for the same foot MM and cross-multiply: p−ib1−i=3p+ic3+i,(p−ib)(3+i)=(3p+ic)(1−i),3p+ip−i3b+b=3p−i3p+ic+c,i(1+3)p=i3b+ic+c−b.\begin{aligned} \frac{p-ib}{1-i}&=\frac{\sqrt3p+ic}{\sqrt3+i},\\ (p-ib)(\sqrt3+i)&=(\sqrt3p+ic)(1-i),\\ \sqrt3p+ip-i\sqrt3b+b&=\sqrt3p-i\sqrt3p+ic+c,\\ i(1+\sqrt3)p&=i\sqrt3b+ic+c-b. \end{aligned} Divide by ii, remembering that 1/i=−i1/i=-i: (1+3)p=3b+c+i(b−c).(1+\sqrt3)p=\sqrt3b+c+i(b-c). Hence p=c+3b+i(b−c)1+3=b+1−i1+3(c−b).\boxed{p=\frac{c+\sqrt3b+i(b-c)}{1+\sqrt3} =b+\frac{1-i}{1+\sqrt3}(c-b).} The second form says: travel along a fraction of BCBC, then make a perpendicular displacement of the same length towards the exterior.

3.5.2  Find qq and keep track of the opposite side

Let NN be the perpendicular foot from QQ to ACAC. The angle at AA is 45∘45^\circ, so AN=NQAN=NQ; the angle at CC is 30∘30^\circ, so NC=3NQNC=\sqrt3NQ. The exterior is now on the left of A→CA\to C.

About NN, rotate the arrow NANA clockwise to obtain NQNQ. Alternatively rotate NCNC anticlockwise and contract by 1/31/\sqrt3: q=n−i(a−n),q=n+i3(c−n).q=n-i(a-n),\qquad q=n+\frac{i}{\sqrt3}(c-n). Solve each for nn: n=q+ia1+i=3q−ic3−i.n=\frac{q+ia}{1+i}=\frac{\sqrt3q-ic}{\sqrt3-i}. Cross-multiplication gives (q+ia)(3−i)=(3q−ic)(1+i),3q−iq+i3a+a=3q+i3q−ic+c,−i(1+3)q=c−a−i3a−ic.\begin{aligned} (q+ia)(\sqrt3-i)&=(\sqrt3q-ic)(1+i),\\ \sqrt3q-iq+i\sqrt3a+a&=\sqrt3q+i\sqrt3q-ic+c,\\ -i(1+\sqrt3)q&=c-a-i\sqrt3a-ic. \end{aligned} Multiplying by ii yields q=c+3a+i(c−a)1+3=a+1+i1+3(c−a).\boxed{q=\frac{c+\sqrt3a+i(c-a)}{1+\sqrt3} =a+\frac{1+i}{1+\sqrt3}(c-a).} The sign of the perpendicular term is determined by the exterior side of the segment. It should be checked from the drawing, not copied from the formula for pp.

3.5.3  Find rr from the midpoint and the height

Let LL be the midpoint of ABAB. Since ABRABR has equal base angles, RA=RBRA=RB, and its altitude from RR passes through LL. The right triangle ALRALR gives LR=ALtan15∘=AB2tan15∘.LR=AL\tan15^\circ=\frac{AB}{2}\tan15^\circ. To compute the tangent, use 15∘=45∘−30∘15^\circ=45^\circ-30^\circ. The angle-addition formulas from Lecture 2 give tan15∘=1−1/31+1/3=3−13+1=(3−1)23−1=2−3.\tan15^\circ=\frac{1-1/\sqrt3}{1+1/\sqrt3} =\frac{\sqrt3-1}{\sqrt3+1} =\frac{(\sqrt3-1)^2}{3-1}=2-\sqrt3. The exterior is to the right of A→BA\to B, so the perpendicular arrow from LL to RR is −i(b−a)(2−3)/2-i(b-a)(2-\sqrt3)/2. Therefore r=a+b2−i(2−3)2(b−a).\boxed{r=\frac{a+b}{2}-\frac{i(2-\sqrt3)}2(b-a).} Equivalently, the turn from RARA to RBRB is 150∘150^\circ clockwise. Thus b−r=(cos(−150∘)+isin(−150∘))(a−r);b-r=\bigl(\cos(-150^\circ)+i\sin(-150^\circ)\bigr)(a-r); solving this equation gives the same coordinate.

3.5.4  The final quarter-turn identity

Put t=3t=\sqrt3 and k=1/(1+t)=(t−1)/2k=1/(1+t)=(t-1)/2. The formulas for p,qp,q become p=(1−k+ik)b+(k−ik)c,q=(1−k−ik)a+(k+ik)c.p=(1-k+ik)b+(k-ik)c,\qquad q=(1-k-ik)a+(k+ik)c. Multiply pp by ii: ip=(−k+i−ik)b+(k+ik)c.ip=(-k+i-ik)b+(k+ik)c. Subtract. The cc terms cancel, leaving q−ip=(1−k−ik)a+(k−i+ik)b.q-ip=(1-k-ik)a+(k-i+ik)b. On the other hand, r=1+i(2−t)2a+1−i(2−t)2b.r=\frac{1+i(2-t)}2a+\frac{1-i(2-t)}2b. Multiplication by 1−i1-i gives (1−i)r=3−t+i(1−t)2a+t−1−i(3−t)2b=(1−k−ik)a+(k−i+ik)b.\begin{aligned} (1-i)r &=\frac{3-t+i(1-t)}2a+\frac{t-1-i(3-t)}2b\\ &=(1-k-ik)a+(k-i+ik)b. \end{aligned} Thus q−ip=(1−i)rq-ip=(1-i)r. Rearranging, q−r=i(p−r).\boxed{q-r=i(p-r).} The arrow RQRQ is an anticlockwise quarter-turn of RPRP. Hence RQ=RPRQ=RP and the two segments are perpendicular.

For completeness, the segments are nonzero. Translate, rotate and scale so that b=0b=0, c=1c=1; then aa lies above the real axis. If p=rp=r, the identity would give q=pq=p. But p=k−ikp=k-ik and q=(1−k−ik)a+k+ikq=(1-k-ik)a+k+ik would then imply a=−2ik1−k−ik=2k2−2ik(1−k)(1−k)2+k2,a=\frac{-2ik}{1-k-ik} =\frac{2k^2-2ik(1-k)}{(1-k)^2+k^2}, whose imaginary part is negative because 0<k<10<k<1. This contradicts the position of AA. Thus the identity describes a genuine right isosceles triangle.

3.6  Equilateral triangles and cube roots of unity

Let ω\omega be the unit point reached by turning the horizontal unit arrow anticlockwise through 120∘120^\circ. A 30∘30^\circ–60∘60^\circ–90∘90^\circ triangle gives its horizontal and vertical components: ω=cos120∘+isin120∘=−12+32i.\omega=\cos120^\circ+i\sin120^\circ =-\frac12+\frac{\sqrt3}{2}i. Two successive multiplications by ω\omega make a 240∘240^\circ turn, and three make one complete turn. The coordinate multiplication from Lecture 2 checks each step without assuming a general theorem about roots: ω2=(−12+32i)2=−12−32i,ω3=(−12−32i)(−12+32i)=1.\begin{aligned} \omega^2&=\left(-\frac12+\frac{\sqrt3}{2}i\right)^2 =-\frac12-\frac{\sqrt3}{2}i,\\ \omega^3&=\left(-\frac12-\frac{\sqrt3}{2}i\right) \left(-\frac12+\frac{\sqrt3}{2}i\right)=1. \end{aligned} Adding the three explicitly known points gives 1+ω+ω2=01+\omega+\omega^2=0. These are the three points on the unit circle obtained from 11 by successive 120∘120^\circ turns. We will call them cube roots of unity because each has cube 11; a continuation of the course develops the corresponding method for finding all roots of a general number.

The points z,zω,zω2z,z\omega,z\omega^2 for z≠0z\ne0 lie on one circle, spaced by 120∘120^\circ. The three chords have equal lengths, since rotating the circle through 120∘120^\circ sends each chord to the next. They therefore form an equilateral triangle centred at the origin. Their sum is z+zω+zω2=z(1+ω+ω2)=0.z+z\omega+z\omega^2=z(1+\omega+\omega^2)=0.

3.6.1  Two possible third vertices on a given side

For distinct A,BA,B, an equilateral vertex CC is obtained by rotating ABAB through 60∘60^\circ in either direction about AA. Thus c=a+(12±32i)(b−a)=a+b2±32i(b−a).\boxed{c=a+\left(\frac12\pm\frac{\sqrt3}{2}i\right)(b-a) =\frac{a+b}{2}\pm\frac{\sqrt3}{2}i(b-a).} The plus sign places CC on the left of A→BA\to B; the minus sign places it on the right.

Theorem 3.2

Three distinct points with coordinates a,b,ca,b,c form an equilateral triangle exactly when a2+b2+c2=ab+bc+ca.\boxed{a^2+b^2+c^2=ab+bc+ca.}

Proof

Use the displacement b−ab-a as the unit arrow. In other words, put t=c−ab−a,c=a+t(b−a).t=\frac{c-a}{b-a},\qquad c=a+t(b-a). Write d=b−ad=b-a, so b=a+db=a+d and c=a+tdc=a+td. Expanding gives a2+b2+c2−ab−bc−ca=a2+(a+d)2+(a+td)2−a(a+d)−(a+d)(a+td)−(a+td)a=d2(1+t2−t).\begin{aligned} a^2+b^2+c^2-ab-bc-ca &=a^2+(a+d)^2+(a+td)^2\\ &\quad-a(a+d)-(a+d)(a+td)-(a+td)a\\ &=d^2(1+t^2-t). \end{aligned} All terms involving aa cancel: the condition depends on the shape, not the position. Since d≠0d\ne0, the expression vanishes exactly when t2−t+1=0t^2-t+1=0. Its two solutions are t=1±i32.t=\frac{1\pm i\sqrt3}{2}. These are exactly the two 60∘60^\circ rotations just obtained geometrically.

The equation uses complex squares, not squared distances. Replacing a2a^2 by |a|2\left|a\right|^2 would give an entirely different statement.

3.6.2  Factoring the criterion

Expand the product (a+ωb+ω2c)(a+ω2b+ωc).(a+\omega b+\omega^2c)(a+\omega^2b+\omega c). The coefficients of a2,b2,c2a^2,b^2,c^2 are 1,ω3,ω31,\omega^3,\omega^3, all equal to 11. The coefficient of abab is ω+ω2=−1\omega+\omega^2=-1; the coefficient of acac is also −1-1; the coefficient of bcbc is ω2+ω4=ω2+ω=−1\omega^2+\omega^4=\omega^2+\omega=-1. Thus a2+b2+c2−ab−bc−ca=(a+ωb+ω2c)(a+ω2b+ωc).\boxed{a^2+b^2+c^2-ab-bc-ca =(a+\omega b+\omega^2c)(a+\omega^2b+\omega c).} Either linear factor being zero proves the equilateral condition. The two factors distinguish the two orientations. Together with the usual sum-of-cubes factorisation, this gives a3+b3+c3−3abc=(a+b+c)(a+ωb+ω2c)(a+ω2b+ωc).a^3+b^3+c^3-3abc =(a+b+c)(a+\omega b+\omega^2c)(a+\omega^2b+\omega c). The factors have geometric meanings: the first records a centroid at the origin, and the other two record equilateral shape.

3.7  When does zero sum imply equilateral?

The condition a+b+c=0a+b+c=0 alone is not enough. For example, 1,2i,−1−2i1,2i,-1-2i sum to zero, but their mutual distances are 5,17,8\sqrt5,\sqrt{17},\sqrt8, which are unequal. The missing condition is that the origin be equidistant from the vertices.

Proposition 3.3

If |a|=|b|=|c|=R>0\left|a\right|=\left|b\right|=\left|c\right|=R>0 and a+b+c=0a+b+c=0, then A,B,CA,B,C form an equilateral triangle of side length 3R\sqrt3R.

Proof

From a+b=−ca+b=-c, R2=|a+b|2=|a|2+|b|2+2Re(ab‾)=2R2+2Re(ab‾).\begin{aligned} R^2&=\left|a+b\right|^2\\ &=\left|a\right|^2+\left|b\right|^2+2\operatorname{Re}(a\bar b)\\ &=2R^2+2\operatorname{Re}(a\bar b). \end{aligned} Hence Re(ab‾)=−R2/2\operatorname{Re}(a\bar b)=-R^2/2. Therefore |a−b|2=|a|2+|b|2−2Re(ab‾)=2R2+R2=3R2.\begin{aligned} \left|a-b\right|^2 &=\left|a\right|^2+\left|b\right|^2-2\operatorname{Re}(a\bar b)\\ &=2R^2+R^2=3R^2. \end{aligned} The same calculation using b+c=−ab+c=-a and c+a=−bc+a=-b gives |b−c|2=|c−a|2=3R2\left|b-c\right|^2=\left|c-a\right|^2=3R^2. All three sides have length 3R>0\sqrt3R>0.

3.8  Three triangle centres and Euler's line

We first define the three centres involved.

The circumcentre OO is the point equidistant from the three vertices. It is obtained by intersecting the perpendicular bisectors of two sides: a point on the first is equally far from its endpoints, and being on the second makes the third distance equal as well. The circle with this centre through the vertices is the circumcircle.

A median joins a vertex to the midpoint of its opposite side. The centroid GG is their common point. To see both the concurrence and its coordinate, let m=(b+c)/2m=(b+c)/2 be the midpoint opposite AA and set g=a+b+c3.g=\frac{a+b+c}{3}. Then g−a=b+c−2a3=23(m−a).g-a=\frac{b+c-2a}{3}=\frac23(m-a). Thus GG lies two thirds of the way along the median from AA. The same expression works for the other two medians, proving that they concur and that AG:GM=2:1AG:GM=2:1.

An altitude is the line through a vertex perpendicular to the opposite side. Their common point is the orthocentre HH. Unlike the centroid, the circumcentre and orthocentre need not lie inside the triangle. Our next calculation proves that the three altitudes have a common point and finds it.

3.9  Napoleon's theorem

Theorem 3.4 · Napoleon's theorem

Construct equilateral triangles externally on the sides of any triangle. The centroids of the three new triangles form an equilateral triangle.

3.10  A further calculation: why the number three is special

Suppose regular nn-gons, with n≥3n\ge3, are constructed externally on the sides of a fixed nonequilateral triangle. For which nn do their centres form an equilateral triangle?

💡 The geometric idea to retain

A construction gives an equation before it gives a formula. The square-centre and mixed-angle problems become quarter-turn identities; the equilateral and Napoleon problems become identities involving cube roots of unity; the Euler line becomes a relation among sums of vertex coordinates.

3.11  Repeated multiplication and de Moivre's theorem

The definition of multiplication already contains a theorem about powers. If multiplying by a point means turning through its angle and multiplying lengths by its modulus, then multiplying by the same point repeatedly repeats exactly the same geometric instruction. There is no new rule to guess.

Let z=r(cosθ+isinθ),r>0.z=r(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta),\qquad r>0. One multiplication by zz multiplies lengths by rr and adds the turn θ\theta. Two multiplications multiply lengths by r2r^2 and add the total turn 2θ2\theta. Three multiplications multiply lengths by r3r^3 and add 3θ3\theta. Thus the pattern is number of multiplicationsscaletotal turn1rθ2r22θ3r33θnrnnθ\begin{array}{c|c|c} \text{number of multiplications}&\text{scale}&\text{total turn}\\\hline 1&r&\theta\\ 2&r^2&2\theta\\ 3&r^3&3\theta\\ n&r^n&n\theta \end{array} The angle nθn\theta records the total instruction. It need not lie in a preferred interval: additional complete turns do not change the final point.

Theorem 3.5 · De Moivre's theorem for positive integer powers

For every positive integer nn, (r(cosθ+isinθ))n=rn(cos(nθ)+isin(nθ)).\boxed{\bigl(r(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)\bigr)^n =r^n\bigl(\cos(n\theta)+i\sin(n\theta)\bigr).} In particular, (cosθ+isinθ)n=cos(nθ)+isin(nθ).(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)^n=\cos(n\theta)+i\sin(n\theta).

Proof

For n=1n=1 the equation simply repeats the given expression for zz. Suppose it has been proved for a positive integer mm. Multiply the formula for zmz^m by zz. The polar multiplication rule gives zm+1=rm(cos(mθ)+isin(mθ))r(cosθ+isinθ)=rm+1(cos(mθ+θ)+isin(mθ+θ))=rm+1(cos((m+1)θ)+isin((m+1)θ)).\begin{aligned} z^{m+1} &=r^m\bigl(\cos(m\theta)+i\sin(m\theta)\bigr) r(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)\\ &=r^{m+1}\bigl(\cos(m\theta+\theta)+i\sin(m\theta+\theta)\bigr)\\ &=r^{m+1}\bigl(\cos((m+1)\theta)+i\sin((m+1)\theta)\bigr). \end{aligned} Therefore the formula for mm implies the formula for m+1m+1. Starting at 11, it holds for every positive integer. This induction is the algebraic record of the repeated instruction in the drawing.

3.12  Fractional turns and the meaning of a rational power

An integer repeats a motion; a fraction divides it into equal steps. A half-step of a specified turn θ\theta should therefore turn through θ/2\theta/2. We must keep track of the chosen angle, as the next example shows.

Example 3.5 · Why a square root is not forced by one polar expression

The point 11 has the two polar descriptions 1=cos0+isin0=cos(2π)+isin(2π).1=\cos0+i\sin0=\cos(2\pi)+i\sin(2\pi). Halving the displayed angle in the first gives 11. Halving it in the second gives cosπ+isinπ=−1.\cos\pi+i\sin\pi=-1. Both are square roots of 11, since 12=(−1)2=11^2=(-1)^2=1. The original endpoints agree, but the recorded turns 00 and 2π2\pi are different before we halve them.

Definition 3.6 · A power with a chosen angle

Fix a nonzero complex number zz and one real angle θ\theta such that z=r(cosθ+isinθ),r>0.z=r(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta),\qquad r>0. The subscript reminds us that the angle has been fixed; it is not part of the ordinary integer-power notation.

Writing the same fraction differently does not change this definition: 1/2=2/41/2=2/4 gives the same scale and angle. Changing the chosen angle from θ\theta to θ+2π\theta+2\pi, however, multiplies the answer by cos(2πt)+isin(2πt)\cos(2\pi t)+i\sin(2\pi t). ⚠ Choose the angle before dividing it

For an integer power there is one answer. For a fractional power there may be several roots. The formula with angle pθ/qp\theta/q selects one of them only after θ\theta has been chosen; it does not remove the other roots.

Choose the angle θ=π/4\theta=\pi/4 for z=1+iz=1+i. Its modulus is 2\sqrt2. 3.13  From rational parameters to real parameters

Every real number can be approximated by fractions. This lets us extend the preceding formula, provided nearby exponents give nearby points. That extra fact is continuity; density alone is not enough.

Keep one angle θ\theta fixed. For a unit point, put Uθ(t)=cos(tθ)+isin(tθ)U_{\theta}(t)=\cos(t\theta)+i\sin(t\theta) for any real tt: geometrically, we carry out tt times the recorded turn. Choose fractions tjt_j tending to tt, for example by truncating its decimal expansion. The angular error is (tj−t)θ(t_j-t)\theta. On a unit circle a chord is no longer than its arc, so, in radians, |Uθ(tj)−Uθ(t)|=2|sin(tj−t)θ2|≤|tj−t||θ|→0.\left|U_{\theta}(t_j)-U_{\theta}(t)\right| =2\left|\sin\frac{(t_j-t)\theta}{2}\right| \le |t_j-t|\,|\theta|\longrightarrow0. Thus every such sequence of fractions gives the same final point. Positive tt keeps the sign of the turn, negative tt reverses it, and t=0t=0 gives no turn.

For a positive real modulus rr, the usual real power rtr^t is also continuous in tt. Hence both the lengths and directions of the chosen rational powers tend to those in zθt=rt(cos(tθ)+isin(tθ)),t∈ℝ.\boxed{z_{\theta}^{\,t} =r^t\bigl(\cos(t\theta)+i\sin(t\theta)\bigr),\qquad t\in\mathbb R.} This uses the familiar continuity of positive real powers, sine and cosine. Their full construction belongs to real analysis. Here the angle stays fixed throughout; the formula defines a chosen-angle family, not an angle-independent power of every complex number.

The multiplication rule remains transparent when the chosen angle is kept fixed. For real s,ts,t, zθszθt=rsrt(cos((s+t)θ)+isin((s+t)θ))=zθs+t.\begin{aligned} z_\theta^{\,s}z_\theta^{\,t} &=r^sr^t\bigl(\cos((s+t)\theta)+i\sin((s+t)\theta)\bigr)\\ &=z_\theta^{\,s+t}. \end{aligned} This is simply addition of the two recorded turns and multiplication of the two positive scales.

3.14  Complex multiplication produces the rotation matrix

We now return from powers to a single rotation. Let a point PP have coordinate z=x+iyz=x+iy, and rotate it anticlockwise through θ\theta about the origin. They are what the already-defined complex multiplication becomes when its two components are written separately.

A column (xy)\binom{x}{y} records the two coordinates in a fixed order. 💡 The geometric idea to retain

Repeated spiral similarity gives de Moivre's theorem: scales are raised to integer powers and turns are multiplied by integers. Inverses reverse the instruction. Fractional or real parameters require us to retain a chosen angle. Separating the real and imaginary components gives the rotation matrix, and its integer powers express the very same repeated motion.
