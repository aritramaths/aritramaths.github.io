LectureDevelopment
1Geometry asks for arithmetic. Lengths, angles and similarity; translations, rotations and dilations; spiral similarity; Cartesian and polar descriptions; multiplication of points.
2One plane, two operations. Translation and addition; the Cartesian multiplication rule; the emergence of ii; arbitrary centres; conjugation, division, distance and geometric quotients.
3Geometry expressed by algebra. Square centres; the mixed-angle construction; equilateral triangles; Euler's line and Napoleon's theorem; de Moivre's theorem, chosen-angle powers and rotation matrices.
4Angles, roots and algebraic rigidity. Directed ray and line angles; collinearity, perpendicularity and concyclicity; general roots and roots of unity; primitive roots, sums, polynomial gcds and the prime-root relation. Similar triangles are offered separately as optional further reading at the end of the lecture.
5From area to weighted coordinates. Area comparisons; Ceva's theorem; unequal segment division and mass points; barycentric coordinates and signed complex area; maximum and minimum values of affine functions.
Appendix A. A classical synthetic route to Euler's line; the diameter–orthocentre lemma; further problems joining synthetic and complex methods.

How notation will be used

A capital letter such as AA denotes a point. Once complex coordinates have been introduced, the corresponding lower-case letter aa denotes its coordinate. Thus b−ab-a is the displacement from AA to BB, and |b−a|\left|b-a\right| is the length ABAB.

Before that stage, (x,y)(x,y) denotes Cartesian coordinates and [r,θ][r,\theta] denotes polar data. The different brackets prevent a common confusion: [1,90∘][1,90^\circ] and (0,1)(0,1) describe the same point, but their entries have different meanings. Brackets around three point names, as in [ABC][ABC], denote ordinary triangle area.

Definitions, theorems and examples are numbered within each lecture or appendix. Exercise numbers likewise begin with the lecture or appendix letter. Select “Show solution” beneath a homework problem to read its worked solution. Select it again to close the solution. Figure captions explain the relationship to be read from the drawing; colours are aids to reading, and no argument depends on colour alone.
