Greedy Pairing and the Rearrangement Inequality

Exchange arguments, Chebyshev’s sum inequality and extremal pairings.

Sections on this page

💡 Lecture aim

Develop a complete chain of ideas beginning with a greedy pairing rule. We shall justify the rule by an exchange argument, obtain the rearrangement inequality, derive Chebyshev's sum inequality, and then use rearrangement to prove both AM–GM and Cauchy–Schwarz. The main question throughout the lecture is simple: once two lists have been ordered, which terms should be paired?

In Lecture 1, most of our inequalities came from non-negative squares, quadratics, or Cauchy–Schwarz. Today we meet a different source of inequalities: order. The numerical values are not the only data; the relative positions of those values may also control the expression.

💡 Intuition · The Central Pairing Question

Suppose two lists of real numbers are to be paired, and we add the resulting products. Which pairing makes the sum as large as possible, and which pairing makes it as small as possible?

The natural greedy guess is: large with large for the maximum, and large with small for the minimum.\boxed{\text{large with large for the maximum, and large with small for the minimum.}} The purpose of this lecture is to prove that this greedy choice is correct and to see how several classical inequalities grow out of it.

Remark · The Route We Shall Follow

I want you to keep the following chain in mind while reading the lecture: two pairs→one exchange→removal of inversions⇓rearrangement→Chebyshev and applications\begin{gathered} \text{two pairs} \longrightarrow \text{one exchange} \longrightarrow \text{removal of inversions}\\ \Downarrow\\ \text{rearrangement} \longrightarrow \text{Chebyshev and applications} \end{gathered} The full rearrangement inequality is therefore not a mysterious formula. It is just the two-pair exchange lemma repeated until no bad pair remains.

2.1  The Greedy Principle and the Pairing Problem

A greedy algorithm makes the best-looking choice available at the present stage and then continues with the remaining data. A greedy rule is not automatically correct. Its correctness must be justified by showing that a locally better choice can be incorporated into a globally optimal solution.

For pairing problems, the relevant local move is an exchange: if two pairs are crossed, we swap their partners and compare the two totals.

Lemma 2.1 · The Fundamental Exchange Lemma

If xi≤xjandyr≤ys,x_i\le x_j \qquad\text{and}\qquad y_r\le y_s, then xiyr+xjys≥xiys+xjyr.x_i y_r+x_jy_s\ge x_i y_s+x_jy_r. Equivalently, pairing the smaller number with the smaller number and the larger number with the larger number never decreases the sum of the products.

Proof

Subtract the crossed pairing from the similarly ordered pairing: (xiyr+xjys)−(xiys+xjyr)=(xj−xi)(ys−yr)≥0.\begin{aligned} (x_i y_r+x_jy_s)-(x_i y_s+x_jy_r) &=(x_j-x_i)(y_s-y_r)\\ &\ge0. \end{aligned} Equality holds precisely when xi=xjx_i=x_j or yr=ysy_r=y_s.

Example 2.1 · A First Greedy Pairing

Pair the numbers 1,4,7with2,5,9.1,\ 4,\ 7 \qquad\text{with}\qquad 2,\ 5,\ 9. The same-order pairing gives 1⋅2+4⋅5+7⋅9=85,1\cdot2+4\cdot5+7\cdot9=85, whereas the reverse-order pairing gives 1⋅9+4⋅5+7⋅2=43.1\cdot9+4\cdot5+7\cdot2=43. The exchange lemma explains why every crossed pair can be repaired until the largest possible or smallest possible arrangement is reached.

Notice what has and has not been proved. The example suggests the answer, but an example can never prove a statement about all permutations. To pass from three numbers to an arbitrary number of terms, we need a precise way of detecting a pair that has been placed in the wrong order. This is why we now introduce inversions.

2.2  Inversions and the Exchange Argument

Let x1<x2<⋯<xn,y1<y2<⋯<yn,x_1<x_2<\cdots<x_n, \qquad y_1<y_2<\cdots<y_n, and let σ\sigma be a permutation of 1,2,…,n1,2,\ldots,n. Consider S(σ)=x1yσ(1)+x2yσ(2)+⋯+xnyσ(n).S(\sigma)=x_1y_{\sigma(1)}+x_2y_{\sigma(2)}+\cdots+x_ny_{\sigma(n)}.

An inversion of σ\sigma is a pair i<ji<j for which σ(i)>σ(j)\sigma(i)>\sigma(j). In that case xi<xj,yσ(j)<yσ(i).x_i<x_j, \qquad y_{\sigma(j)}<y_{\sigma(i)}. If we swap only σ(i)\sigma(i) and σ(j)\sigma(j), the exchange lemma shows that the value of the sum strictly increases. Thus an arrangement containing an inversion cannot be maximal.

This is the standard proof pattern for a greedy algorithm: find a local defect→exchange it→improve the objective.\boxed{\text{find a local defect}\;\longrightarrow\;\text{exchange it}\;\longrightarrow\; \text{improve the objective}.}

2.3  The Rearrangement Inequality

Theorem 2.1 · Rearrangement Inequality

Let n≥2n\ge2, and let x1<x2<⋯<xn,y1<y2<⋯<ynx_1<x_2<\cdots<x_n, \qquad y_1<y_2<\cdots<y_n be two ordered sequences of real numbers. Among all sums S(σ)=x1yσ(1)+x2yσ(2)+⋯+xnyσ(n),S(\sigma)=x_1y_{\sigma(1)}+x_2y_{\sigma(2)}+\cdots+x_ny_{\sigma(n)}, where σ\sigma runs over all permutations of 1,2,…,n1,2,\ldots,n, the maximal sum is x1y1+x2y2+⋯+xnyn,x_1y_1+x_2y_2+\cdots+x_ny_n, and the minimal sum is x1yn+x2yn−1+⋯+xny1.x_1y_n+x_2y_{n-1}+\cdots+x_ny_1.

Proof

Choose a permutation σ\sigma for which S(σ)S(\sigma) is maximal. Such a permutation exists because there are only finitely many permutations.

Suppose that σ\sigma has an inversion. Then there are indices i<ji<j such that σ(i)>σ(j)\sigma(i)>\sigma(j). Define σ′\sigma' by swapping σ(i)\sigma(i) and σ(j)\sigma(j) and leaving all other values unchanged. Then S(σ′)−S(σ)=xiyσ(j)+xjyσ(i)−xiyσ(i)−xjyσ(j)=(xj−xi)(yσ(i)−yσ(j))>0.\begin{aligned} S(\sigma')-S(\sigma) &=x_i y_{\sigma(j)}+x_jy_{\sigma(i)} -x_i y_{\sigma(i)}-x_jy_{\sigma(j)}\\ &=(x_j-x_i)\bigl(y_{\sigma(i)}-y_{\sigma(j)}\bigr)>0. \end{aligned} This contradicts the maximality of S(σ)S(\sigma). Therefore a maximal permutation has no inversions, so it must be the identity permutation. Hence the same-order pairing is maximal.

Applying the maximal statement to the increasing sequence −yn<−yn−1<⋯<−y1-y_n<-y_{n-1}<\cdots<-y_1, or repeating the same exchange argument for a minimal permutation, shows that the reverse-order pairing is minimal.

Remark · Non-strictly Ordered Sequences

The theorem remains valid if x1≤x2≤⋯≤xn,y1≤y2≤⋯≤yn.x_1\le x_2\le\cdots\le x_n, \qquad y_1\le y_2\le\cdots\le y_n. Repeated terms may produce more than one maximal or minimal permutation. In the extreme case x1=x2=⋯=xnx_1=x_2=\cdots=x_n, every permutation gives the same sum.

Remark · Opposite Monotonicity

If one sequence is increasing and the other is decreasing, then their displayed same-index pairing is the minimum, while reversing one of the sequences gives the maximum.

Caution · The Most Common Mistake

Before applying rearrangement, write both sequences explicitly and mark the direction in which each one is ordered. Statements such as “the largest terms are together” are not a proof unless we have first identified the two lists. Also remember that rearrangement compares pairings of two fixed lists; it does not allow us to change the terms themselves.

2.4  Chebyshev's Sum Inequality

The first major consequence of rearrangement is obtained by comparing the same-order pairing with all cyclic shifts of one sequence.

Theorem 2.2 · Chebyshev's Sum Inequality

Let a1≤a2≤⋯≤an,b1≤b2≤⋯≤bna_1\le a_2\le\cdots\le a_n, \qquad b_1\le b_2\le\cdots\le b_n be similarly ordered sequences of real numbers. Then a1+⋯+annb1+⋯+bnn≤a1b1+a2b2+⋯+anbnn.\frac{a_1+\cdots+a_n}{n}\, \frac{b_1+\cdots+b_n}{n} \le \frac{a_1b_1+a_2b_2+\cdots+a_nb_n}{n}. Equivalently, n∑i=1naibi≥(∑i=1nai)(∑i=1nbi).n\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_ib_i \ge \left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_i\right) \left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}b_i\right).

Proof

For each cyclic permutation σk\sigma_k of the bib_i's, rearrangement gives ∑i=1naibi≥∑i=1naibσk(i).\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_ib_i \ge \sum_{i=1}^{n}a_i b_{\sigma_k(i)}. Sum these nn inequalities over all cyclic shifts. On the left we obtain n∑i=1naibi.n\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_ib_i. On the right, each aia_i is paired exactly once with each bjb_j, so the sum is (∑i=1nai)(∑j=1nbj).\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_i\right) \left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}b_j\right). This proves the result.

Equality case

For similarly ordered sequences, equality holds if at least one of the two sequences is constant. Conversely, if neither sequence is constant, then a1<ana_1<a_n and b1<bnb_1<b_n, so the inequality is strict.

Corollary 2.1 · Reverse Chebyshev Inequality

If a1≤⋯≤ana_1\le\cdots\le a_n and b1≥⋯≥bnb_1\ge\cdots\ge b_n, then 1n∑i=1naibi≤(1n∑i=1nai)(1n∑i=1nbi).\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_ib_i \le \left(\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_i\right) \left(\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^{n}b_i\right).

2.5  First Applications of Rearrangement

☠ Problem 2.1

Let a,b,c∈ℝa,b,c\in\mathbb{R}. Prove that a2+b2+c2≥ab+bc+ca.a^2+b^2+c^2\ge ab+bc+ca.

Show solutionHide solution

By symmetry, arrange the three numbers so that a≤b≤ca\le b\le c. Apply rearrangement to the two identical ordered sequences (a,b,c)(a,b,c). The same-order pairing is maximal, so a2+b2+c2≥ab+bc+ca.a^2+b^2+c^2\ge ab+bc+ca. Equality holds when a=b=ca=b=c.

Remark · A General Form

For real numbers x1,x2,…,xnx_1,x_2,\ldots,x_n and every permutation σ\sigma, x12+x22+⋯+xn2≥x1xσ(1)+x2xσ(2)+⋯+xnxσ(n).x_1^2+x_2^2+\cdots+x_n^2 \ge x_1x_{\sigma(1)}+x_2x_{\sigma(2)}+\cdots+x_nx_{\sigma(n)}. Indeed, first arrange the xix_i's in increasing order and then apply the maximal part of rearrangement to two copies of that ordered sequence.

☠ Problem 2.2

Let a,b,c>0a,b,c>0. Prove that a3+b3+c3≥3abc.a^3+b^3+c^3\ge3abc.

Show solutionHide solution

Assume without loss of generality that a≤b≤c,a\le b\le c, so that a2≤b2≤c2a^2\le b^2\le c^2. Rearrangement gives a3+b3+c3=a⋅a2+b⋅b2+c⋅c2≥ab2+bc2+ca2.a^3+b^3+c^3 =a\cdot a^2+b\cdot b^2+c\cdot c^2 \ge a b^2+b c^2+c a^2. Also a≤b≤ca\le b\le c implies bc≥ca≥ab.bc\ge ca\ge ab. Applying rearrangement to a,b,ca,b,c and bc,ca,abbc,ca,ab, which are oppositely ordered, shows that ab2+bc2+ca2=a⋅(ab)+b⋅(bc)+c⋅(ca)≥a⋅(bc)+b⋅(ca)+c⋅(ab)=3abc.a b^2+b c^2+c a^2 =a\cdot(ab)+b\cdot(bc)+c\cdot(ca) \ge a\cdot(bc)+b\cdot(ca)+c\cdot(ab)=3abc. Therefore a3+b3+c3≥3abc.a^3+b^3+c^3\ge3abc. Equality holds exactly when a=b=ca=b=c.

2.6  AM–GM from the Rearrangement Inequality

Theorem 2.3 · Arithmetic Mean–Geometric Mean Inequality

For positive real numbers a1,a2,…,ana_1,a_2,\ldots,a_n, a1+a2+⋯+ann≥a1a2⋯ann.\frac{a_1+a_2+\cdots+a_n}{n} \ge \sqrt[n]{a_1a_2\cdots a_n}. Equality holds if and only if a1=a2=⋯=an.a_1=a_2=\cdots=a_n.

Proof by Rearrangement

Let G=a1a2⋯annG=\sqrt[n]{a_1a_2\cdots a_n} be the geometric mean. Define x1=a1G,x2=a1a2G2,…,xn−1=a1a2⋯an−1Gn−1,xn=1,\begin{aligned} x_1&=\frac{a_1}{G},& x_2&=\frac{a_1a_2}{G^2},& &\ldots,& x_{n-1}&=\frac{a_1a_2\cdots a_{n-1}}{G^{n-1}},& x_n&=1, \end{aligned} and y1=Ga1,y2=G2a1a2,…,yn−1=Gn−1a1a2⋯an−1,yn=1.\begin{aligned} y_1&=\frac{G}{a_1},& y_2&=\frac{G^2}{a_1a_2},& &\ldots,& y_{n-1}&=\frac{G^{n-1}}{a_1a_2\cdots a_{n-1}},& y_n&=1. \end{aligned} Thus xiyi=1x_i y_i=1 for every ii, and hence x1y1+x2y2+⋯+xnyn=n.x_1y_1+x_2y_2+\cdots+x_ny_n=n. If the xix_i's are rearranged in increasing order, then their reciprocals yi=1/xiy_i=1/x_i are arranged in decreasing order. Therefore the reciprocal pairing displayed above is the minimal pairing. This conclusion is independent of the labels we originally used: every other bijective pairing of the same two lists has sum at least nn.

We now choose one particular cyclic pairing, using the original definitions of the xix_i's and yiy_i's. Rearrangement gives n≤x1yn+x2y1+x3y2+⋯+xnyn−1=a1G+a2G+⋯+anG.\begin{aligned} n &\le x_1y_n+x_2y_1+x_3y_2+\cdots+x_ny_{n-1}\\ &=\frac{a_1}{G}+\frac{a_2}{G}+\cdots+\frac{a_n}{G}. \end{aligned} Multiplying by G/nG/n, we obtain a1+a2+⋯+ann≥G.\frac{a_1+a_2+\cdots+a_n}{n}\ge G. This is AM–GM. Equality in the minimal rearrangement forces all the xix_i's along this cyclic pairing to be equal. Since the cyclic shift connects all the indices, this forces x1=x2=⋯=xn=1x_1=x_2=\cdots=x_n=1, which is equivalent to a1=a2=⋯=ana_1=a_2=\cdots=a_n.

Remark · Why This Proof Belongs Here

The proof does not begin with a sum of squares or induction. It manufactures two reciprocal sequences whose opposite-order product sum is exactly nn. The rearrangement inequality then compares this minimal sum with a carefully chosen cyclic pairing that produces (a1+⋯+an)/G(a_1+\cdots+a_n)/G.

2.7  Cauchy–Schwarz from Rearrangement

Theorem 2.4 · Cauchy–Schwarz Inequality

For real numbers a1,…,ana_1,\ldots,a_n and b1,…,bnb_1,\ldots,b_n, (∑i=1nai2)(∑i=1nbi2)≥(∑i=1naibi)2.\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_i^2\right) \left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}b_i^2\right) \ge \left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_ib_i\right)^2.

Proof by Rearrangement

If all aia_i's or all bib_i's are zero, the result is immediate. Otherwise set A=a12+⋯+an2,B=b12+⋯+bn2,A=\sqrt{a_1^2+\cdots+a_n^2}, \qquad B=\sqrt{b_1^2+\cdots+b_n^2}, and define 2n2n real numbers by xi=aiA(1≤i≤n),xn+i=biB(1≤i≤n).x_i=\frac{a_i}{A}\quad(1\le i\le n), \qquad x_{n+i}=\frac{b_i}{B}\quad(1\le i\le n). From the general rearrangement consequence ∑k=12nxk2≥∑k=12nxkxσ(k)\sum_{k=1}^{2n}x_k^2 \ge \sum_{k=1}^{2n}x_kx_{\sigma(k)} for every permutation σ\sigma, choose σ\sigma to exchange the two blocks coordinatewise. Since ∑k=12nxk2=1+1=2,\sum_{k=1}^{2n}x_k^2=1+1=2, we get 2≥2a1b1+a2b2+⋯+anbnAB.2\ge 2\frac{a_1b_1+a_2b_2+\cdots+a_nb_n}{AB}. Hence AB≥a1b1+a2b2+⋯+anbn.AB\ge a_1b_1+a_2b_2+\cdots+a_nb_n. Replacing every bib_i by −bi-b_i gives AB≥−(a1b1+a2b2+⋯+anbn).AB\ge-(a_1b_1+a_2b_2+\cdots+a_nb_n). Therefore AB≥|a1b1+a2b2+⋯+anbn|.AB\ge\left|a_1b_1+a_2b_2+\cdots+a_nb_n\right|. Squaring proves Cauchy–Schwarz.

2.8  A Classical Olympiad Application

☠ Problem 2.3 · An Old IMO Problem

Let a1,a2,…,ana_1,a_2,\ldots,a_n be distinct positive integers. Prove that ∑k=1nakk2≥∑k=1n1k.\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{a_k}{k^2} \ge \sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac1k.

Show solutionHide solution

Arrange the integers increasingly: b1<b2<⋯<bn.b_1<b_2<\cdots<b_n. The sequence 1,122,…,1n21,\frac1{2^2},\ldots,\frac1{n^2} is decreasing. By the minimal part of rearrangement, every pairing of the bkb_k's with these weights is at least the opposite-order pairing ∑k=1nbkk2.\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{b_k}{k^2}. Consequently, ∑k=1nakk2≥∑k=1nbkk2.\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{a_k}{k^2} \ge \sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{b_k}{k^2}. Since the bkb_k's are distinct positive integers, bk≥kb_k\ge k for each kk. Therefore ∑k=1nbkk2≥∑k=1nkk2=∑k=1n1k.\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{b_k}{k^2} \ge \sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{k}{k^2} = \sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac1k. Combining the two estimates proves the result.

2.9  Practice Problems on Rearrangement

☠ Exercise · Rearrange the Terms

The following problems are intended to be solved primarily by ordering the variables and choosing an appropriate pairing.

  1. Let a,b,c>0a,b,c>0. Prove that ab+c+bc+a+ca+b≥32.\frac{a}{b+c}+\frac{b}{c+a}+\frac{c}{a+b}\ge\frac32.

  2. Let a,b,c>0a,b,c>0. Prove that a3b2+c2+b3c2+a2+c3a2+b2≥a+b+c2.\frac{a^3}{b^2+c^2} +\frac{b^3}{c^2+a^2} +\frac{c^3}{a^2+b^2} \ge\frac{a+b+c}{2}.

  3. Let a,b,c>0a,b,c>0. Prove that a+b+c≤a2+b22c+b2+c22a+c2+a22b≤a3bc+b3ca+c3ab.a+b+c \le \frac{a^2+b^2}{2c} +\frac{b^2+c^2}{2a} +\frac{c^2+a^2}{2b} \le \frac{a^3}{bc}+\frac{b^3}{ca}+\frac{c^3}{ab}.

  4. Let a,b,c>0a,b,c>0. Prove that a2b(b−c)a+b+b2c(c−a)b+c+c2a(a−b)c+a≥0.\frac{a^2b(b-c)}{a+b} +\frac{b^2c(c-a)}{b+c} +\frac{c^2a(a-b)}{c+a} \ge0.

  5. Let a1≤a2≤⋯≤an,b1≤b2≤⋯≤bna_1\le a_2\le\cdots\le a_n, \qquad b_1\le b_2\le\cdots\le b_n be positive ordered sequences. Prove that, for every permutation σ\sigma, (a1+b1)(a2+b2)⋯(an+bn)≤(a1+bσ(1))(a2+bσ(2))⋯(an+bσ(n)).(a_1+b_1)(a_2+b_2)\cdots(a_n+b_n) \le (a_1+b_{\sigma(1)})(a_2+b_{\sigma(2)})\cdots (a_n+b_{\sigma(n)}).

💡 Strategy · A Rearrangement Checklist

When a problem appears to involve ordering, I suggest that you ask these four questions in this order:

  1. What are the two lists whose terms are being paired?

  2. Are the lists similarly ordered or oppositely ordered?

  3. Do I need the maximum, the minimum, or only a comparison with one particular permutation?

  4. What does equality in the relevant exchange step require?

If you cannot answer the first question, rearrangement is probably not yet the right method.

💡 Lecture summary

  1. The exchange identity (xj−xi)(ys−yr)≥0(x_j-x_i)(y_s-y_r)\ge0 is the local reason that “large with large” improves a product sum.

  2. Repeatedly removing inversions gives the maximal pairing; reversing one list gives the minimal pairing. This is the rearrangement inequality.

  3. Adding the rearrangement comparisons over cyclic shifts produces Chebyshev's sum inequality.

  4. Reciprocal partial-product sequences turn rearrangement into AM–GM. A doubled normalised sequence turns it into Cauchy–Schwarz.

  5. In an application, our first job is to identify the two ordered lists. Equality must then be traced back to the exchange steps.
