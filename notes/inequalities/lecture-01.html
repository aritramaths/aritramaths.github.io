💡 Lecture aim

This is our first lecture on inequalities. The purpose is not to memorise a large number of formulas. We shall begin with a few elementary facts and learn how the same ideas return again and again in different forms. The main tools of this lecture are modulus, triangle inequalities, non-negativity of squares, the quadratic method, Cauchy–Schwarz, Engel form, and Weierstrass's inequality.

Remark · How to Read This Lecture

Whenever we prove an inequality, we shall keep three questions in mind:

  1. Why is the expression non-negative?

  2. Where can equality occur?

  3. Which part of the argument should we recognise in a new problem?

The equality case is not an extra decoration. Very often it tells us which form of an inequality we should use.

1.1  Modulus as Distance

Definition · Modulus

For a real number xx, |x|={x,x≥0,−x,x<0.|x|= \begin{cases} x, & x\ge 0,\\ -x, & x<0. \end{cases} Equivalently, |x|=max{x,−x}.|x|=\max\{x,-x\}.

Do not think of modulus only as a piecewise formula. From now on, it is more useful to think of modulus as distance.

💡 Intuition · Distance Interpretation

The number |x||x| is the distance of the point xx from the origin. More generally, |x−a||x-a| is the distance between the points xx and aa on the real line.

For example, |−2−4|=|−6|=6,|-2-4|=|-6|=6, and this is exactly the distance between −2-2 and 44.

Theorem 1.1 · Basic Facts About Modulus

For real numbers a,b,xa,b,x, the following hold.

  1. |a|≥0|a|\ge0, and |a|=0|a|=0 if and only if a=0a=0.

  2. −|a|≤a≤|a|-|a|\le a\le |a|.

  3. |−a|=|a||-a|=|a|.

  4. |ab|=|a||b||ab|=|a||b|.

  5. If b≥0b\ge0, then |x−a|≤b⇔a−b≤x≤a+b.|x-a|\le b \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad a-b\le x\le a+b.

Proof

We prove the statements one by one.

1. If a≥0a\ge0, then |a|=a≥0|a|=a\ge0. If a<0a<0, then |a|=−a>0|a|=-a>0. Therefore |a|≥0|a|\ge0 in all cases. Also, |a|=0|a|=0 forces a=0a=0, and clearly |0|=0|0|=0.

2. We again separate the two cases. If a≥0a\ge0, then |a|=a|a|=a, so −|a|=−a≤a=|a|.-|a|=-a\le a=|a|. If a<0a<0, then |a|=−a|a|=-a, so −|a|=a≤−a=|a|.-|a|=a\le -a=|a|. Hence −|a|≤a≤|a|-|a|\le a\le |a| for every real aa.

3. If a≥0a\ge0, then −a≤0-a\le0, and therefore |−a|=−(−a)=a=|a|.|-a|=-(-a)=a=|a|. The case a<0a<0 is similar.

4. Since both sides are non-negative, it is enough to compare their squares: |ab|2=(ab)2=a2b2=|a|2|b|2=(|a||b|)2.|ab|^2=(ab)^2=a^2b^2=|a|^2|b|^2=(|a||b|)^2. Therefore |ab|=|a||b||ab|=|a||b|.

5. Since b≥0b\ge0, the statement |x−a|≤b|x-a|\le b says that the distance of xx from aa is at most bb. Algebraically, |x−a|≤b⇔−b≤x−a≤b.|x-a|\le b \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad -b\le x-a\le b. Adding aa to all three parts gives a−b≤x≤a+b.a-b\le x\le a+b. Every step is reversible, so the two conditions are equivalent.

Remark · The Other Three Standard Forms

For b>0b>0, the same distance interpretation gives |x−a|<b⇔a−b<x<a+b,|x-a|<b \iff a-b<x<a+b, |x−a|≥b⇔x≤a−borx≥a+b,|x-a|\ge b \iff x\le a-b\ \text{or}\ x\ge a+b, and |x−a|>b⇔x<a−borx>a+b.|x-a|>b \iff x<a-b\ \text{or}\ x>a+b. The first two describe points inside an interval; the last two describe points outside an interval.

1.2  Triangle and Reverse Triangle Inequalities

The shortest distance between two points is the straight-line distance. On the real line, this becomes the triangle inequality.

Theorem 1.2 · Triangle Inequality for Real Numbers

For all real numbers a,ba,b, |a+b|≤|a|+|b|.|a+b|\le |a|+|b|. More generally, for real numbers a1,…,ana_1,\ldots,a_n, |a1+a2+⋯+an|≤|a1|+|a2|+⋯+|an|.|a_1+a_2+\cdots+a_n| \le |a_1|+|a_2|+\cdots+|a_n|.

Proof

We first prove the two-term inequality. Both sides are non-negative, so we may square them without changing the direction of the inequality.

We have |a+b|2=(a+b)2=a2+2ab+b2.|a+b|^2=(a+b)^2=a^2+2ab+b^2. Since ab≤|ab|=|a||b|ab\le |ab|=|a||b|, a2+2ab+b2≤a2+2|a||b|+b2.a^2+2ab+b^2 \le a^2+2|a||b|+b^2. But a2=|a|2a^2=|a|^2 and b2=|b|2b^2=|b|^2, hence a2+2|a||b|+b2=(|a|+|b|)2.a^2+2|a||b|+b^2=(|a|+|b|)^2. Therefore |a+b|2≤(|a|+|b|)2.|a+b|^2\le(|a|+|b|)^2. Both sides are non-negative, so taking square roots gives |a+b|≤|a|+|b|.|a+b|\le |a|+|b|.

Now we prove the many-term version. Apply the two-term inequality repeatedly: |a1+a2+⋯+an|=|(a1+⋯+an−1)+an|≤|a1+⋯+an−1|+|an|≤|a1|+⋯+|an−1|+|an|.\begin{aligned} |a_1+a_2+\cdots+a_n| &=|(a_1+\cdots+a_{n-1})+a_n|\\ &\le |a_1+\cdots+a_{n-1}|+|a_n|\\ &\le |a_1|+\cdots+|a_{n-1}|+|a_n|. \end{aligned} This completes the proof.

Equality case · Equality in the Real Triangle Inequality

In |a+b|≤|a|+|b|,|a+b|\le |a|+|b|, equality holds exactly when aa and bb have the same sign, or at least one of them is zero. Equivalently, ab≥0.ab\ge0. For several real numbers, equality holds when all nonzero terms have the same sign.

Theorem 1.3 · Reverse Triangle Inequality

For all real numbers a,ba,b, ||a|−|b||≤|a−b|.\bigl||a|-|b|\bigr|\le |a-b|.

Proof

Write a=(a−b)+b.a=(a-b)+b. Applying the triangle inequality, |a|=|(a−b)+b|≤|a−b|+|b|.|a|=|(a-b)+b|\le |a-b|+|b|. Subtracting |b||b| from both sides gives |a|−|b|≤|a−b|.|a|-|b|\le |a-b|. Now interchange aa and bb. Then |b|−|a|≤|b−a|=|a−b|.|b|-|a|\le |b-a|=|a-b|. Thus both numbers |a|−|b||a|-|b| and |b|−|a||b|-|a| are at most |a−b||a-b|. Since ||a|−|b||=max{|a|−|b|,|b|−|a|},\bigl||a|-|b|\bigr|= \max\{|a|-|b|,\,|b|-|a|\}, we obtain ||a|−|b||≤|a−b|.\bigl||a|-|b|\bigr|\le |a-b|.

1.3  When Three Numbers Form a Triangle

Let a,b,c>0a,b,c>0. We now collect several equivalent ways of saying that a,b,ca,b,c are the side lengths of a non-degenerate triangle.

Proposition 1.1 · Equivalent Triangle Conditions

For positive real numbers a,b,ca,b,c, the following conditions are equivalent.

  1. a+b>c,b+c>a,c+a>b.a+b>c,\qquad b+c>a,\qquad c+a>b.

  2. After relabelling so that c=max{a,b,c}c=\max\{a,b,c\}, we have a+b>c.a+b>c.

  3. a>|b−c|,b>|c−a|,c>|a−b|.a>|b-c|,\qquad b>|c-a|,\qquad c>|a-b|.

  4. |a−b|<c<a+b.|a-b|<c<a+b.

  5. (a+b−c)(b+c−a)(c+a−b)>0.(a+b-c)(b+c-a)(c+a-b)>0.

  6. There exist positive real numbers x,y,zx,y,z such that a=y+z,b=z+x,c=x+y.a=y+z,\qquad b=z+x,\qquad c=x+y.

Proof

We prove the implications in a cycle.

(1) ⇒\Rightarrow (2). This is immediate. Condition (1) contains all three triangle inequalities, so it certainly contains the one involving the largest side.

(2) ⇒\Rightarrow (3). Assume c=max{a,b,c}c=\max\{a,b,c\} and a+b>ca+b>c. Since c≥bc\ge b, we have |b−c|=c−b|b-c|=c-b. From a+b>ca+b>c, a>c−b=|b−c|.a>c-b=|b-c|. Similarly, since c≥ac\ge a, we have |c−a|=c−a|c-a|=c-a, and b>c−a=|c−a|.b>c-a=|c-a|. Finally, because c≥ac\ge a and c≥bc\ge b, |a−b|≤max{a,b}≤c.|a-b|\le \max\{a,b\}\le c. The equality |a−b|=c|a-b|=c would force the smaller of a,ba,b to be zero, which is impossible because a,b>0a,b>0. Hence c>|a−b|.c>|a-b|. Thus condition (3) holds.

(3) ⇒\Rightarrow (4). The inequality c>|a−b|c>|a-b| gives the left-hand part of (4). Also, a>|b−c|≥b−ca>|b-c|\ge b-c implies a+c>ba+c>b, while b>|c−a|≥c−ab>|c-a|\ge c-a implies a+b>ca+b>c. In particular, c<a+bc<a+b. Hence (4) holds.

(4) ⇒\Rightarrow (5). From c<a+bc<a+b, a+b−c>0.a+b-c>0. From |a−b|<c|a-b|<c, we have both a−b<candb−a<c.a-b<c \quad\text{and}\quad b-a<c. Rearranging, b+c−a>0andc+a−b>0.b+c-a>0 \quad\text{and}\quad c+a-b>0. Therefore every factor in (a+b−c)(b+c−a)(c+a−b)(a+b-c)(b+c-a)(c+a-b) is positive, so their product is positive.

(5) ⇒\Rightarrow (6). First note that among the three numbers a+b−c,b+c−a,c+a−b,a+b-c,\qquad b+c-a,\qquad c+a-b, at most one can be negative. Indeed, if both a+b−c<0a+b-c<0 and b+c−a<0b+c-a<0, then c>a+bc>a+b and a>b+ca>b+c, which is impossible after adding the two inequalities. The same argument works for any pair.

Since their product is positive and at most one factor can be negative, none of the factors can be negative. Also none can be zero. Hence all three are positive. Define x=b+c−a2,y=c+a−b2,z=a+b−c2.x=\frac{b+c-a}{2},\qquad y=\frac{c+a-b}{2},\qquad z=\frac{a+b-c}{2}. Then x,y,z>0x,y,z>0. (6) ⇒\Rightarrow (1). If a=y+z,b=z+x,c=x+ya=y+z,\qquad b=z+x,\qquad c=x+y with x,y,z>0x,y,z>0, then a+b−c=(y+z)+(z+x)−(x+y)=2z>0.a+b-c=(y+z)+(z+x)-(x+y)=2z>0. Therefore a+b>ca+b>c. Similarly, b+c−a=2x>0,c+a−b=2y>0.b+c-a=2x>0, \qquad c+a-b=2y>0. Hence all three triangle inequalities hold.

By Cauchy–Schwarz, (a12+b12)(a22+b22)≥(a1a2+b1b2)2.(a_1^2+b_1^2)(a_2^2+b_2^2) \ge(a_1a_2+b_1b_2)^2. Taking square roots gives (a12+b12)(a22+b22)≥|a1a2+b1b2|.\sqrt{(a_1^2+b_1^2)(a_2^2+b_2^2)} \ge |a_1a_2+b_1b_2|. Finally, |a1a2+b1b2|≥a1a2+b1b2.|a_1a_2+b_1b_2|\ge a_1a_2+b_1b_2. Thus the required inequality follows.

Corollary 1.3 · Many Vectors in the Plane

For real numbers ai,bia_i,b_i, ∑i=1nai2+bi2≥(∑i=1nai)2+(∑i=1nbi)2.\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sqrt{a_i^2+b_i^2} \ge \sqrt{ \left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_i\right)^2 + \left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}b_i\right)^2 }. Equality holds when all nonzero vectors (ai,bi)(a_i,b_i) point in the same direction.

Proof

We use induction on nn.

For n=2n=2, the result is exactly Theorem 1.6.

Assume the result is true for n−1n-1 vectors. Then ∑i=1n−1ai2+bi2≥(∑i=1n−1ai)2+(∑i=1n−1bi)2.\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\sqrt{a_i^2+b_i^2} \ge \sqrt{ \left(\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}a_i\right)^2 + \left(\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}b_i\right)^2 }. Add an2+bn2\sqrt{a_n^2+b_n^2} to both sides: ∑i=1nai2+bi2≥(∑i=1n−1ai)2+(∑i=1n−1bi)2+an2+bn2.\begin{aligned} \sum_{i=1}^{n}\sqrt{a_i^2+b_i^2} &\ge \sqrt{ \left(\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}a_i\right)^2 + \left(\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}b_i\right)^2 } +\sqrt{a_n^2+b_n^2}. \end{aligned} Apply the two-vector triangle inequality to the vectors (∑i=1n−1ai,∑i=1n−1bi)and(an,bn).\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}a_i,\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}b_i\right) \quad\text{and}\quad (a_n,b_n). We obtain (∑i=1n−1ai)2+(∑i=1n−1bi)2+an2+bn2≥(∑i=1nai)2+(∑i=1nbi)2.\begin{aligned} &\sqrt{ \left(\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}a_i\right)^2 + \left(\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}b_i\right)^2 } +\sqrt{a_n^2+b_n^2}\\ &\qquad\ge \sqrt{ \left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_i\right)^2 + \left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}b_i\right)^2 }. \end{aligned} Combining the two inequalities proves the result for nn. Hence it holds for all positive integers nn.

Remark · Complex Form

If zk=ak+ibkz_k=a_k+ib_k, then |zk|=ak2+bk2.|z_k|=\sqrt{a_k^2+b_k^2}. Therefore the preceding corollary is exactly |z1|+|z2|+⋯+|zn|≥|z1+z2+⋯+zn|,|z_1|+|z_2|+\cdots+|z_n| \ge |z_1+z_2+\cdots+z_n|, the triangle inequality in the complex plane.

1.9  Weierstrass's Inequality

Theorem 1.7 · Weierstrass's Product Inequalities

Let n≥2n\ge2.

  1. If a1,a2,…,an≥0a_1,a_2,\ldots,a_n\ge0, then (1+a1)(1+a2)⋯(1+an)≥1+a1+a2+⋯+an.(1+a_1)(1+a_2)\cdots(1+a_n) \ge1+a_1+a_2+\cdots+a_n. If all ai>0a_i>0, the inequality is strict.

  2. If 0≤a1,a2,…,an≤10\le a_1,a_2,\ldots,a_n\le1, then (1−a1)(1−a2)⋯(1−an)≥1−(a1+a2+⋯+an).(1-a_1)(1-a_2)\cdots(1-a_n) \ge1-(a_1+a_2+\cdots+a_n). If all ai>0a_i>0, the inequality is strict.

Proof

We prove both statements by induction.

The plus form. For n=2n=2, (1+a1)(1+a2)=1+a1+a2+a1a2.(1+a_1)(1+a_2) =1+a_1+a_2+a_1a_2. Since a1a2≥0a_1a_2\ge0, (1+a1)(1+a2)≥1+a1+a2.(1+a_1)(1+a_2)\ge1+a_1+a_2. If a1,a2>0a_1,a_2>0, then a1a2>0a_1a_2>0, so the inequality is strict.

Assume that for some n≥2n\ge2, ∏i=1n(1+ai)≥1+∑i=1nai.\prod_{i=1}^{n}(1+a_i) \ge1+\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_i. Since 1+an+1>01+a_{n+1}>0, multiply both sides by this positive number: ∏i=1n+1(1+ai)≥(1+∑i=1nai)(1+an+1).\prod_{i=1}^{n+1}(1+a_i) \ge \left(1+\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_i\right)(1+a_{n+1}). Expand the right-hand side: (1+∑i=1nai)(1+an+1)=1+∑i=1nai+an+1+an+1∑i=1nai=1+∑i=1n+1ai+an+1∑i=1nai.\begin{aligned} \left(1+\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_i\right)(1+a_{n+1}) &=1+\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_i+a_{n+1} +a_{n+1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_i\\ &=1+\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}a_i +a_{n+1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_i. \end{aligned} The last term is non-negative, so ∏i=1n+1(1+ai)≥1+∑i=1n+1ai.\prod_{i=1}^{n+1}(1+a_i) \ge1+\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}a_i. This completes the induction. If every ai>0a_i>0, then the additional term is positive, giving strict inequality.

The minus form. For n=2n=2, (1−a1)(1−a2)=1−a1−a2+a1a2≥1−a1−a2.(1-a_1)(1-a_2) =1-a_1-a_2+a_1a_2 \ge1-a_1-a_2.

Assume ∏i=1n(1−ai)≥1−∑i=1nai.\prod_{i=1}^{n}(1-a_i) \ge1-\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_i. Because 0≤an+1≤10\le a_{n+1}\le1, we have 1−an+1≥01-a_{n+1}\ge0. Multiplying by this non-negative number, ∏i=1n+1(1−ai)≥(1−∑i=1nai)(1−an+1).\prod_{i=1}^{n+1}(1-a_i) \ge \left(1-\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_i\right)(1-a_{n+1}). Expanding, (1−∑i=1nai)(1−an+1)=1−∑i=1nai−an+1+an+1∑i=1nai=1−∑i=1n+1ai+an+1∑i=1nai.\begin{aligned} \left(1-\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_i\right)(1-a_{n+1}) &=1-\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_i-a_{n+1} +a_{n+1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_i\\ &=1-\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}a_i +a_{n+1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_i. \end{aligned} Again the last term is non-negative. Therefore ∏i=1n+1(1−ai)≥1−∑i=1n+1ai.\prod_{i=1}^{n+1}(1-a_i) \ge1-\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}a_i. This proves the result. If all ai>0a_i>0, then at every stage from n=2n=2 onward a positive product term appears, so the inequality is strict.

Remark · What the Product Expansion Is Saying

In the plus form, after expanding ∏i=1n(1+ai),\prod_{i=1}^{n}(1+a_i), we obtain 1+∑ai1+\sum a_i together with products such as aiaja_ia_j, aiajaka_ia_ja_k, and so on. All these extra terms are non-negative. Weierstrass's inequality records exactly this observation in a reusable form.

  1. Modulus is distance. The triangle inequality says that a direct route is no longer than a route taken in stages.

  2. The triangle conditions for three positive numbers can be written in several equivalent forms. The substitution a=y+z,b=z+x,c=x+ya=y+z,\qquad b=z+x,\qquad c=x+y is obtained directly from the triangle differences.
