Part A: Weierstrass's Inequality and Algebraic Applications

☠ Problem 1

If a1,a2,…,ana_1,a_2,\ldots,a_n are positive real numbers, prove that (1+a1)(1+a2)⋯(1+an)>1+a1+a2+⋯+an(n≥2).(1+a_1)(1+a_2)\cdots(1+a_n) >1+a_1+a_2+\cdots+a_n \qquad (n\ge2).

Show solutionHide solution

We have already proved Weierstrass's inequality in the form ∏i=1n(1+ai)≥1+∑i=1nai(ai≥0).\prod_{i=1}^{n}(1+a_i)\ge1+\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_i \qquad(a_i\ge0). Here every aia_i is positive and n≥2n\ge2, so at least one of the omitted product terms is positive. For example, the full expansion contains a1a2>0a_1a_2>0. Therefore the inequality is strict: (1+a1)(1+a2)⋯(1+an)>1+a1+a2+⋯+an.(1+a_1)(1+a_2)\cdots(1+a_n) >1+a_1+a_2+\cdots+a_n.

This is worth noticing: the non-strict inequality comes from discarding non-negative terms, while strictness comes from checking that at least one of those discarded terms is actually positive.

☠ Problem 2

Let a,b,p,qa,b,p,q be positive real numbers such that a<ba<b and q<pq<p. Prove that (ap+bp)(aq−bq)>(aq+bq)(ap−bp).(a^p+b^p)(a^q-b^q) >(a^q+b^q)(a^p-b^p).

Show solutionHide solution

The two sides look complicated, but both are products of the same four basic powers. Therefore the natural first step is to subtract the right-hand side from the left-hand side. We obtain (ap+bp)(aq−bq)−(aq+bq)(ap−bp)=ap+q−apbq+aqbp−bp+q−ap+q+aqbp−apbq+bp+q=2aqbp−2apbq=2aqbq(bp−q−ap−q).\begin{aligned} &(a^p+b^p)(a^q-b^q) -(a^q+b^q)(a^p-b^p)\\ &=a^{p+q}-a^pb^q+a^qb^p-b^{p+q}\\ &\quad-a^{p+q}+a^qb^p-a^pb^q+b^{p+q}\\ &=2a^qb^p-2a^pb^q\\ &=2a^qb^q\bigl(b^{p-q}-a^{p-q}\bigr). \end{aligned} Now p−q>0p-q>0 and 0<a<b0<a<b, so bp−q>ap−q.b^{p-q}>a^{p-q}. All the remaining factors are positive. Hence the difference is positive, which proves the desired strict inequality.

☠ Problem 3

In a right-angled triangle ABCABC, right-angled at CC, prove that an+bn<cnfor every n>2.a^n+b^n<c^n \qquad\text{for every }n>2.

Show solutionHide solution

Because the triangle is right-angled at CC, the side cc is the hypotenuse and a2+b2=c2.a^2+b^2=c^2. Also 0<a<c0<a<c and 0<b<c0<b<c. Since n>2n>2, we have 0<ac<1⇒(ac)n<(ac)2,0<\frac ac<1 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \left(\frac ac\right)^n <\left(\frac ac\right)^2, and similarly (bc)n<(bc)2.\left(\frac bc\right)^n <\left(\frac bc\right)^2. Adding the two strict inequalities gives (ac)n+(bc)n<(ac)2+(bc)2=1.\left(\frac ac\right)^n+ \left(\frac bc\right)^n < \left(\frac ac\right)^2+ \left(\frac bc\right)^2 =1. Multiplying by cn>0c^n>0, we get an+bn<cn.a^n+b^n<c^n.

The whole point is that, on the interval (0,1)(0,1), increasing a positive exponent makes the number smaller.

☠ Problem 4

For positive real numbers a,b,ca,b,c, prove that ab+cbc+aca+b≤(aabbcc)2.a^{b+c}b^{c+a}c^{a+b} \le \bigl(a^a b^b c^c\bigr)^2.

Show solutionHide solution

All quantities are positive, so we may take logarithms. The desired inequality is equivalent to (2a−b−c)loga+(2b−c−a)logb+(2c−a−b)logc≥0.\begin{aligned} &(2a-b-c)\log a+(2b-c-a)\log b+(2c-a-b)\log c\ge0. \end{aligned} At first this expression may not look useful. The key is the following pairwise rewriting: (2a−b−c)loga+(2b−c−a)logb+(2c−a−b)logc=(a−b)(loga−logb)+(b−c)(logb−logc)+(c−a)(logc−loga).\begin{aligned} &(2a-b-c)\log a+(2b-c-a)\log b+(2c-a-b)\log c\\ &=(a-b)(\log a-\log b) +(b-c)(\log b-\log c) +(c-a)(\log c-\log a). \end{aligned} The logarithm is an increasing function on the positive real numbers. Hence each product on the right is non-negative. Therefore the entire expression is non-negative, proving ab+cbc+aca+b≤(aabbcc)2.a^{b+c}b^{c+a}c^{a+b} \le \bigl(a^ab^bc^c\bigr)^2. Equality holds exactly when a=b=ca=b=c.

We shall later recognise this argument as a three-variable form of Chebyshev's inequality applied to a,b,ca,b,c and loga,logb,logc\log a,\log b,\log c.

☠ Problem 5

For positive real numbers a,ba,b, prove that a+b1+a+b<a1+a+b1+b.\frac{a+b}{1+a+b} <\frac{a}{1+a}+\frac{b}{1+b}.

Show solutionHide solution

Bring everything to one side and use a common denominator. Since all denominators are positive, a1+a+b1+b−a+b1+a+b=ab(2+a+b)(1+a)(1+b)(1+a+b).\begin{aligned} &\frac{a}{1+a}+\frac{b}{1+b}-\frac{a+b}{1+a+b}\\ &=\frac{ab(2+a+b)}{(1+a)(1+b)(1+a+b)}. \end{aligned} Every factor in the final expression is positive because a,b>0a,b>0. Thus a1+a+b1+b>a+b1+a+b.\frac{a}{1+a}+\frac{b}{1+b} >\frac{a+b}{1+a+b}.

The strictness is immediate from the factor ab>0ab>0. If zero values had been allowed, equality would occur when ab=0ab=0.

☠ Problem 6

For n=1,2,3,…n=1,2,3,\ldots, let An=34−(34)2+(34)3−⋯+(−1)n−1(34)n,Bn=1−An.A_n=\frac34-\left(\frac34\right)^2 +\left(\frac34\right)^3-\cdots +(-1)^{n-1}\left(\frac34\right)^n, \qquad B_n=1-A_n. Part B: Triangle, SOS, and Cauchy Applications

☠ Problem 7

For every natural number n≥2n\ge2, prove that nn>1⋅3⋅5⋯(2n−1).n^n>1\cdot3\cdot5\cdots(2n-1).

Show solutionHide solution

The odd numbers 1,3,5,…,2n−11,3,5,\ldots,2n-1 are symmetric about their average nn. Pair equally distant terms from the two ends. For every positive tt, (n−t)(n+t)=n2−t2<n2.(n-t)(n+t)=n^2-t^2<n^2.

If nn is even, all nn odd numbers split into n/2n/2 such pairs, so 1⋅3⋅5⋯(2n−1)<(n2)n/2=nn.1\cdot3\cdot5\cdots(2n-1)<\left(n^2\right)^{n/2}=n^n. If nn is odd, the central factor is nn, while all the other factors again form (n−1)/2(n-1)/2 pairs. Therefore 1⋅3⋅5⋯(2n−1)<n(n2)(n−1)/2=nn.1\cdot3\cdot5\cdots(2n-1) <n\left(n^2\right)^{(n-1)/2}=n^n. This proves the result for every n≥2n\ge2.

☠ Problem 8

In a triangle ABCABC, prove that 32≤ab+c+bc+a+ca+b<2.\frac32 \le \frac{a}{b+c}+\frac{b}{c+a}+\frac{c}{a+b} <2.

Show solutionHide solution

We prove the two bounds separately.

For the lower bound, Engel form gives ab+c+bc+a+ca+b=a2ab+ac+b2bc+ab+c2ca+bc≥(a+b+c)22(ab+bc+ca).\begin{aligned} \frac{a}{b+c}+\frac{b}{c+a}+\frac{c}{a+b} &=\frac{a^2}{ab+ac}+\frac{b^2}{bc+ab} +\frac{c^2}{ca+bc}\\ &\ge \frac{(a+b+c)^2}{2(ab+bc+ca)}. \end{aligned} Also (a+b+c)2≥3(ab+bc+ca),(a+b+c)^2\ge3(ab+bc+ca), because a2+b2+c2−ab−bc−ca=12((a−b)2+(b−c)2+(c−a)2)≥0.a^2+b^2+c^2-ab-bc-ca =\frac12\bigl((a-b)^2+(b-c)^2+(c-a)^2\bigr)\ge0. Consequently, ab+c+bc+a+ca+b≥32.\frac{a}{b+c}+\frac{b}{c+a}+\frac{c}{a+b}\ge\frac32. Equality occurs at a=b=ca=b=c.

For the upper bound, use the triangle substitution from Section 1.3: a=y+z,b=z+x,c=x+y,a=y+z,\qquad b=z+x,\qquad c=x+y, where x,y,z>0x,y,z>0. Put T=x+y+zT=x+y+z. Then ab+c=T−xT+x,\frac{a}{b+c}=\frac{T-x}{T+x}, and similarly for the other two terms. Hence ∑cycab+c=3−2∑cycxT+x.\sum_{\mathrm{cyc}}\frac{a}{b+c} =3-2\sum_{\mathrm{cyc}}\frac{x}{T+x}. By Engel form, ∑cycxT+x=∑cycx2x(T+x)≥T2T2+x2+y2+z2.\sum_{\mathrm{cyc}}\frac{x}{T+x} =\sum_{\mathrm{cyc}}\frac{x^2}{x(T+x)} \ge \frac{T^2}{T^2+x^2+y^2+z^2}. Since x,y,z>0x,y,z>0, x2+y2+z2<T2.x^2+y^2+z^2<T^2. Therefore ∑cycxT+x>12,\sum_{\mathrm{cyc}}\frac{x}{T+x}>\frac12, which gives ab+c+bc+a+ca+b<2.\frac{a}{b+c}+\frac{b}{c+a}+\frac{c}{a+b}<2. The upper bound is strict for every non-degenerate triangle. This is an early instance of the Ravi workflow developed in Appendix B.

☠ Problem 9

If a,b,ca,b,c are the side lengths of a scalene triangle, prove that (a+b+c)3>27(a+b−c)(b+c−a)(c+a−b).(a+b+c)^3 >27(a+b-c)(b+c-a)(c+a-b).

Show solutionHide solution

Because a,b,ca,b,c are side lengths of a triangle, the three numbers x=a+b−c,y=b+c−a,z=c+a−bx=a+b-c,\qquad y=b+c-a,\qquad z=c+a-b are positive. Moreover, x+y+z=a+b+c.x+y+z=a+b+c. These are twice the three Ravi variables; compare Section B.1. By AM–GM for three positive numbers, (a+b+c)3=(x+y+z)3≥27xyz.(a+b+c)^3=(x+y+z)^3\ge27xyz. Thus (a+b+c)3≥27(a+b−c)(b+c−a)(c+a−b).(a+b+c)^3 \ge27(a+b-c)(b+c-a)(c+a-b). Equality in AM–GM would require x=y=zx=y=z, which in turn gives a=b=ca=b=c. The triangle is scalene, so equality is impossible. Hence the inequality is strict.

☠ Problem 10

💡 Summary · What Homework 1 Was Really Testing

The problems were deliberately mixed. Some required direct expansion or factorisation; some required the triangle substitution; and many reduced to a careful use of Cauchy–Schwarz or Engel form. The habit I want you to develop is to look first for the structure:

  • products of 1+ai1+a_i: expand or use Weierstrass;

  • triangle side expressions: use triangle inequalities or a=y+za=y+z, b=z+xb=z+x, c=x+yc=x+y;

  • a square of a linear expression: try Cauchy–Schwarz;

  • sums of fractions with positive denominators: try Engel form;

  • strict inequalities: identify exactly which discarded term is positive.
