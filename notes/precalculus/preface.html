Preface

How to read these notes

On this pageThe six-lecture route

These notes develop Precalculus as a conversation between formulas and pictures. A formula tells us how to calculate; a graph shows how quantities behave. Neither replaces the other. Our aim is to become comfortable moving between algebra, geometry, graphs and precise statements about sets.

The course is organised into six lectures, each building on the ideas developed before it. This volume contains Lectures 1–6, two reference appendices on logarithms and a collection of detailed solutions. The final lecture brings the methods together through graphical transformations.

Our first lecture makes the real number line completely familiar. We interpret absolute value as distance, learn how signs change across critical points, and use the median principle to minimise sums of absolute distances. We then use the number-line and wavy-curve methods to solve equations and inequalities. Its practice problems follow immediately after the lecture.

Lecture 2 introduces functions. It begins with mappings and the function-machine idea, distinguishes domain, codomain and range, counts functions between finite sets, and introduces the vertical-line test. Domain and range are then studied through successive function families: algebraic restrictions first, trigonometric functions next, followed by exponential functions and logarithms. The lecture ends with only an informal glimpse of reversing a function. Formal composition, one-to-one and many-to-one functions, and inverse functions are reserved for Lecture 3.

Lecture 3 makes that preview precise. Composition explains what it means to feed the output of one function into another. One-to-one functions, domain restrictions and inverse functions then lead to a broader investigation of piecewise, greatest-integer and fractional-part functions. The longer example bank is intended for patient reading as well as classroom use.

Lecture 4 investigates the behaviour of functions. It begins with even and odd functions, adds the closely related ideas of monotonicity and boundedness, then studies periodic functions and fundamental periods. It finally constructs inverse-trigonometric composite curves one branch at a time. Branches, endpoints, holes and domain restrictions matter: a simplified formula must never silently restore an input that was excluded originally.

Lecture 5 uses this preparation to read and construct graphs, solve trigonometric inequalities, and count solutions of equations. We learn to ask what a picture proves and what still needs an argument. The domain, the period and the equality cases guide each calculation. The first reading uses linked definitions, algebra, trigonometric identities and explanations of familiar graph shapes. Wherever a picture needs continuity for its formal justification, that future idea is named and explained intuitively. The optional section Further Study after Calculus supplies the later arguments. Leave it until after that module.

Lecture 6 develops graphical transformations from the movement of a single point. Translations, rescalings and reflections lead to modulus, greatest-integer and fractional-part constructions. We distinguish functions from relations, examine inverse graphs, and then combine curves by taking maximums, minimums, sums and products. Domains, endpoint choices and envelopes tie these constructions to the ideas of the preceding lectures.

Read actively. Sketch before looking at a finished figure; mark excluded endpoints; test a proposed answer in the original expression. When a proof uses a result that feels unfamiliar, follow the nearby link to its explanation and then return to the problem. Each homework problem has a button to show or hide its worked solution.

The six-lecture route

The following route shows how the ideas of the course fit together.

LecturePrincipal topics
1The real number system; intervals; absolute value as distance; sums of absolute values and the median principle; equations and inequalities; the number-line rule and the wavy-curve method.
2Function language and finite mappings; domain, codomain and range; the vertical-line test; algebraic domain restrictions; elementary range methods; trigonometric, exponential and logarithmic functions; an informal preview of reversing a function.
3Composition of functions; one-to-one and many-to-one functions; formal inverse functions; restrictions of domain; deeper investigation of functions.
4Even and odd functions; symmetry of graphs; monotonicity and boundedness; periodic functions and fundamental periods; inverse-trigonometric composite curves.
5Graphical representation of standard functions and basic transformations; trigonometric inequalities; counting intersections rigorously; piecewise and greatest-integer graph problems.
6Graphical transformations: translations, rescalings and reflections; modulus, greatest-integer and fractional-part graphs and relations; inverse graphs; maximum and minimum curves; envelopes of sums and products.

Two reference appendices provide a detailed study of logarithms. Appendix A develops the algebraic and numerical viewpoint, including characteristic, mantissa, bar notation, logarithm tables and applications. Appendix B develops the graphical and Calculus viewpoint, including transformations, inequalities, domain and range methods, derivatives, limits and growth. Links from the lectures lead to the relevant definitions, methods and examples in these appendices.

How the book is arranged

An idea is motivated first, stated precisely, and then used in worked examples. Each lecture ends with homework through which the reader can practise the ideas independently. The homework is organised by day, with a detailed solution beneath each question. Try each problem before opening its solution, and return to the relevant discussion whenever a step needs further explanation.

How to study a worked example

Before reading the solution, write down the domain and make a small sketch. Then read one mathematical line at a time: what fact permits the next line, and is the implication reversible? Finally check the boundary cases. A correct final interval with an unjustified endpoint is not yet a complete solution.

Throughout the book, I shall repeatedly ask us to translate between algebra, geometry, graphs and set notation. That habit is the real preparation for limits and Calculus.
