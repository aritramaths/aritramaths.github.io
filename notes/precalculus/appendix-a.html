A.2 Why logarithms were invented

Consider the multiplication 3478×5926.3478\times5926. It is possible by long multiplication, but the work is much more delicate than adding two numbers of comparable size. Suppose instead that 3478=10u,5926=10v.3478=10^u, \qquad 5926=10^v. Then the exponent law gives 3478×5926=10u10v=10u+v.3478\times5926=10^u10^v=10^{u+v}. Thus the difficult multiplication of the original numbers becomes the easier addition u+vu+v. The two numbers uu and vv are their common logarithms. After adding them, we reverse the logarithm to recover the product.

For centuries, logarithm tables and slide rules used exactly this idea. The point is not that a modern student should replace a calculator with a table. The point is to understand why logarithms naturally turn multiplicative questions into additive ones. That structural idea is still used throughout algebra, calculus, probability, data analysis and science.

Example A.1

See the simplification before computing

Suppose M=103.5412M=10^{3.5412} and N=102.1875N=10^{2.1875}. Express MNMN, M/NM/N and M\sqrt M as powers of 1010.

Solution.

For the product, add exponents: MN=103.5412102.1875=103.5412+2.1875=105.7287.MN=10^{3.5412}10^{2.1875} =10^{3.5412+2.1875} =\boxed{10^{5.7287}}. For the quotient, subtract exponents: MN=103.5412−2.1875=101.3537.\frac MN=10^{3.5412-2.1875} =\boxed{10^{1.3537}}. For the square root, multiply the exponent by 1/21/2: M=(103.5412)1/2=103.5412/2=101.7706.\sqrt M=(10^{3.5412})^{1/2} =10^{3.5412/2} =\boxed{10^{1.7706}}. Logarithm tables simply begin with ordinary numbers, look up these exponents, perform the easy arithmetic, and then reverse the lookup.

A.3 Before logarithms: the language of exponents

Logarithms are not a disconnected collection of rules. They are the language we use when the exponent is unknown. We must therefore make the laws of exponents precise first.

For a nonzero real number aa and a positive integer nn, an=a⋅a⋯a⏟n factors.a^n=\underbrace{a\cdot a\cdots a}_{n\text{ factors}}. The exponent tells us how many copies of the base are multiplied. To make the usual exponent laws continue to hold, we define a0=1,a−n=1an.a^0=1,\qquad a^{-n}=\frac1{a^n}. For example, 2−3=123=18,100=1.2^{-3}=\frac1{2^3}=\frac18, \qquad 10^0=1.

Rational exponents

For positive aa, integers mm, and positive integers nn, we define am/n=amn=(an)m.a^{m/n}=\sqrt[n]{a^m}=\left(\sqrt[n]{a}\right)^m. The assumption a>0a>0 keeps this expression real for every positive integer nn. Thus 272/3=(273)2=32=9,16−3/4=1163/4=1(164)3=18.27^{2/3}=\left(\sqrt[3]{27}\right)^2=3^2=9, \qquad 16^{-3/4}=\frac1{16^{3/4}}=\frac1{(\sqrt[4]{16})^3}=\frac18.

☠ A domain warning that will matter later

The real expression (−8)1/3(-8)^{1/3} is meaningful, but (−8)1/2(-8)^{1/2} is not real. A positive base avoids this case distinction. That is why a real exponential function axa^x, with arbitrary real xx, is defined with a>0a>0, and why a logarithm base must also be positive.

The laws of exponents

For a,b>0a,b>0 and real exponents r,sr,s, aras=ar+s,aras=ar−s,(ar)s=ars,(ab)r=arbr.\begin{aligned} a^r a^s&=a^{r+s}, &\frac{a^r}{a^s}&=a^{r-s},\\ (a^r)^s&=a^{rs}, &(ab)^r&=a^r b^r. \end{aligned} The logarithm laws in Section A.5 will be obtained by translating these four identities from the language of powers into the language of exponents.

Example A.2

Solve an exponential equation by matching bases

Solve 8x−1=42x+18^{x-1}=4^{2x+1}.

Solution.

Write both sides with base 22: 8x−1=(23)x−1=23x−3,42x+1=(22)2x+1=24x+2.8^{x-1}=(2^3)^{x-1}=2^{3x-3}, \qquad 4^{2x+1}=(2^2)^{2x+1}=2^{4x+2}. The function 2t2^t gives different positive outputs for different real values of tt. Therefore equal powers of base 22 have equal exponents: 3x−3=4x+2.3x-3=4x+2. Subtracting 3x3x and then subtracting 22, we obtain −5=x.-5=x. Hence x=−5\boxed{x=-5}. A direct check gives 8−6=2−18=4−98^{-6}=2^{-18}=4^{-9}, so the answer is correct.

A.4 What a logarithm means

Suppose we ask: 2?=8.2^{\,?}=8. The missing exponent is 33. A logarithm records precisely that answer.

§ Definition: logarithm to a real base

Let a>0a>0, a≠1a\ne1, and let x>0x>0. Then logax=y⇔ay=x.\boxed{\log_a x=y\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad a^y=x.} The number aa is the base, xx is the argument, and yy is the exponent to which the base must be raised to produce the argument.

Every elementary logarithm statement should be readable in both directions: log28=3⇔23=8,\log_2 8=3\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad2^3=8, log100.01=−2⇔10−2=0.01,\log_{10}0.01=-2\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad10^{-2}=0.01, log1/28=−3⇔(12)−3=8.\log_{1/2}8=-3\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \left(\frac12\right)^{-3}=8.

Why the restrictions are necessary

The three restrictions in the definition are structural, not cosmetic.

  1. a>0a>0: a positive base gives a real value ay>0a^y>0 for every real yy. This creates a smooth real exponential function.

  2. a≠1a\ne1: if a=1a=1, then 1y=11^y=1 for every yy. There is no unique exponent producing 11, and no exponent produces any other number.

  3. x>0x>0: a positive exponential aya^y can never be zero or negative. Therefore loga0\log_a0 and loga(−5)\log_a(-5) are not real numbers.

💡 The translation habit

When a logarithm seems unfamiliar, rewrite it as a power. When an exponent is unknown, rewrite the equation as a logarithm. This two-way translation is the foundation of every later calculation.

Values that should become automatic

For every admissible base aa, loga1=0because a0=1,logaa=1because a1=a.\log_a1=0\quad\text{because }a^0=1, \qquad \log_a a=1\quad\text{because }a^1=a. More generally, loga(at)=tfor every real t,\boxed{\log_a(a^t)=t\quad\text{for every real }t,} and alogax=xfor every x>0.\boxed{a^{\log_a x}=x\quad\text{for every }x>0.} These are inverse identities. Their graphical meaning is developed in Section B.2, alongside the inverse-function discussion in Section 4.4.

A.5 The logarithm laws, proved rather than memorised

Let a>0a>0, a≠1a\ne1, and let M,N>0M,N>0. Every law below requires positive logarithm arguments.

Product law

§ Product law

loga(MN)=logaM+logaN.\boxed{\log_a(MN)=\log_a M+\log_a N.}

Proof.

Put u=logaM,v=logaN.u=\log_a M, \qquad v=\log_a N. By the definition of a logarithm, M=au,N=av.M=a^u, \qquad N=a^v. Multiplying, MN=auav=au+v.MN=a^u a^v=a^{u+v}. The exponent to which aa must be raised to obtain MNMN is therefore u+vu+v. Hence loga(MN)=u+v=logaM+logaN.\log_a(MN)=u+v=\log_a M+\log_a N.

Quotient law

§ Quotient law

loga(MN)=logaM−logaN.\boxed{\log_a\left(\frac MN\right)=\log_aM-\log_aN.}

Proof.

With M=auM=a^u and N=avN=a^v, MN=auav=au−v.\frac MN=\frac{a^u}{a^v}=a^{u-v}. Therefore loga(MN)=u−v=logaM−logaN.\log_a\left(\frac MN\right)=u-v =\log_aM-\log_aN.

Power law

§ Power law

For real rr, whenever the real expression MrM^r is defined, loga(Mr)=rlogaM.\boxed{\log_a(M^r)=r\log_aM.} In particular, for a positive integer nn, logaMn=1nlogaM.\log_a\sqrt[n]{M}=\frac1n\log_aM.

Proof.

If u=logaMu=\log_aM, then M=auM=a^u. Consequently Mr=(au)r=aur.M^r=(a^u)^r=a^{ur}. The exponent producing MrM^r is urur, so loga(Mr)=ur=rlogaM.\log_a(M^r)=ur=r\log_aM.

☠ The most common false rules

There is no general law that splits a sum or difference: loga(M+N)≠logaM+logaN,loga(M−N)≠logaM−logaN.\log_a(M+N)\ne\log_aM+\log_aN, \qquad \log_a(M-N)\ne\log_aM-\log_aN. For example, log10(1+9)=1,log101+log109=log109≠1.\log_{10}(1+9)=1, \qquad \log_{10}1+\log_{10}9=\log_{10}9\ne1. Also, (logaM)r(\log_aM)^r is not the same expression as loga(Mr)\log_a(M^r). The power law moves an exponent on the argument; it does not remove a power placed outside the logarithm.

Example A.4

Expand a logarithm completely

For positive x,y,zx,y,z, expand loga(x3yz2).\log_a\left(\frac{x^3\sqrt y}{z^2}\right).

Solution.

First use the quotient law: loga(x3yz2)=loga(x3y)−loga(z2).\log_a\left(\frac{x^3\sqrt y}{z^2}\right) =\log_a(x^3\sqrt y)-\log_a(z^2). Use the product law in the first term: =loga(x3)+loga(y)−loga(z2).=\log_a(x^3)+\log_a(\sqrt y)-\log_a(z^2). Now use the power law on all three terms: 3logax+12logay−2logaz.\boxed{3\log_ax+\frac12\log_ay-2\log_az.} The positivity conditions are important: they ensure every logarithm written in the expanded expression is real.

Example A.5

Condense an expression into one logarithm

Condense 2log3x−12log3y+log3z,x,y,z>0.2\log_3x-\frac12\log_3y+\log_3z, \qquad x,y,z>0.

Solution.

Move the coefficients inside as powers: 2log3x=log3(x2),12log3y=log3(y).2\log_3x=\log_3(x^2), \qquad \frac12\log_3y=\log_3(\sqrt y). Thus 2log3x−12log3y+log3z=log3(x2)+log3z−log3(y).2\log_3x-\frac12\log_3y+\log_3z =\log_3(x^2)+\log_3z-\log_3(\sqrt y). Combine the sum as a product and the difference as a quotient: log3(x2zy).\boxed{\log_3\left(\frac{x^2z}{\sqrt y}\right).}

A.6 Changing the base

Tables and calculators cannot store a separate key for every possible base. The next formula lets us convert every logarithm to one convenient base.

§ Change-of-base formula

If a,b>0a,b>0, a,b≠1a,b\ne1, and x>0x>0, then logax=logbxlogba.\boxed{\log_a x=\frac{\log_bx}{\log_ba}.} In particular, logax=lnxlna=log10xlog10a.\log_ax=\frac{\ln x}{\ln a} =\frac{\log_{10}x}{\log_{10}a}.

Proof.

Let y=logaxy=\log_ax. Then ay=xa^y=x. Take logarithm base bb on both sides: logb(ay)=logbx.\log_b(a^y)=\log_bx. By the power law, ylogba=logbx.y\log_ba=\log_bx. Since a≠1a\ne1, logba≠0\log_ba\ne0. Dividing gives y=logbxlogba.y=\frac{\log_bx}{\log_ba}. Since y=logaxy=\log_ax, the formula follows.

Three bases occur frequently: logx=log10x(common logarithm),\log x=\log_{10}x\quad\text{(common logarithm)}, lnx=logex(natural logarithm),\ln x=\log_ex\quad\text{(natural logarithm)}, log2x(binary logarithm).\log_2x\quad\text{(binary logarithm)}. The base ee is developed from calculus in Section B.7. In numerical work with decimal notation, base 1010 is especially convenient; in calculus, base ee is the natural choice; in computer science, base 22 counts repeated doublings.

Find log27\log_2 7 to four decimal places, given ln2≈0.693147\ln2\approx0.693147 and ln7≈1.945910\ln7\approx1.945910.

Solution.

By change of base, log27=ln7ln2≈1.9459100.693147≈2.8073549.\log_27=\frac{\ln7}{\ln2} \approx\frac{1.945910}{0.693147} \approx2.8073549. To four decimal places, log27≈2.8074.\boxed{\log_27\approx2.8074.} This is sensible because 22=4<7<8=232^2=4<7<8=2^3, so the answer must lie between 22 and 33.

A.7 Algebraic equations involving logarithms

The safest order is always:

  1. write all positivity and base restrictions;

  2. use logarithm laws only where they are legal;

  3. solve the resulting algebraic equation;

  4. substitute every candidate back into the original domain conditions.

Example A.7

One logarithm equals a number

Solve log3(2x−1)=2\log_3(2x-1)=2.

Solution.

The logarithm requires 2x−1>0⇒x>12.2x-1>0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad x>\frac12. Translate the logarithm into exponential form: log3(2x−1)=2⇔2x−1=32=9.\log_3(2x-1)=2 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad 2x-1=3^2=9. Therefore 2x=10,x=5.2x=10, \qquad x=5. The value 55 satisfies x>1/2x>1/2, so x=5.\boxed{x=5}.

Example A.8

Combine first, then solve

Solve log2(x−1)+log2(x−3)=3.\log_2(x-1)+\log_2(x-3)=3.

Solution.

Both arguments must be positive: x−1>0,x−3>0.x-1>0, \qquad x-3>0. Together these give x>3x>3. On this domain the product law gives log2((x−1)(x−3))=3.\log_2\bigl((x-1)(x-3)\bigr)=3. Translate to exponential form: (x−1)(x−3)=23=8.(x-1)(x-3)=2^3=8. Expand and move all terms to one side: x2−4x+3=8,x^2-4x+3=8, x2−4x−5=0,x^2-4x-5=0, (x−5)(x+1)=0.(x-5)(x+1)=0. The algebraic candidates are x=5x=5 and x=−1x=-1. The original domain was x>3x>3, so −1-1 is extraneous. Therefore x=5.\boxed{x=5}.

Example A.9

A quadratic in logx\log x

Solve (log2x)2−5log2x+6=0.(\log_2x)^2-5\log_2x+6=0.

Solution.

The domain is x>0x>0. Put t=log2x.t=\log_2x. The equation becomes t2−5t+6=0,t^2-5t+6=0, (t−2)(t−3)=0.(t-2)(t-3)=0. Hence t=2t=2 or t=3t=3. Returning to xx, log2x=2⇒x=22=4,\log_2x=2\Longrightarrow x=2^2=4, log2x=3⇒x=23=8.\log_2x=3\Longrightarrow x=2^3=8. Both values are positive. Thus x∈{4,8}.\boxed{x\in\{4,8\}}.

A.8 Common logarithms: characteristic and mantissa

From this point through Section A.10, an unlabelled symbol logx\log x means log10x\log_{10}x.

§ Definition: characteristic and mantissa

For every N>0N>0, write logN=C+M,\log N=C+M, where C∈ℤC\in\mathbb{Z} and 0≤M<10\le M<1. Then

  • CC is the characteristic;

  • MM is the mantissa.

Equivalently, C=⌊logN⌋,M=logN−⌊logN⌋.C=\lfloor\log N\rfloor, \qquad M=\log N-\lfloor\log N\rfloor. The mantissa is always chosen non-negative.

Why moving the decimal point preserves the mantissa

If N=m⋅10kN=m\cdot10^k, where 1≤m<101\le m<10, then logN=log(m⋅10k)=logm+k.\log N=\log(m\cdot10^k)=\log m+k. Because 1≤m<101\le m<10, 0≤logm<1.0\le\log m<1. Thus kk is the characteristic and logm\log m is the mantissa. Multiplying or dividing a number by 1010 changes kk by an integer but leaves mm, and therefore the mantissa, unchanged.

For example, log472=2+log4.72,log47.2=1+log4.72,log4.72=0+log4.72,log0.472=−1+log4.72,log0.0472=−2+log4.72,log0.00472=−3+log4.72.\begin{aligned} \log472&=2+\log4.72,\\ \log47.2&=1+\log4.72,\\ \log4.72&=0+\log4.72,\\ \log0.472&=-1+\log4.72,\\ \log0.0472&=-2+\log4.72,\\ \log0.00472&=-3+\log4.72. \end{aligned} All six numbers have the same significant digits 472472, so they have the same mantissa.

Finding the characteristic by inspection

💡 Numbers at least 11

If N≥1N\ge1 has dd digits before the decimal point, then characteristic=d−1.\boxed{\text{characteristic}=d-1.} Indeed, 10d−1≤N<10d10^{d-1}\le N<10^d, so d−1≤logN<dd-1\le\log N<d.

Examples: 7.34 has characteristic 0,7.34\text{ has characteristic }0, 73.4 has characteristic 1,73.4\text{ has characteristic }1, 734 has characteristic 2.734\text{ has characteristic }2.

💡 Numbers between 00 and 11

If 0<N<10<N<1 and the first nonzero digit occurs in the kk-th place after the decimal point, then characteristic=−k.\boxed{\text{characteristic}=-k.} For example, in 0.004720.00472 the first nonzero digit is in the third decimal place, so the characteristic is −3-3.

The proof is a direct comparison with powers of 1010. If the first nonzero digit is in the kk-th decimal place, then 10−k≤N<10−k+1.10^{-k}\le N<10^{-k+1}. Taking common logarithms gives −k≤logN<−k+1,-k\le\log N<-k+1, so ⌊logN⌋=−k\lfloor\log N\rfloor=-k.

Bar notation for a negative characteristic

Traditional logarithm tables keep the mantissa non-negative. Thus log(0.00472)=−3+0.673942…\log(0.00472)=-3+0.673942\ldots is written log(0.00472)=3¯.673942…\boxed{\log(0.00472)=\overline{3}.673942\ldots} The bar applies only to the integer characteristic: 3¯.673942=−3+0.673942=−2.326058.\overline{3}.673942=-3+0.673942=-2.326058. It does not mean −3.673942-3.673942.

Example A.12

A.9 Logarithm tables and antilogarithms

Before electronic calculators, logarithm tables turned multiplication into addition, division into subtraction, powers into multiplication, and roots into division. Understanding the method still clarifies what a calculator is doing numerically.

A miniature common-logarithm table

The following row lists mantissas rounded to four decimal places.

Number2.302.312.322.332.342.352.362.372.382.39
Mantissa.3617.3636.3655.3674.3692.3711.3729.3747.3766.3784

The table gives only the mantissa. The decimal position of the original number supplies the characteristic. For instance, log235≈2.3711,\log 235\approx2.3711, whereas log0.0235≈2¯.3711=−2+0.3711=−1.6289.\log0.0235\approx\overline2.3711=-2+0.3711=-1.6289.

The four-step lookup process

To approximate logN\log N from a table:

  1. write N=m⋅10CN=m\cdot10^C, with 1≤m<101\le m<10;

  2. obtain the characteristic CC from the decimal position;

  3. look up the mantissa logm\log m from the significant digits;

  4. combine the characteristic and mantissa, using bar notation if C<0C<0.

Traditional four-figure tables use the first two or three significant digits to choose a row and column. A final digit is handled by a small proportional correction called a mean difference. This is simply linear interpolation over a very short interval.

Example A.13

Use a table entry

Solution.

The significant digits are 235235, and the table gives log2.35≈0.3711.\log2.35\approx0.3711. Now 235=2.35⋅102,235=2.35\cdot10^2, so log235≈2+0.3711=2.3711.\log235\approx2+0.3711=\boxed{2.3711}. Also 0.00235=2.35⋅10−3,0.00235=2.35\cdot10^{-3}, so log0.00235≈−3+0.3711=3¯.3711.\log0.00235\approx-3+0.3711 =\boxed{\overline3.3711}. As an ordinary decimal, this is −2.6289-2.6289.

Antilogarithms

The antilogarithm reverses a common logarithm: antilog(y)=10y.\operatorname{antilog}(y)=10^y. If y=C+My=C+M, where C∈ℤC\in\mathbb{Z} and 0≤M<10\le M<1, then 10y=10C+M=10C⋅10M.10^y=10^{C+M}=10^C\cdot10^M. An antilogarithm table supplies 10M10^M, a number between 11 and 1010, and the characteristic CC places the decimal point.

Example A.14

Read an antilogarithm

Find the number whose common logarithm is 2¯.4314\overline2.4314, given 100.4314≈2.70010^{0.4314}\approx2.700.

Solution.

The notation means 2¯.4314=−2+0.4314.\overline2.4314=-2+0.4314. Therefore 102¯.4314=10−2+0.4314=10−2⋅100.4314.10^{\overline2.4314} =10^{-2+0.4314} =10^{-2}\cdot10^{0.4314}. Using the given antilogarithm, 102¯.4314≈10−2⋅2.700=0.01⋅2.700=0.02700.10^{\overline2.4314} \approx10^{-2}\cdot2.700 =0.01\cdot2.700 =\boxed{0.02700}. The characteristic −2-2 correctly places the first nonzero digit in the second decimal place.

Multiplication, division, powers and roots

If P=MNP=MN, then logP=logM+logN.\log P=\log M+\log N. Thus we look up two logarithms, add them, and take one antilogarithm. Similarly, log(MN)=logM−logN,\log\left(\frac MN\right)=\log M-\log N, log(Mr)=rlogM,logMn=1nlogM.\log(M^r)=r\log M, \qquad \log\sqrt[n]{M}=\frac1n\log M.

Example A.15

Multiply using logarithms

Estimate 23.47×5.8223.47\times5.82, given log23.47≈1.3705,log5.82≈0.7649.\log23.47\approx1.3705, \qquad \log5.82\approx0.7649.

Solution.

Let P=23.47×5.82P=23.47\times5.82. Then logP=log23.47+log5.82.\log P=\log23.47+\log5.82. Substitute the table values: logP≈1.3705+0.7649=2.1354.\log P\approx1.3705+0.7649=2.1354. The characteristic 22 shows that PP has three digits before the decimal point. An antilogarithm table gives 100.1354≈1.366.10^{0.1354}\approx1.366. Therefore P≈102⋅1.366=136.6.P\approx10^2\cdot1.366=\boxed{136.6}. Direct multiplication gives 136.5954136.5954, so the small difference is caused only by rounding the logarithms.

Example A.16

Extract a cube root using logarithms

Estimate 57.23\sqrt[3]{57.2}, given log57.2≈1.7574\log57.2\approx1.7574.

Solution.

Let u=57.23u=\sqrt[3]{57.2}. Then u3=57.2u^3=57.2, so 3logu=log57.2≈1.7574.3\log u=\log57.2\approx1.7574. Divide by 33: logu≈1.75743=0.5858.\log u\approx\frac{1.7574}{3}=0.5858. An antilogarithm table gives 100.5858≈3.854.10^{0.5858}\approx3.854. Hence 57.23≈3.854.\boxed{\sqrt[3]{57.2}\approx3.854}. The estimate is reasonable because 33=27<57.2<64=433^3=27<57.2<64=4^3.

Accuracy and significant figures

If a table records a mantissa to four decimal places, its logarithm has been rounded. Every later addition, subtraction and antilogarithm carries that rounding error. We should therefore avoid reporting more significant digits than the data justify. Keep one guard digit during intermediate work and round only once, at the end.

Counting significant figures. Every nonzero digit is significant. Zeros trapped between nonzero digits are significant. Leading zeros merely locate the decimal point and are not significant. Trailing zeros to the right of a decimal point are significant because they record measured precision. Thus 0.0040700.004070 has four significant figures: 4,0,7,04,0,7,0. The number 205.00205.00 has five. The whole number 25002500 is ambiguous unless its accuracy is declared; 2.5×1032.5\times10^3 has two significant figures, whereas 2.500×1032.500\times10^3 has four.

Rounding. To round 0.004732680.00473268 to four significant figures, retain 4,7,3,24,7,3,2 and inspect the next digit, 66. Since 6≥56\ge5, increase the last retained digit: 0.00473268≈0.004733(four significant figures).\boxed{0.00473268\approx0.004733\quad\text{(four significant figures)}.}

Precision after multiplication or division. Measured data do not become more accurate merely because a calculator prints more digits. For example, 12.4×0.05678=0.704072.12.4\times0.05678=0.704072. The first factor has only three significant figures, so a sensible final report is 0.704.\boxed{0.704}. Exact counted numbers do not limit precision, but measured inputs do.

☠ Numerical honesty

A four-place table does not justify an eight-digit answer. Carry at least one extra guard digit in intermediate work, then round once at the end. Also check the order of magnitude independently: the characteristic should agree with a rough mental estimate of the size of the answer.

A.10 Applications of common logarithms

Counting digits

If NN is a positive integer and 10k≤N<10k+1,10^k\le N<10^{k+1}, then NN has k+1k+1 decimal digits. Taking logarithms, k≤logN<k+1,k\le\log N<k+1, so k=⌊logN⌋k=\lfloor\log N\rfloor. Therefore number of digits of N=⌊logN⌋+1.\boxed{\text{number of digits of }N =\lfloor\log N\rfloor+1.}

Example A.17

How many digits does 71007^{100} have?

Solution.

We use log(7100)=100log7.\log(7^{100})=100\log7. With log7≈0.845098\log7\approx0.845098, 100log7≈84.5098.100\log7\approx84.5098. Hence ⌊log(7100)⌋=84.\lfloor\log(7^{100})\rfloor=84. Therefore the number of digits is 84+1=85.84+1=\boxed{85}. Equivalently, 1084<7100<1085.10^{84}<7^{100}<10^{85}.

Locating the first nonzero decimal digit

Suppose 0<N<10<N<1 and ⌊logN⌋=−k\lfloor\log N\rfloor=-k. Then 10−k≤N<10−k+1,10^{-k}\le N<10^{-k+1}, so the first nonzero digit occurs in the kk-th decimal place.

Example A.18

Leading zeros in a small power

In which decimal place does the first nonzero digit of 2−202^{-20} occur?

Solution.

log(2−20)=−20log2.\log(2^{-20})=-20\log2. Using log2≈0.301030\log2\approx0.301030, log(2−20)≈−6.02060.\log(2^{-20})\approx-6.02060. Its floor is −7-7, so the characteristic is −7-7. Hence the first nonzero digit occurs in the seventh decimal place. Indeed, 2−20≈0.000000953674…2^{-20}\approx0.000000953674\ldots and the first nonzero digit is the 99 in the seventh place.

Scientific notation and order of magnitude

If N=m⋅10kN=m\cdot10^k, with 1≤m<101\le m<10, then logN=k+logm.\log N=k+\log m. Thus the characteristic gives the power of ten in scientific notation. It is also a quick measure of scale: numbers whose common logarithms differ by 11 differ by a factor of 1010; a difference of 33 means a factor of 10001000.

A logarithmic measurement scale

The pH model is pH=−log[H+],\mathrm{pH}=-\log[\mathrm H^+], where the concentration is measured in moles per litre.

Example A.19

Compute a pH value

If [H+]=3.2×10−5[\mathrm H^+]=3.2\times10^{-5}, find the pH to two decimal places.

Solution.

pH=−log(3.2×10−5)=−(log3.2+log10−5)=−(log3.2−5)=5−log3.2.\begin{aligned} \mathrm{pH} &=-\log(3.2\times10^{-5})\\ &=-\bigl(\log3.2+\log10^{-5}\bigr)\\ &=-\bigl(\log3.2-5\bigr)\\ &=5-\log3.2. \end{aligned} Using log3.2≈0.50515\log3.2\approx0.50515, pH≈5−0.50515=4.49485.\mathrm{pH}\approx5-0.50515=4.49485. Therefore pH≈4.49.\boxed{\mathrm{pH}\approx4.49}. The minus sign is what turns a small concentration into a positive pH.

Growth and doubling time

If a quantity grows according to A=A0btA=A_0b^t, then AA0=bt.\frac{A}{A_0}=b^t. Taking logarithms gives t=log(A/A0)logb.t=\frac{\log(A/A_0)}{\log b}. Thus logarithms recover the time hidden in an exponent.

Example A.20

Repeated percentage growth

A quantity is multiplied by 1.051.05 each year. After how many years will it first be at least twice its initial value?

Solution.

We need the least integer nn for which (1.05)n≥2.(1.05)^n\ge2. Because the common logarithm is increasing, taking logarithms preserves the inequality: nlog1.05≥log2.n\log1.05\ge\log2. Since log1.05>0\log1.05>0, division preserves the inequality: n≥log2log1.05.n\ge\frac{\log2}{\log1.05}. Using log2≈0.301030\log2\approx0.301030 and log1.05≈0.021189\log1.05\approx0.021189, n≥14.206…n\ge14.206\ldots The least integer satisfying this is n=15.\boxed{n=15}. It is important to round upward: the question asks for the first whole year at which the quantity has reached at least twice its initial value.

Decibels: equal additions mean equal ratios

One common intensity model is L=10log(II0) decibels,L=10\log\left(\frac{I}{I_0}\right)\text{ decibels}, where I/I0I/I_0 is an intensity ratio. If the ratio is multiplied by 1010, the level increases by 1010 decibels because 10log(10II0)=10+10log(II0).10\log\left(10\frac{I}{I_0}\right) =10+10\log\left(\frac{I}{I_0}\right).

Example A.21

Reverse a decibel reading

What intensity ratio corresponds to 3030 decibels?

Solution.

Put L=30L=30: 30=10log(II0).30=10\log\left(\frac{I}{I_0}\right). Divide by 1010: 3=log(II0).3=\log\left(\frac{I}{I_0}\right). Translate from logarithmic to exponential form: II0=103=1000.\frac{I}{I_0}=10^3=\boxed{1000}. Thus a 3030-decibel difference corresponds to an intensity ratio of one thousand in this model.

Geometric means and multiplicative averaging

For positive numbers x1,…,xnx_1,\ldots,x_n, their geometric mean is G=(x1x2⋯xn)1/n.G=(x_1x_2\cdots x_n)^{1/n}. Taking logarithms gives logG=1n(logx1+⋯+logxn).\log G=\frac1n\bigl(\log x_1+\cdots+\log x_n\bigr). Thus the logarithm of the geometric mean is the ordinary arithmetic mean of the logarithms. This is why logs are natural when changes multiply rather than add.

Example A.22

A geometric mean

Find the geometric mean of 2,8,322,8,32.

Solution.

Directly, G=(2⋅8⋅32)1/3=5121/3=8.G=(2\cdot8\cdot32)^{1/3}=512^{1/3}=8. The logarithmic route displays the averaging principle: log2G=13(log22+log28+log232)=13(1+3+5)=3.\begin{aligned} \log_2G &=\frac13(\log_22+\log_28+\log_232)\\ &=\frac13(1+3+5)=3. \end{aligned} Therefore G=23=8G=2^3=\boxed8.

An exponent that cannot be found by matching bases

Example A.23

Solve 5x=735^x=73

Solution.

There is no convenient way to write 7373 as an exact power of 55. Take logarithms on both sides: log(5x)=log73.\log(5^x)=\log73. Use the power law: xlog5=log73.x\log5=\log73. Since log5≠0\log5\ne0, divide: x=log73log5.\boxed{x=\frac{\log73}{\log5}}. Numerically, x≈1.8633230.698970≈2.6658.x\approx\frac{1.863323}{0.698970}\approx2.6658. The estimate is sensible because 52=25<73<125=535^2=25<73<125=5^3, so 2<x<32<x<3.

Example A.24

Mixed exact values

Evaluate log4132+log1/327.\log_4\frac1{32}+\log_{1/3}27.

Solution.

For the first term, write 4=224=2^2 and 1/32=2−51/32=2^{-5}: log4132=y⇔(22)y=2−5⇔2y=−5.\log_4\frac1{32}=y \Longleftrightarrow (2^2)^y=2^{-5} \Longleftrightarrow 2y=-5. Example A.25

Determine an unknown base

Find all admissible real bases aa for which loga16=4\log_a16=4.

Solution.

The logarithmic statement is equivalent to a4=16.a^4=16. Algebraically, a=2a=2 or a=−2a=-2. However a real logarithm base must satisfy a>0a>0 and a≠1a\ne1. Therefore −2-2 is inadmissible and a=2.\boxed{a=2}. This example shows why base restrictions must be applied after an algebraic equation is solved.

Example A.26

A parameter recovered from two logarithms

If loga2=p\log_a2=p and loga3=q\log_a3=q, express loga72\log_a72 in terms of p,qp,q.

Solution.

Factor 72=23⋅32.72=2^3\cdot3^2. Then loga72=loga(23⋅32)=loga(23)+loga(32)=3loga2+2loga3=3p+2q.\begin{aligned} \log_a72 &=\log_a(2^3\cdot3^2)\\ &=\log_a(2^3)+\log_a(3^2)\\ &=3\log_a2+2\log_a3\\ &=\boxed{3p+2q}. \end{aligned}

Example A.27

Characteristic of a power without evaluating the power

Find the number of digits in 32503^{250}, given log3≈0.477121\log3\approx0.477121.

Solution.

log(3250)=250log3≈250(0.477121)=119.28025.\log(3^{250})=250\log3 \approx250(0.477121)=119.28025. Therefore ⌊log(3250)⌋=119,\lfloor\log(3^{250})\rfloor=119, and the number of digits is 119+1=120.\boxed{119+1=120}.

§ Appendix A summary

  • logax=y\log_ax=y means exactly ay=xa^y=x, with a>0a>0, a≠1a\ne1, x>0x>0.

  • Product, quotient and power laws are exponent laws translated through the definition. No corresponding sum or difference law exists.

  • logax=logbx/logba\log_ax=\log_bx/\log_ba.

  • For common logarithms, logN=C+M\log N=C+M, where C∈ℤC\in\mathbb{Z} is the characteristic and 0≤M<10\le M<1 is the mantissa.

  • Moving the decimal point changes the characteristic but not the mantissa.

  • A negative characteristic is written with a bar so that the mantissa remains positive.

  • Logarithms convert multiplication to addition and powers to scalar multiplication; antilogarithms convert the final result back.
