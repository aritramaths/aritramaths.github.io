§ Lecture Objectives

Lecture 3 made composition and inverse functions precise. In this lecture we read four large-scale features of graphs. First we decide whether a graph has reflection or half-turn symmetry. We then describe where a function rises or falls and whether its outputs stay above or below fixed levels. Next we study horizontal repetition and learn the difference between a period and the fundamental period. Finally we build inverse-trigonometric composite curve one branch at a time, carefully recording domains, principal ranges, endpoints, jumps and holes. No curve in this lecture is meant to be memorised without an explanation.

💡 The order of today's ideas

We shall proceed in the following order:

  1. test the domain before asking whether a function is even or odd;

  2. translate algebraic parity into symmetry of the graph;

  3. compare outputs to identify increasing or decreasing intervals, then record upper and lower bounds;

  4. recognise a legal horizontal shift and then check whether it is the least positive one;

  5. redraw all six principal inverse-trigonometric functions, then construct their composite curves interval by interval.

The definitions of composition and inverse functions from Lecture 3 will guide our work with composite curves.

4.1 Even and Odd Functions: Reading Symmetry from a Formula

Before doing any algebra, we must check whether the domain is symmetric about the origin. This means x∈Df⇒−x∈Df.x\in D_f\quad\Longrightarrow\quad -x\in D_f. If the domain is not symmetric, then the usual labels “even” and “odd” do not apply on that domain. For example, x2x^2 is even on ℝ\mathbb{R}, but the restriction x2:[0,∞)→[0,∞)x^2:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty) is not called even because negative inputs are absent.

§ Definitions: even and odd

Let the domain of ff be symmetric about 00.

  • The function is even if f(−x)=f(x)for every x∈Df.f(-x)=f(x)\qquad\text{for every }x\in D_f. Its graph is symmetric about the yy-axis.

  • The function is odd if f(−x)=−f(x)for every x∈Df.f(-x)=-f(x)\qquad\text{for every }x\in D_f. Its graph is symmetric about the origin: rotating the graph through 180∘180^\circ gives the same graph.

The words even and odd describe functions, not merely powers of xx.

Why does the algebra produce the stated symmetry? If (x,f(x))(x,f(x)) lies on an even graph, then (−x,f(−x))=(−x,f(x))(-x,f(-x))=(-x,f(x)) also lies on it. These two points have opposite horizontal coordinates and the same height, so they are mirror images in the yy-axis. For an odd function, (−x,f(−x))=(−x,−f(x)),(-x,f(-x))=(-x,-f(x)), which is the point opposite (x,f(x))(x,f(x)) through the origin.

💡 A four-step parity test

For a formula f(x)f(x):

  1. find the domain and check that it is symmetric about 00;

  2. write f(−x)f(-x) by replacing every occurrence of xx by −x-x;

  3. simplify carefully, without guessing from one or two values;

  4. compare the result with f(x)f(x) and −f(x)-f(x).

If neither comparison works for every input, the function is neither even nor odd.

4.2 Monotonicity and Boundedness: Direction and Height

Parity tells us how the left and right sides of a graph are related. Monotonicity asks a different question: as the input moves from left to right, do the outputs rise or fall? Boundedness asks whether the graph can cross every horizontal level, or whether one or more horizontal levels act as barriers.

The words “increasing” and “decreasing” must always be attached to an interval. A graph may fall on one interval and rise on another. For example, the parabola y=x2y=x^2 falls until x=0x=0 and rises after x=0x=0; it is not increasing or decreasing on all of ℝ\mathbb{R}.

§ Definitions: increasing and decreasing

Let II be an interval contained in the domain of ff, and choose any two points x1,x2∈Ix_1,x_2\in I with x1<x2x_1<x_2.

  • ff is increasing on II if f(x1)<f(x2).f(x_1)<f(x_2). The later input must give the larger output.

  • ff is decreasing on II if f(x1)>f(x2).f(x_1)>f(x_2). The later input must give the smaller output.

  • If the strict signs are replaced by ≤\le and ≥\ge, the terms are non-decreasing and non-increasing, respectively.

The word monotone means that one of these behaviours holds throughout the interval being discussed.

Notice that a constant function is both non-decreasing and non-increasing, but it is neither strictly increasing nor strictly decreasing. This is why the strict and non-strict definitions must not be mixed.

Example 4.5

Solution.

First take −2≤x1<x2≤0-2\le x_1<x_2\le0. Then f(x1)−f(x2)=x12−x22=(x1−x2)(x1+x2).f(x_1)-f(x_2)=x_1^2-x_2^2=(x_1-x_2)(x_1+x_2). Here x1−x2<0x_1-x_2<0, and x1+x2<0x_1+x_2<0. § Definitions: bounds, maximum and minimum

Let ff have domain DD.

  • ff is bounded above if there is a real number MM such that f(x)≤Mf(x)\le M for every x∈Dx\in D.

  • ff is bounded below if there is a real number mm such that m≤f(x)m\le f(x) for every x∈Dx\in D.

  • It is bounded if it is bounded both above and below.

  • A number MM is the maximum value only if M=f(c)M=f(c) for some actual input c∈Dc\in D. Similarly, mm is the minimum value only if m=f(c)m=f(c) for some c∈Dc\in D.

A bound need not be attained. Thus “bounded below by 00” does not by itself mean “has minimum value 00.”

💡 Connection with inverse functions

A strictly increasing or strictly decreasing function on an interval cannot take the same output at two different inputs. It is therefore one-to-one on that interval and passes the horizontal-line test. This is the reason a strictly monotone branch can be inverted.

For example, x2x^2 is not one-to-one on ℝ\mathbb{R}, but its restriction to [0,∞)[0,\infty) is strictly increasing. That restricted branch has the inverse x\sqrt{x}. Monotonicity therefore tells us not only how a graph moves, but also which branch can legally be reflected in y=xy=x to produce an inverse.

4.3 Periodic Functions: A Graph That Repeats

Imagine drawing a small pattern on a transparent strip and sliding the strip to the right. If the drawing falls exactly on top of itself after a shift of TT units, then the corresponding function has period TT. Periodicity is therefore a statement about horizontal translation, not about reflection.

§ Definition: period and fundamental period

A positive number TT is a period of ff if translating every legal input by TT preserves both the domain and the output: x∈Df⇔x+T∈Df,f(x+T)=f(x).x\in D_f\Longleftrightarrow x+T\in D_f, \qquad f(x+T)=f(x). If at least one positive period exists, ff is periodic. The least positive period, when it exists, is called the fundamental period.

The word “least” matters. If TT is a period, then 2T,3T,…2T,3T,\ldots are also periods, but these larger numbers are not fundamental. A constant function is a special case: every positive number is a period, so there is no least positive one. Thus a constant function is periodic but has no fundamental period.

4.4 Inverse-Trigonometric Functions and Their Composite Curves

Why a principal branch is necessary

The ordinary trigonometric functions repeat values. For example, sinπ6=sin5π6=sin13π6=12.\sin\frac\pi6=\sin\frac{5\pi}6=\sin\frac{13\pi}6=\frac12. Consequently, the unrestricted sine function is many-to-one. If we tried to write an inverse without first making a choice, the input 1/21/2 would have many possible outputs. That would violate the definition of a function, which permits exactly one output for each input.

The remedy is to keep one carefully chosen part of the trigonometric graph. That one-to-one part is called a branch. The interval chosen for the branch is called the principal interval, and the corresponding inverse outputs are called principal values. Why the two orders of composition are not interchangeable

For a genuine bijection f:A→Bf:A\to B, the inverse has the opposite direction, f−1:B→Af^{-1}:B\to A. The first composition starts with an element of AA; the second starts with an element of BB. Thus even for a true inverse, the two compositions have different input spaces. Function composition is not a commutative operation: we may not exchange the order merely because the same two names appear.

For trigonometric functions there is an additional difficulty. The symbol arcsin\arcsin is the inverse of the restricted sine branch, not of the many-to-one sine function on all of ℝ\mathbb{R}. ☠ The precise statement

It is common to say informally that “a trigonometric function and its inverse do not commute.” The precise statement is this: composition is order-sensitive, and the inverse-trigonometric functions invert only the chosen principal branches. On those branches the two inverse identities are completely valid; outside them, the inverse-after-trigonometric composition folds the input back to a principal representative.

A first look at branches and branch cuts

The real-variable restriction above is a first example of choosing a branch. A many-to-one rule is replaced by one consistent one-to-one part so that a single-valued inverse can be defined.

Complex analysis uses the same guiding idea, but the geometry is two-dimensional. For example, a nonzero complex number can be written as z=rei(θ+2kπ),k∈ℤ.z=re^{i(\theta+2k\pi)},\qquad k\in\mathbb{Z}. Its angle is not unique. Consequently the unrestricted complex logarithm has infinitely many possible values, logz=lnr+i(θ+2kπ).\log z=\ln r+i(\theta+2k\pi). To obtain the principal logarithm, we choose one continuous range of angles, for example −π<θ<π-\pi<\theta<\pi, and remove the non-positive real axis. The removed ray is called a branch cut. On the plane with that cut, the chosen angle cannot jump by 2π2\pi while we move continuously around the origin, so the principal logarithm becomes single-valued.

There is an important distinction. In real trigonometry we usually restrict the input interval of sine, cosine, tangent and their reciprocals. In complex analysis a branch cut removes a curve from a region of the complex plane so that a consistent branch can be followed. These are not identical procedures, but they solve the same logical problem: many possible inverse values must be reduced to one declared choice. Complex inverse-trigonometric functions are built from complex logarithms and square roots, so they inherit branch choices and branch cuts. No complex calculation is required in this course; the purpose of this preview is to show that the principal-value idea continues into higher mathematics.

A trigonometric function outside its inverse

Now the inverse acts first. The outer trigonometric function therefore undoes it immediately, but only for inputs belonging to the inverse function's domain. The correct conclusion is not merely “y=xy=x”; it is “y=xy=x on the stated restricted domain.”

Multiple-angle identities inside inverse functions

The next six curves all use the same substitution idea. We choose θ\theta in a principal interval, recognise a double- or triple-angle identity, and then fold the resulting angle into the principal range of the outer inverse. The folding is the essential step; cancelling symbols would give the wrong graph.

4.5 Lecture Summary

§ What must be remembered from Lecture 4

  • An even/odd test begins with the domain. The identities f(−x)=f(x)f(-x)=f(x) and f(−x)=−f(x)f(-x)=-f(x) are meaningful only on a domain symmetric about 00.

  • An even graph is reflected in the yy-axis; an odd graph is unchanged by a half-turn about the origin. A function may be neither.

  • Monotonicity is always stated on an interval. Increasing means that a larger input gives a larger output; decreasing means that it gives a smaller output. A bound need not be attained, whereas a maximum or minimum must be an actual function value.

  • A period is a positive horizontal shift that preserves both the legal inputs and all output values. The fundamental period, if it exists, is the least positive period.

  • A common multiple of component periods gives a candidate period only when the component periods are commensurable. Cancellation or extra symmetry may shorten it, so the least period still requires proof.

  • An inverse trigonometric function returns a principal value. Therefore sin(arcsinx)=x\sin(\arcsin x)=x on the domain of arcsin\arcsin, but arcsin(sinx)\arcsin(\sin x) folds the whole sine wave into [−π/2,π/2][-\pi/2,\pi/2].

  • A principal branch keeps one one-to-one part of a many-to-one function. A branch cut is the related complex-analysis device that removes a curve so a single-valued branch can be chosen consistently.

  • Before cancelling reciprocal trigonometric factors, retain the holes from the original formula. Algebraic simplification does not restore an input at which the original expression was undefined.
