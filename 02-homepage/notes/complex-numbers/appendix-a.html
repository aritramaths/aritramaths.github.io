Appendix A

A synthetic route to Euler's line

Sections on this page
A.1 The points in the constructionA.2 The diameter–orthocentre theoremA.3 Euler's line from similar trianglesA.4 How the picture generates the complex formulaA.5 Problems for further study

💡 Why this appendix is here

Lecture 3 obtained Euler's line from the complex-coordinate identity h−o=3(g−o).h-o=3(g-o). That proof is short because the algebra stores several geometric facts at once. This appendix travels in the other direction. A diameter of the circumcircle creates two right angles; those right angles create a parallelogram; the parallelogram creates a midpoint relation; and that relation leads to Euler's line. The final calculation then shows that the synthetic picture and the complex formula are two descriptions of the same structure.

A.1  The points in the construction

Let ABCABC be a nondegenerate triangle. We use the same three centres as in Lecture 3:

  • OO is the circumcentre, the centre of the circle through A,B,CA,B,C;

  • HH is the orthocentre, the common point of the three altitudes;

  • GG is the centroid, the common point of the three medians.

Definition A.1 · Antipodal point

Two points on a circle are antipodal if the segment joining them is a diameter. Thus the point PP antipodal to AA is the unique point on the circumcircle for which A,O,PA,O,P are collinear and OA=OPOA=OP, with OO between AA and PP.

Let PP be the antipode of AA, so APAP is a diameter. Let A′A' be the foot of the perpendicular from OO to the line BCBC.

We will use two elementary facts. They are stated explicitly because each one performs a visible job in the proof.

Lemma A.2 · The angle in a semicircle

If APAP is a diameter of a circle and XX is any other point of the circle, then ∠AXP=90∘\angle AXP=90^\circ.

Proof

Join OO to A,X,PA,X,P. The triangles AOXAOX and POXPOX are isosceles because all four displayed radii have the same length. If the two angles at XX adjacent to XOXO are α\alpha and β\beta, then the corresponding angles at OO are 180∘−2α180^\circ-2\alpha and 180∘−2β180^\circ-2\beta. These two central angles form a straight angle, so (180∘−2α)+(180∘−2β)=180∘.(180^\circ-2\alpha)+(180^\circ-2\beta)=180^\circ. Hence α+β=90∘\alpha+\beta=90^\circ, which is precisely ∠AXP\angle AXP.

Lemma A.3 · A perpendicular from the centre bisects a chord

If OA′⊥BCOA'\perp BC, where OO is the centre of a circle and B,CB,C lie on the circle, then BA′=A′CBA'=A'C.

Proof

The right triangles OBA′OBA' and OCA′OCA' have equal hypotenuses OB=OCOB=OC and the common leg OA′OA'. They are congruent, so their remaining legs are equal: BA′=A′CBA'=A'C.

A.2  The diameter–orthocentre theorem

Theorem A.4 · Antipode–orthocentre parallelogram

Let HH be the orthocentre of ABCABC, let PP be the point antipodal to AA on the circumcircle, and let OA′⊥BCOA'\perp BC. Then BCBC and PHPH bisect each other at A′A'. Moreover, AH→=2OA′→and henceAH=2OA′.\boxed{\overrightarrow{AH}=2\overrightarrow{OA'}} \qquad\text{and hence}\qquad \boxed{AH=2\,OA'.} In the ordinary noncollapsed configuration, BPCHBPCH is a parallelogram.

Proof

The proof is a chain of short geometric consequences.

Step 1: the diameter creates two right angles. By the angle-in-a-semicircle lemma, AB⊥BP,AC⊥CP.AB\perp BP, \qquad AC\perp CP.

Step 2: the orthocentre creates two more right angles. Because HH lies on the altitudes from BB and CC, BH⊥AC,CH⊥AB.BH\perp AC, \qquad CH\perp AB. Two lines perpendicular to the same line are parallel. Therefore BP∥CH,PC∥BH.BP\parallel CH, \qquad PC\parallel BH. Thus BPCHBPCH is a parallelogram whenever its four vertices are distinct.

Step 3: the parallelogram supplies a common midpoint. The diagonals of a parallelogram bisect each other. Hence the midpoint of BCBC is also the midpoint of PHPH. By the centre-to-chord lemma, the condition OA′⊥BCOA'\perp BC says exactly that A′A' is the midpoint of BCBC. Consequently A′A' is also the midpoint of PHPH.

Step 4: apply the midpoint theorem once more. In triangle APHAPH, the point OO is the midpoint of APAP, and A′A' is the midpoint of PHPH. The segment joining these midpoints is parallel to the third side and half as long. More precisely, OA′→=12AH→.\overrightarrow{OA'}=\frac12\overrightarrow{AH}. Multiplying by 22 gives the required vector identity and therefore the length identity AH=2OA′AH=2\,OA'.

There are only two collapsed cases. If the right angle is at BB, then H=BH=B and the antipode of AA is P=CP=C; hence PHPH and BCBC are the same segment with opposite orientations. If the right angle is at CC, then H=CH=C and P=BP=B, with the same conclusion. If the right angle is at AA, then H=AH=A, the chord BCBC is a diameter and A′=OA'=O; both vectors in the identity are zero. Thus the midpoint and vector conclusions remain valid in every right-triangle case. The noncollapsed diagram displays the mechanism most clearly.

Example A.1 · Reading the vector identity

The statement AH→=2OA′→\overrightarrow{AH}=2\overrightarrow{OA'} contains more information than the length equation AH=2OA′AH=2\,OA'. It says simultaneously that

  • AHAH and OA′OA' are parallel;

  • they point in the same direction;

  • the first arrow has twice the length of the second.

This is the same three-part reading used throughout Lectures 1 and 2: an algebraic equality between arrows records direction, orientation and scale at once.

A.3  Euler's line from similar triangles

Theorem A.5 · Euler line theorem

In a nonequilateral triangle, the circumcentre OO, centroid GG and orthocentre HH are collinear. The centroid lies between the other two centres and GH=2OG.\boxed{GH=2\,OG.} The line through them is called the Euler line. In an equilateral triangle the three centres coincide, so they do not determine a unique line.

Proof

Because ABCABC is nonequilateral, choose the name AA for a vertex for which AB≠ACAB\ne AC; such a vertex must exist. Then AOAO cannot be the altitude from AA: if it were, both AOAO and the perpendicular bisector of BCBC would be the same line, forcing AA to lie on that perpendicular bisector and hence AB=ACAB=AC. Since A,O,PA,O,P are collinear while A,HA,H is the altitude, the points A,P,HA,P,H are not collinear. Thus triangle APHAPH is nondegenerate. Let G′=AA′∩OH.G'=AA'\cap OH. The point A′A' is the midpoint of BCBC, so AA′AA' is a median of ABCABC.

By Theorem A.4, the lines AHAH and OA′OA' are parallel and AH/OA′=2AH/OA'=2. At G′G', the lines AA′AA' and OHOH cross. Hence the triangles AHG′AHG' and A′OG′A'OG' have equal corresponding angles:

  • the angles at G′G' are vertical angles;

  • the angles made with AHAH equal the corresponding angles made with the parallel line OA′OA'.

The two triangles are therefore similar. Their corresponding sides give AG′G′A′=HG′G′O=AHA′O=2.\frac{AG'}{G'A'} =\frac{HG'}{G'O} =\frac{AH}{A'O} =2.

Thus G′G' lies two thirds of the way from AA to the midpoint A′A' of BCBC. Lecture 3 proved that this point is the centroid. Therefore G′=GG'=G. Since G′G' was chosen on OHOH, the three points O,G,HO,G,H are collinear; and the second ratio gives GH=2OG.GH=2\,OG.

If ABCABC is equilateral, every median is also a perpendicular bisector and an altitude. Hence O=G=HO=G=H. There is then no single distinguished line through the three coincident points.

A.4  How the picture generates the complex formula

Now assign complex coordinates a,b,c,o,h,pa,b,c,o,h,p to A,B,C,O,H,PA,B,C,O,H,P. Every line of the calculation below comes directly from a midpoint in the first diagram.

Because OO is the midpoint of the diameter APAP, o=a+p2,sop=2o−a.o=\frac{a+p}{2}, \qquad\text{so}\qquad p=2o-a. Because BCBC and PHPH have the same midpoint A′A', b+c2=p+h2.\frac{b+c}{2}=\frac{p+h}{2}. Multiply by 22 and substitute p=2o−ap=2o-a: b+c=p+h,h=b+c−p,=b+c−(2o−a),h=a+b+c−2o.\begin{aligned} b+c&=p+h,\\ h&=b+c-p,\\ &=b+c-(2o-a),\\ h&=a+b+c-2o. \end{aligned} Thus h=a+b+c−2o.\boxed{h=a+b+c-2o.} The centroid has coordinate g=a+b+c3.g=\frac{a+b+c}{3}. Therefore h−o=a+b+c−3o=3(a+b+c3−o),\begin{aligned} h-o &=a+b+c-3o\\ &=3\left(\frac{a+b+c}{3}-o\right), \end{aligned} and hence h−o=3(g−o).\boxed{h-o=3(g-o).}

This is exactly the identity derived in Lecture 3 by placing the circumcentre at the origin. The synthetic proof explains where it comes from: the coefficient 22 in h=a+b+c−2oh=a+b+c-2o is forced by the two midpoint relations in the diameter construction, and the coefficient 33 is forced by the centroid's average of three vertices.

💡 One theorem, read in two languages

The synthetic statement GH=2OGGH=2\,OG and the algebraic statement h−o=3(g−o)h-o=3(g-o) are equivalent. Indeed, h−o=3(g−o)⇔h−g=2(g−o).h-o=3(g-o) \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad h-g=2(g-o). The final equation says that the arrow from GG to HH is twice the arrow from OO to GG. It records both collinearity and the ratio in a single line.

A.5  Problems for further study

The first problem checks every point in a concrete triangle. The next four problems build new geometry from the formulas and constructions already established in Lectures 1–3.

☠ Problem A.1

Let a=0,b=4,c=1+3i.a=0,\qquad b=4,\qquad c=1+3i. Find the circumcentre OO, centroid GG, orthocentre HH, and the point PP antipodal to AA. If A′A' is the midpoint of BCBC, verify directly that A′A' is also the midpoint of PHPH, that AH→=2OA′→\overrightarrow{AH}=2\overrightarrow{OA'}, and that GH→=2OG→\overrightarrow{GH}=2\overrightarrow{OG}.

☠ Problem A.2

Suppose the circumcircle of ABCABC is the unit circle centred at the origin. Use the chord-reflection formula from Lecture 3 to prove that the reflection of the orthocentre HH in the side BCBC lies on the circumcircle. Find its coordinate in terms of a,b,ca,b,c.

☠ Problem A.3

Let NN be the midpoint of OHOH, and let the circumradius of ABCABC be RR. Prove that the following nine points lie on the circle with centre NN and radius R/2R/2:

  • the three side midpoints;

  • the three midpoints of AH,BH,CHAH,BH,CH;

  • the three feet of the altitudes.

This circle is called the nine-point circle. Some of the nine named points may coincide in a special triangle.

☠ Problem A.4

Let A1,B1,C1A_1,B_1,C_1 be the midpoints of BC,CA,ABBC,CA,AB, respectively. Find the circumcentre, centroid and orthocentre of the medial triangle A1B1C1A_1B_1C_1. Prove that, when ABCABC is nonequilateral, its Euler line is the same line as the Euler line of ABCABC.

☠ Problem A.5

Fix two distinct points A,BA,B on a circle with centre OO and radius RR. A third point CC moves around the same circle, avoiding AA and BB, and HH is the orthocentre of ABCABC. Determine the path traced by HH. State exactly which points of that path are omitted.
