☠ Starting question

How can we move a point by turning it and changing its distance from a fixed centre? Can the instruction for doing this itself be represented by a point?

1.1  The language of a drawing

A point records a position. The straight line through two distinct points AA and BB extends indefinitely in both directions. The segment ABAB is the part between them, and its length is a nonnegative real number, also written ABAB when the meaning is clear. The ray OAOA begins at OO, passes through AA, and continues beyond it.

Two rays from the same point determine an angle. A complete turn measures 360∘360^\circ; a half-turn measures 180∘180^\circ; a quarter-turn measures 90∘90^\circ. Rays forming a quarter-turn are perpendicular. Parallel lines have the same direction, or opposite directions, and do not meet.

When a turn is an instruction, its direction matters. We call an anticlockwise turn positive and a clockwise turn negative. Thus +60∘+60^\circ and −60∘-60^\circ are different instructions. A turn of 420∘420^\circ has the same final direction as a turn of 60∘60^\circ, because the first includes one additional complete turn. We shall say this in words whenever needed.

An ordinary angle of a triangle is always between 0∘0^\circ and 180∘180^\circ. A directed turn can be negative or can include several revolutions. We will use these two ideas for different purposes and will not silently interchange them.

1.1.1  Three elementary facts about triangles

A triangle consists of three noncollinear points and their joining segments. Its three interior angles add to 180∘180^\circ. One way to see this is to draw through one vertex a line parallel to the opposite side. The other two angles reappear there as alternate angles, and the three angles together fill a straight angle.

A triangle is isosceles when two sides are equal. The angles opposite those sides are equal. It is equilateral when all three sides are equal; all three angles then equal 60∘60^\circ.

Two triangles are similar when their corresponding angles are equal and their corresponding side lengths have one common ratio. Two equal corresponding angles suffice: the third is then determined by the angle sum, and parallel-line constructions give the proportional sides. The order of the letters matters. For example, △OAB∼△OCDmeansOCOA=ODOB=CDAB.\triangle OAB\sim\triangle OCD \quad\text{means}\quad \frac{OC}{OA}=\frac{OD}{OB}=\frac{CD}{AB}. The correspondences are O↔OO\leftrightarrow O, A↔CA\leftrightarrow C and B↔DB\leftrightarrow D. Writing down the correspondence before the ratios avoids reversing a scale factor.

1.2  Transformations: what changes, and what stays fixed?

A transformation of the plane is a rule assigning an image point to every point. If the rule sends PP to P′P', the prime is read “PP prime”. Applying the rule to every point of a figure gives the image figure.

The same idea may be written compactly as T:ℝ2→ℝ2,P↦T(P).T:\mathbb R^2\longrightarrow\mathbb R^2, \qquad P\longmapsto T(P). Here ℝ2\mathbb R^2 means the set of all ordered pairs of real numbers, and hence the set of all points of the coordinate plane. The notation says only that every input point has one output point. It does not yet say whether lengths, angles or shapes are preserved. In everyday language, a transformation is a rule for moving every point of a figure.

An isometry preserves the distance between every pair of points. Its image has the same size and shape as the original. A similarity of scale factor k>0k>0 multiplies every distance by kk. Its image has the same shape, with a possibly different size. If d(A,B)d(A,B) denotes the distance between AA and BB, these two conditions are d(T(A),T(B))=d(A,B)for an isometry,d(T(A),T(B))=kd(A,B)for a similarity of scale factor k.\begin{array}{rcll} d\bigl(T(A),T(B)\bigr)&=&d(A,B)&\text{for an isometry},\\[2pt] d\bigl(T(A),T(B)\bigr)&=&k\,d(A,B)&\text{for a similarity of scale factor }k. \end{array} The equations must hold for every pair of points A,BA,B, not merely for the vertices shown in one drawing.

💡 Three levels of information

An isometry preserves every distance. A similarity multiplies every distance by one fixed positive number, so it preserves shape. A general transformation need do neither. Translation, rotation and reflection are isometries; a homothety is a similarity and is an isometry only when its scale factor is 11.

Our aim is to place an arithmetic on this same plane so that movements, turns and uniform changes of scale can be calculated together. The points of the plane will not change when we later call it the complex plane; the new feature will be the arithmetic carried by those points.

1.2.1  Translation: the same displacement everywhere

To translate a figure, specify a displacement: for example, four units to the right and one unit upward. Move every point by that same displacement. A triangle keeps its side lengths and angles, and each joining segment PP′PP' is parallel to the others and has the same length and direction.

1.2.2  Rotation: the same turn about one centre

Fix a point OO and a directed angle θ\theta. A rotation about OO sends PP to the point P′P' satisfying two conditions: OP′=OPOP'=OP, and the ray OPOP turns through θ\theta to reach OP′OP'. The centre OO stays fixed. Every other point travels along a circle centred at OO. A circle is the set of points at one fixed distance, its radius, from its centre.

For two points A,BA,B, rotation leaves OAOA, OBOB and the angle between them unchanged. The two triangles before and after rotation are congruent, so A′B′=ABA'B'=AB. Rotation is therefore an isometry. Notice that the points need not be the same distance from OO: it is the angle of turn that is common.

1.2.3  Dilation or homothety: the same scale about one centre

Fix OO and a positive number kk. Put P′P' on the ray OPOP so that OP′=kOPOP'=k\,OP. This transformation is a dilation, also called a homothety, with centre OO and scale factor kk. We write HO,kH_{O,k}.

The triangles OABOAB and OA′B′OA'B' have the same angle at OO, and OA′OA=OB′OB=k.\frac{OA'}{OA}=\frac{OB'}{OB}=k. The side-angle-side similarity criterion gives A′B′=kABA'B'=k\,AB. If one endpoint of a segment is OO, the same conclusion follows directly from OP′=kOPOP'=k\,OP. Thus every distance, including a distance not measured from OO, is multiplied by kk.

If a third point CC has image C′C', then the complete ratio record is OA′OA=OB′OB=OC′OC=A′B′AB=B′C′BC=C′A′CA=k.\frac{OA'}{OA}=\frac{OB'}{OB}=\frac{OC'}{OC} =\frac{A'B'}{AB}=\frac{B'C'}{BC}=\frac{C'A'}{CA}=k. Corresponding sides are parallel: AB∥A′B′,BC∥B′C′,CA∥C′A′.AB\parallel A'B',\qquad BC\parallel B'C',\qquad CA\parallel C'A'. These statements describe the homothety in the direction ABC→A′B′C′ABC\to A'B'C'. If instead one records the old-to-new ratio r=ABA′B′=BCB′C′=CAC′A′,r=\frac{AB}{A'B'}=\frac{BC}{B'C'}=\frac{CA}{C'A'}, then r=1/kr=1/k. Both conventions occur in geometry, so the direction of the transformation must always be stated.

If k>1k>1, the figure expands. If 0<k<10<k<1, it contracts. If k=1k=1, it is unchanged. A projection from a point onto two parallel screens is a useful model: similar triangles explain why moving the screen changes all lengths by the same factor.

A negative signed ratio can be treated as a positive dilation followed by a half-turn. For the moment, our scale factors will be positive, so that scale and direction are kept separate.

1.2.4  Reflection and orientation

Reflection in a line sends each point to its mirror image across that line. It is an isometry, but it reverses orientation. To understand this word, traverse the vertices of a triangle. They may be visited in clockwise order or in anticlockwise order. Translation, rotation and positive dilation preserve that order; reflection reverses it. A similarity preserving orientation is called direct. This distinction will prevent us from confusing a turn with a mirror image.

1.3  Spiral similarity: rotation and dilation together

Suppose two nonzero directed segments are given, so A≠BA\ne B and C≠DC\ne D, and we want A↦C,B↦D.A\longmapsto C,\qquad B\longmapsto D. The image segment may have a different direction and a different length. The required change of direction is the turn from the ray ABAB to the ray CDCD, and the required scale factor is k=CDAB.k=\frac{CD}{AB}. These two numbers tell us what turn and scale are needed. They do not yet tell us the point about which both operations should be performed.

Further diagrams: finding the centre of a spiral similarity · Yufei Zhao, Three Lemmas in Geometry, §2.

Definition 1.1 · Spiral similarity

A spiral similarity with centre OO, scale factor k>0k>0 and directed angle θ\theta is a dilation by kk and a rotation through θ\theta, both about OO.

For P≠OP\ne O, its image P′P' is obtained by replacing the distance OPOP by kOPk\,OP and adding θ\theta to the direction of OPOP. The centre remains fixed. The name does not require a point to travel along a spiral: the transformation specifies the final point.

Does the order matter? If we rotate first, the distance remains OPOP, after which the dilation makes it kOPk\,OP. If we dilate first, the ray remains OPOP, after which the rotation changes its direction by θ\theta. Both routes have the same final distance and direction. They therefore have the same endpoint. This argument relies on using the same centre for both operations.

Example 1.1 · The transformation carrying one ray-point to another

Relative to a fixed origin OO, BB has distance r2r_2 and direction θ2\theta_2, while DD has distance r1r_1 and direction θ1\theta_1. Find the required scale and turn.

Solution

The scale kk must satisfy kr2=r1kr_2=r_1, so k=r1/r2k=r_1/r_2. The turn θ\theta must satisfy θ2+θ=θ1\theta_2+\theta=\theta_1, so we may take θ=θ1−θ2\theta=\theta_1-\theta_2.

For instance, if r2=4r_2=4, r1=2r_1=2, θ2=140∘\theta_2=140^\circ and θ1=35∘\theta_1=35^\circ, then k=1/2k=1/2 and θ=−105∘\theta=-105^\circ. We contract distances by one half and turn clockwise through 105∘105^\circ. The turn 255∘255^\circ anticlockwise has the same endpoint.

1.4  Why finding a common centre is a real question

Suppose that AA is to move to CC and BB to DD. The ratio CD/ABCD/AB suggests a scale, and the change from the direction ABAB to the direction CDCD suggests a turn. But a spiral similarity must perform both actions about one common centre OO.

A guess is to intersect the lines joining corresponding points. That works for a pure dilation, because O,A,CO,A,C lie on one line in a dilation. It need not work when a rotation is present. We must check OCOA=ODOB\frac{OC}{OA}=\frac{OD}{OB} and check that the turn from OAOA to OCOC is the same as the turn from OBOB to ODOD. A picture that looks plausible is not enough.

1.4.1  A first guess that fails

The failure can occur even when the two turns agree perfectly. Let X=AC∩BDX=AC\cap BD in the following example. The points A,X,CA,X,C lie on one line with XA=XCXA=XC, while B,X,DB,X,D lie on another line with XD=2XBXD=2XB.

The turn from XAXA to XCXC is a half-turn, and so is the turn from XBXB to XDXD. To send AA to CC, however, the scale must be kA=XCXA=1.k_A=\frac{XC}{XA}=1. With that scale, the image B*B^* satisfies XB*=XBXB^*=XB, whereas XD=2XBXD=2XB. To send BB to DD we would need kB=XDXB=2.k_B=\frac{XD}{XB}=2. One transformation cannot have both scale factors. More generally, when the two joining lines meet at a candidate XX, making AA reach CC forces XB*=XCXAXB.XB^*=\frac{XC}{XA}\,XB. Thus B*=DB^*=D precisely when XCXA=XDXB,\frac{XC}{XA}=\frac{XD}{XB}, provided the two required turns also agree. Equivalently, in this crossing arrangement the triangles XABXAB and XCDXCD must be similar. This is the test hidden behind our original question: a common centre must satisfy the scale condition and the turn condition simultaneously.

1.4.2  The circle fact behind a construction

A chord is a segment joining two points of a circle. An arc is a portion of the circumference between two points. A quadrilateral inscribed in a circle has all four vertices on the circle. We will use two facts about a circle. Angles standing on the same chord and having their vertices on the same side of that chord are equal. Angles standing on that chord from opposite sides add to 180∘180^\circ. Equivalently, opposite angles of a quadrilateral inscribed in a circle add to 180∘180^\circ.

Here is the reason. Join the endpoints of the chord and a point on the circle to the circle's centre KK. The resulting triangles have two equal radii and are isosceles. Let the radius from the point on the circle split its angle into the two parts α\alpha and β\beta. The corresponding angles at KK are 180∘−2α180^\circ-2\alpha and 180∘−2β180^\circ-2\beta. Subtracting their sum from 360∘360^\circ shows that the remaining central angle is 2(α+β)2(\alpha+\beta). Thus the angle at the circumference is one half the central angle subtending the opposite arc. If the centre lies outside the angle, the same calculation uses a difference instead of a sum. Points on the same arc give the same angle; switching to the other arc replaces its measure by 180∘180^\circ minus that angle.

The second form needed in the construction is worth isolating because it often shortens an angle chase.

Proposition 1.2 · Exterior angle of a cyclic quadrilateral

Let A,B,C,DA,B,C,D lie on a circle in that order, and extend CDCD beyond DD to EE. Then the exterior angle at DD equals the opposite interior angle at BB: ∠ADE=∠ABC.\angle ADE=\angle ABC.

Proof

Opposite interior angles of the cyclic quadrilateral satisfy ∠ABC+∠CDA=180∘.\angle ABC+\angle CDA=180^\circ. The rays DCDC and DEDE are opposite, so the adjacent angles at DD satisfy ∠CDA+∠ADE=180∘.\angle CDA+\angle ADE=180^\circ. Both ∠ABC\angle ABC and ∠ADE\angle ADE are therefore equal to 180∘−∠CDA180^\circ-\angle CDA.

1.4.3  A two-circle construction, with the correspondence fixed

Consider the configuration drawn below. Let PP be the intersection of the lines ACAC and BDBD. Draw the circle through A,B,PA,B,P and the circle through C,D,PC,D,P. Let their second intersection be OO. The letters specify the intended correspondence A→CA\to C, B→DB\to D.

Read the ordinary angles in this particular drawing. In the first circle, the angles OABOAB and OPBOPB stand on chord OBOB from the same side, so ∠OAB=∠OPB.\angle OAB=\angle OPB. Because B,P,DB,P,D lie on a line with PP between B,DB,D, ∠OPB=180∘−∠OPD.\angle OPB=180^\circ-\angle OPD. In the second circle, opposite angles OPDOPD and OCDOCD add to 180∘180^\circ. Combining the three statements gives ∠OAB=180∘−∠OPD=∠OCD.\angle OAB=180^\circ-\angle OPD=\angle OCD. For the second pair, BB and PP lie on opposite sides of chord OAOA in the first circle, whereas DD and PP lie on the same side of chord OCOC in the second. Using the straight line APCAPC between these two circle facts gives ∠OBA=180∘−∠OPA=∠OPC=∠ODC.\angle OBA=180^\circ-\angle OPA=\angle OPC=\angle ODC. Therefore △OAB∼△OCD\triangle OAB\sim\triangle OCD, and OCOA=ODOB=CDAB.\frac{OC}{OA}=\frac{OD}{OB}=\frac{CD}{AB}. Both triangles have the same orientation in the drawing. To see why the turns agree, measure the directions of the four rays from any fixed reference ray. Equality of the angle from OAOA to OBOB and the angle from OCOC to ODOD says that subtracting the two corresponding ray directions gives the same result. Rearranging says that the turn from OAOA to OCOC equals the turn from OBOB to ODOD, up to complete turns. Thus OO supplies both the common scale and the common turn.

💡 The angle chase at a glance

The two chains used above are ∠OAB=∠OPB=180∘−∠OPD=∠OCD\angle OAB=\angle OPB=180^\circ-\angle OPD=\angle OCD and ∠OBA=180∘−∠OPA=∠OPC=∠ODC.\angle OBA=180^\circ-\angle OPA=\angle OPC=\angle ODC. The first chain gives the pair marked α\alpha in the figure; the second gives the pair marked β\beta. Two equal corresponding angles prove the triangle similarity.

The correspondence is O↔OO\leftrightarrow O, A↔CA\leftrightarrow C, B↔DB\leftrightarrow D. Hence the forward transformation A↦C,B↦DA\longmapsto C,\qquad B\longmapsto D has scale factor k=OCOA=ODOB=CDAB.k=\frac{OC}{OA}=\frac{OD}{OB}=\frac{CD}{AB}. Its inverse sends C↦AC\mapsto A and D↦BD\mapsto B, reverses the turn, and has scale factor 1k=OAOC=OBOD=ABCD.\frac1k=\frac{OA}{OC}=\frac{OB}{OD}=\frac{AB}{CD}. This explains why a ratio written in the opposite order describes the inverse motion rather than the forward one.

Proposition 1.3 · Uniqueness of the centre

A non-identity spiral similarity has exactly one centre.

Proof

Its given centre exists. Suppose two distinct points OO and O′O' were both centres of the same transformation. Every centre is fixed, so the endpoints of the segment OO′OO' would both remain fixed.

If the scale factor were k≠1k\ne1, the length OO′OO' would have to become kOO′k\,OO'. Since both endpoints remain fixed, that length would also have to remain OO′OO'. This is impossible because OO′>0OO'>0.

It remains to consider k=1k=1. A non-identity spiral similarity with this scale factor is a rotation through an angle that is not a whole number of complete turns. Viewed from OO, the nonzero ray OO′OO' would have to change direction. Yet O′O' is fixed, so the ray would remain unchanged. This is again impossible. Therefore the two centres cannot be distinct.

The identity transformation is the excluded case: it fixes every point, so every point can be regarded as a centre. A nonzero translation, considered in Lecture 2, has no finite centre at all.

The construction is a useful geometric model, not a claim that every pair of segments produces this exact arrangement of circles and interior angles. Parallel joining lines or coincident intersections require separate treatment. In Lecture 2 an algebraic calculation will find the centre whenever a finite centre exists, and will identify the translation case as well.

1.5  Cartesian coordinates describe position

Choose an origin OO, a horizontal axis, a vertical axis perpendicular to it, and one unit of length on both axes. The point (x,y)(x,y) is reached by moving xx units horizontally and yy units vertically. Negative coordinates indicate leftward or downward movement.

For a point P=(x,y)P=(x,y), the perpendicular projections to the axes form a right triangle with side lengths |x|,|y|\left|x\right|,\left|y\right| and hypotenuse OPOP. The Pythagorean theorem gives OP=x2+y2.OP=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}. For completeness, the theorem follows by placing four copies of a right triangle of legs a,ba,b inside a square of side a+ba+b. The remaining central square has side equal to the hypotenuse cc. Comparing areas gives (a+b)2=4(ab/2)+c2(a+b)^2=4(ab/2)+c^2, hence c2=a2+b2c^2=a^2+b^2.

1.6  Polar coordinates describe distance and direction

Instead of asking how far to move horizontally and vertically, ask how far the point is from OO and in which direction it lies. Write P=[r,θ],r=OP≥0,P=[r,\theta],\qquad r=OP\ge0, where θ\theta is measured from the positive horizontal ray. These are polar coordinates. The origin OO is the reference point, and the positive horizontal ray is the reference ray. A direction needs a ray rather than an undirected line: the positive and negative halves of the horizontal axis point in opposite directions. Changing the reference point or the reference ray changes the polar entries, although the geometric point itself has not moved.

1.6.1  Sine and cosine from the right triangle

For an acute angle θ\theta in a right triangle, define cosθ=adjacent sidehypotenuse,sinθ=opposite sidehypotenuse.\cos\theta=\frac{\text{adjacent side}}{\text{hypotenuse}},\qquad \sin\theta=\frac{\text{opposite side}}{\text{hypotenuse}}. Similar right triangles give the same ratios, so these definitions depend only on the angle. Applied to the preceding diagram, they give x=rcosθ,y=rsinθ.\boxed{x=r\cos\theta,\qquad y=r\sin\theta.} For directions outside the first quadrant, define cosine and sine as the horizontal and vertical coordinates of the point on the unit circle, the circle of radius 11 about OO. The same equations then hold with the correct signs in every quadrant. In particular, cos2θ+sin2θ=1.\cos^2\theta+\sin^2\theta=1. This is the Pythagorean theorem on the unit circle.

An isosceles right triangle with legs 1,11,1 has hypotenuse 2\sqrt2, so cos45∘=sin45∘=1/2\cos45^\circ=\sin45^\circ=1/\sqrt2. Bisect an equilateral triangle of side 22. 1.6.2  Returning from Cartesian to polar coordinates

For P=(x,y)≠(0,0)P=(x,y)\ne(0,0), first find r=x2+y2r=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}. Then choose a direction satisfying cosθ=x/r,sinθ=y/r.\cos\theta=x/r,\qquad\sin\theta=y/r. The signs of both coordinates determine the quadrant. Knowing only y/xy/x is not enough, because a ray and its opposite have the same ratio.

Example 1.2 · Four points with the same distance

Solution

All have r=1+3=2r=\sqrt{1+3}=2. The reference angle is 60∘60^\circ, since the coordinate magnitudes divided by 22 are 1/21/2 and 3/2\sqrt3/2. 1.6.3  An inverse tangent gives a reference angle

When the angle is not one of the familiar special angles, we can specify it using inverse tangent. For t>0t>0, arctant\arctan t denotes the acute angle whose tangent is tt. 1.6.4  Uniqueness, whole turns and the origin

A nonzero point has many polar descriptions. For example, [2,30∘]=[2,390∘]=[2,−330∘].[2,30^\circ]=[2,390^\circ]=[2,-330^\circ]. If we require 0∘≤θ<360∘0^\circ\le\theta<360^\circ, it has exactly one description. The final endpoint 360∘360^\circ is excluded because it duplicates 0∘0^\circ.

The origin is different: [0,θ][0,\theta] is the origin for every angle. There is no distinguished ray from OO to itself. Consequently the origin has no direction or argument. Restricting the angle range does not repair that lack of uniqueness at r=0r=0.

1.6.5  Radians: measuring an angle by an arc

On a circle of radius rr, an angle cutting off an arc of length ss has radian measure s/rs/r. Similar circles have arc lengths proportional to their radii, so this ratio depends only on the angle. A complete circle has circumference 2πr2\pi r, hence a complete turn is 2π2\pi radians. Thus 180∘=π,90∘=π2,60∘=π3,30∘=π6.180^\circ=\pi,\qquad90^\circ=\frac\pi2,\qquad60^\circ=\frac\pi3, \qquad30^\circ=\frac\pi6. For a directed angle, we attach a positive sign to an anticlockwise turn and a negative sign to a clockwise turn, exactly as we did with degrees. To convert degrees to radians, multiply by π/180\pi/180; to convert radians to degrees, multiply by 180/π180/\pi. An angle without a degree sign will be measured in radians after this point. We will continue to use degrees when they make a construction easier to read.

1.7  A point can be read as an instruction

Let EE be the point one unit to the right of OO. Its Cartesian coordinates are (1,0)(1,0) and its polar data are [1,0][1,0]. Given a nonzero point P=[r,θ]P=[r,\theta], there is one spiral similarity about OO carrying EE to PP: multiply distances by rr and turn through θ\theta. Different choices of θ\theta differing by complete turns produce the same transformation.

We can therefore read PP in two ways: as a position, or as an instruction to scale by rr and turn through θ\theta. Denote that instruction by SPS_P. What does it do to Q=[s,ϕ]Q=[s,\phi]? The old distance ss becomes rsrs, and the old direction ϕ\phi becomes ϕ+θ\phi+\theta. Thus SP(Q)=[rs,θ+ϕ].S_P(Q)=[rs,\theta+\phi].

Definition 1.4 · Multiplication of points

For nonzero points, define [r,θ][s,ϕ]=[rs,θ+ϕ].\boxed{[r,\theta]\,[s,\phi]=[rs,\theta+\phi].} Define the product to be OO if either factor is OO. The product PQPQ is the image of QQ under the spiral similarity described by PP.

From this definition onward, juxtaposition of polar-labelled points, as in PQPQ, denotes their product. When the same two letters denote a geometric segment or its length, the surrounding sentence will say so explicitly. This operation is well defined. Replacing an input direction by that direction plus any number of complete turns changes the output direction by the same number of complete turns. The output point is unchanged. The separate rule for OO avoids assigning it a direction.

Example 1.3 · Forward and reverse instructions

Let C=[r1,θ1],A=[r2,θ2],r1,r2>0.C=[r_1,\theta_1],\qquad A=[r_2,\theta_2],\qquad r_1,r_2>0. The following diagram uses OO as the reference point and the positive horizontal ray as the reference ray.

Define the instruction U=[r2r1,θ2−θ1].U=\left[\frac{r_2}{r_1},\theta_2-\theta_1\right]. Applying it to CC gives the calculation line by line: UC=[r2r1,θ2−θ1][r1,θ1]=[r2r1r1,(θ2−θ1)+θ1]=[r2,θ2]=A.\begin{aligned} U\,C &=\left[\frac{r_2}{r_1},\theta_2-\theta_1\right][r_1,\theta_1]\\ &=\left[\frac{r_2}{r_1}\,r_1, (\theta_2-\theta_1)+\theta_1\right]\\ &=[r_2,\theta_2]=A. \end{aligned} Thus the radial entries multiply and the direction entries add. The reverse action is generally a different instruction. It is V=[r1r2,θ1−θ2],V=\left[\frac{r_1}{r_2},\theta_1-\theta_2\right], and a direct calculation gives VA=CV\,A=C. The scale factors are reciprocal and the turns have opposite directions.

The next calculation deliberately returns to the letters of Example 1.1. There the ratio and turn were found geometrically; here the same data are checked using the new multiplication.

Example 1.4 · Recovering the original scale-and-turn calculation

Take B=[r2,θ2]B=[r_2,\theta_2] and D=[r1,θ1]D=[r_1,\theta_1], with positive distances. Let P=[r1r2,θ1−θ2].P=\left[\frac{r_1}{r_2},\theta_1-\theta_2\right]. Then, line by line, PB=[r1r2,θ1−θ2][r2,θ2]=[r2r1r2,θ2+(θ1−θ2)]=[r1,θ1]=D.\begin{aligned} P\,B&=\left[\frac{r_1}{r_2},\theta_1-\theta_2\right][r_2,\theta_2]\\ &=\left[r_2\frac{r_1}{r_2},\ \theta_2+(\theta_1-\theta_2)\right]\\ &=[r_1,\theta_1]=D. \end{aligned} The multiplication rule has recorded exactly the geometric construction: multiply the old distance by the required ratio and add the required change in direction.

Example 1.5 · An actual product

[2,30∘][3,60∘]=[6,90∘]=(0,6).[2,30^\circ][3,60^\circ]=[6,90^\circ]=(0,6). The answer lies on the positive vertical axis. The distances multiply, and the directions add. The entries of the polar pairs do not multiply component by component.

⚠ The centre is part of the motion

The pair [r,θ][r,\theta] records a scale factor and a turn, but it does not record a centre. Our multiplication rule describes composition because every instruction in this section uses the same centre OO. The same scale and turn performed about a different point generally give a different transformation. Consequently, the rule “multiply the radial entries and add the directions” cannot be applied blindly to motions with different centres.

For a concrete warning, place the points −1,0,1,2,5-1,0,1,2,5 on the horizontal axis. A half-turn about 00 sends 11 to −1-1. A second half-turn about 22 sends −1-1 to 55, because 22 is the midpoint of −1-1 and 55. Although the two turn amounts add to one complete turn, the point 11 does not return to itself: the centres were different.

1.8  The familiar multiplication laws, proved geometrically

Let P=[r,θ]P=[r,\theta], Q=[s,ϕ]Q=[s,\phi] and R=[t,ψ]R=[t,\psi] be nonzero points.

Closure. The product [rs,θ+ϕ][rs,\theta+\phi] is another point of the same plane. We do not leave the plane by multiplying.

Commutativity. Real multiplication and real addition are commutative, so PQ=[rs,θ+ϕ]=[sr,ϕ+θ]=QP.PQ=[rs,\theta+\phi]=[sr,\phi+\theta]=QP. Two rotations and dilations about OO can be performed in either order.

Associativity. Keep the intermediate product visible: (PQ)R=[rs,θ+ϕ][t,ψ]=[(rs)t,(θ+ϕ)+ψ],P(QR)=[r,θ][st,ϕ+ψ]=[r(st),θ+(ϕ+ψ)].\begin{aligned} (PQ)R&=[rs,\theta+\phi][t,\psi] =[(rs)t,(\theta+\phi)+\psi],\\ P(QR)&=[r,\theta][st,\phi+\psi] =[r(st),\theta+(\phi+\psi)]. \end{aligned} The two distances are equal because (rs)t=r(st)(rs)t=r(st), and the two directions are equal because (θ+ϕ)+ψ=θ+(ϕ+ψ)(\theta+\phi)+\psi=\theta+(\phi+\psi). Therefore (PQ)R=P(QR)(PQ)R=P(QR).

Identity. The point E=[1,0]E=[1,0] satisfies PE=[r⋅1,θ+0]=P,EP=P.PE=[r\cdot1,\theta+0]=P,\qquad EP=P. Its instruction is to leave distance and direction unchanged. We henceforth call this point 11.

Inverse. To undo the instruction [r,θ][r,\theta], scale by 1/r1/r and turn by −θ-\theta. The inverse point is P−1=[1/r,−θ],PP−1=[1,0]=1.P^{-1}=[1/r,-\theta],\qquad PP^{-1}=[1,0]=1. For [4,70∘][4,70^\circ], the inverse is [1/4,−70∘][1/4,-70^\circ]: contract by four and turn back clockwise.

The origin. We call OO the point 00. Our rule gives 0P=P0=00P=P0=0. Commutativity and associativity still hold when a factor is 00, since any such product equals 00. But 00 has no inverse: 0Q=00Q=0 for every QQ, so it can never equal 11.

These are substantial reasons to call the operation multiplication. One further property is still missing: compatibility with addition. We have not defined addition yet, so we cannot claim the distributive law before doing that work.

1.9  A copy of the real line in this arithmetic

Represent a positive real number aa by the point [a,0][a,0] on the positive horizontal ray. Represent a negative real number −a-a, where a>0a>0, by [a,180∘][a,180^\circ]. Then [a,0][b,0]=[ab,0],[a,180∘][b,0]=[ab,180∘],[a,180∘][b,180∘]=[ab,360∘]=[ab,0].\begin{aligned} [a,0][b,0]&=[ab,0],\\ [a,180^\circ][b,0]&=[ab,180^\circ],\\ [a,180^\circ][b,180^\circ]&=[ab,360^\circ]=[ab,0]. \end{aligned} So positive times positive is positive, negative times positive is negative, and negative times negative is positive. The final rule records two half-turns making one complete turn. Plane multiplication agrees with ordinary real multiplication on the horizontal axis. In Lecture 2 we will make precise the identification between a real number and its corresponding horizontal point.

Let J=[1,90∘]=(0,1)J=[1,90^\circ]=(0,1). Then J2=[1,180∘]=(−1,0).J^2=[1,180^\circ]=(-1,0). We have found a point whose square is the real point −1-1. There is no geometric impossibility here: two quarter-turns make a half-turn. In the next lecture this point will receive the name ii.

💡 The bridge to the next lecture

The plane is already doing two jobs. A point [r,θ][r,\theta] is a location, and the same point can encode an origin-centred rotation and dilation. Polar multiplication composes those scale-and-turn instructions.

Translation is the remaining motion. It is naturally described by a displacement, and displacements will lead to addition. Once addition and multiplication are both available, an arbitrary-centre motion can be carried out in three visible stages: move the chosen centre to the origin, apply the scale and turn there, and move the origin back. This is how one plane will eventually calculate translations, rotations and dilations together.

💡 The geometric idea to retain

A point away from the origin can represent a rotation and a dilation. Composing those instructions produces multiplication: lengths multiply, directions add. The origin and the point one unit to its right have already become 00 and 11.
