☠ Starting question

A midpoint is the average of two positions. What replaces an ordinary average when the point divides a segment unequally? How can three numbers locate a point in a triangle, and what geometric meaning should those numbers have?

In Section 3.8, the centroid was the average g=(a+b+c)/3g=(a+b+c)/3: all three vertices received the same weight. We now let those weights differ. The key is to find a geometric measurement for each weight, so the formula tells us where the point lies.

5.1  Area begins with a base and a perpendicular height

Write [ABC][ABC] for the ordinary, nonnegative area of triangle ABCABC. If a side has length bb and the perpendicular distance from the opposite vertex to its line is hh, then [ABC]=12bh.[ABC]=\frac12bh. The word perpendicular matters. A sloping side of the triangle is generally not its height. If the perpendicular foot falls outside the side segment, the same formula still uses the distance to the full line containing the base.

There are two immediate comparisons. Triangles with the same base have areas in the ratio of their perpendicular heights. Triangles whose bases lie on the same line and whose opposite vertices have the same perpendicular distance from that line have areas in the ratio of their base lengths.

Lemma 5.1 · The common-height comparison

In a nondegenerate triangle ABCABC, if DD lies strictly between BB and CC, then [ABD][ADC]=BDDC.\boxed{\frac{[ABD]}{[ADC]}=\frac{BD}{DC}.}

Proof

Use BDBD and DCDC as bases. Both triangles have the same altitude hh from AA to line BCBC. Therefore [ABD][ADC]=12BD⋅h12DC⋅h=BDDC.\frac{[ABD]}{[ADC]}= \frac{\tfrac12 BD\cdot h}{\tfrac12 DC\cdot h}=\frac{BD}{DC}. The common factor is not just the side ADAD: it is the height to the line containing both chosen bases.

5.2  The common-side comparison

Let B≠CB\ne C. Suppose A,P,DA,P,D lie on a line that meets line BCBC at DD, with neither AA nor PP on line BCBC. Then [ABC][PBC]=ADPD.\boxed{\frac{[ABC]}{[PBC]}=\frac{AD}{PD}.} Both triangles use the same base BCBC. If line ADAD is perpendicular to BCBC, the two heights are exactly ADAD and PDPD. Otherwise, dropping perpendiculars from AA and PP to BCBC gives similar right triangles: they have the same acute angle between the lines ADAD and BCBC, and their hypotenuses are ADAD and PDPD. Thus the heights are in the ratio AD:PDAD:PD. This remains true whether A,PA,P are on the same side or on opposite sides of BCBC.

There is a second proof that makes the cancellations explicit. When D≠CD\ne C, insert the two intermediate triangles ADCADC and DCPDCP: [ABC][BCP]=[ABC][ADC]⋅[ADC][DCP]⋅[DCP][BCP]=BCDC⋅ADDP⋅DCBC=ADDP.\begin{aligned} \frac{[ABC]}{[BCP]} &=\frac{[ABC]}{[ADC]}\cdot\frac{[ADC]}{[DCP]}\cdot\frac{[DCP]}{[BCP]}\\ &=\frac{BC}{DC}\cdot\frac{AD}{DP}\cdot\frac{DC}{BC}\\ &=\frac{AD}{DP}. \end{aligned} The first and third ratios compare bases on line BCBC. In the middle ratio, use the bases AD,DPAD,DP on their common line and the common height from CC. The factors BCBC and DCDC cancel. If D=CD=C, use BB as the auxiliary vertex instead, or use the height proof directly.

Example 5.1 · Read a ratio in both directions

If PP lies between AA and DD and AP:PD=3:2AP:PD=3:2, then AD:PD=5:2AD:PD=5:2, so [ABC]:[PBC]=5:2[ABC]:[PBC]=5:2. Conversely, if [PBC]=25[ABC][PBC]=\tfrac25[ABC] and PP is between AA and DD, then PD/AD=2/5PD/AD=2/5 and AP/PD=3/2AP/PD=3/2. The small triangle's area measures the perpendicular distance of PP from the base line, not its distance from the vertex AA.

5.3  Ceva's theorem from areas

Definition 5.2 · Cevians and concurrence

A cevian of a triangle is a segment from a vertex to a point on the opposite side. Three lines are concurrent when they pass through one common point.

Theorem 5.3 · Ceva's theorem, interior form

In a nondegenerate triangle ABCABC, let D,E,FD,E,F lie in the interiors of BC,CA,ABBC,CA,AB, respectively. The cevians AD,BE,CFAD,BE,CF are concurrent exactly when BDDC⋅CEEA⋅AFFB=1.\boxed{\frac{BD}{DC}\cdot\frac{CE}{EA}\cdot\frac{AF}{FB}=1.}

Proof

First suppose the cevians meet at GG inside the triangle. The triangles ABGABG and ACGACG have common base AGAG. Their heights are the distances from B,CB,C to line AGAG, which line BCBC meets at DD. The common-side comparison therefore gives BDDC=[ABG][ACG].\frac{BD}{DC}=\frac{[ABG]}{[ACG]}. Similarly, CEEA=[BCG][BAG],AFFB=[CAG][CBG].\frac{CE}{EA}=\frac{[BCG]}{[BAG]},\qquad \frac{AF}{FB}=\frac{[CAG]}{[CBG]}. The names ABGABG and BAGBAG denote the same ordinary triangle area, and likewise for the other reversed orders. Multiplying gives [ABG][ACG][BCG][ABG][ACG][BCG]=1.\frac{[ABG]}{[ACG]} \frac{[BCG]}{[ABG]} \frac{[ACG]}{[BCG]}=1. Every area appears once in a numerator and once in a denominator.

Conversely, suppose the product of the three side ratios is 11. The vertices BB and CC lie on opposite sides of line ADAD, and EE, strictly between AA and CC, is on CC's side of that line. Thus the segment BEBE crosses ADAD at a point GG inside the triangle. Extend the ray from CC through GG to meet ABAB at F′F'. Applying the direction already proved to AD,BE,CF′AD,BE,CF' gives BDDCCEEAAF′F′B=1.\frac{BD}{DC}\frac{CE}{EA}\frac{AF'}{F'B}=1. Comparing with the assumed equation gives AF′/F′B=AF/FBAF'/F'B=AF/FB. There is only one point on ABAB with a given positive division ratio: if AX/XB=tAX/XB=t, then AX=tAB/(1+t)AX=t\,AB/(1+t). Therefore F′=FF'=F, so the original three cevians are concurrent.

For example, the three medians have ratios 1,1,11,1,1, so Ceva's theorem proves that they concur. The coordinate calculation in Lecture 3 also found the exact position of their common point.

5.4  Unequal division of a segment

Suppose PP lies between A,BA,B and AP:PB=m:nAP:PB=m:n, with m,n>0m,n>0. Since AB=AP+PBAB=AP+PB, APAB=mm+n.\frac{AP}{AB}=\frac{m}{m+n}. The arrow from AA to PP is this fraction of the arrow from AA to BB. Therefore p=a+mm+n(b−a)=(m+n)a+mb−mam+n=na+mbm+n.\begin{aligned} p&=a+\frac{m}{m+n}(b-a)\\ &=\frac{(m+n)a+mb-ma}{m+n}\\ &=\boxed{\frac{na+mb}{m+n}.} \end{aligned} The coefficient of aa is the length-ratio part next to BB, and the coefficient of bb is the part next to AA. This apparent reversal is easy to check: if PP is very close to AA, then mm is small and the coefficient of aa is large.

Example 5.2 · A section formula in Cartesian and complex form

Let a=1+2ia=1+2i, b=7+5ib=7+5i, and let AP:PB=1:2AP:PB=1:2. Then p=2a+b3=2+4i+7+5i3=3+3i.p=\frac{2a+b}{3} =\frac{2+4i+7+5i}{3}=3+3i. The arrow p−a=2+ip-a=2+i is one third of b−a=6+3ib-a=6+3i, so the point is indeed one third of the way from AA to BB.

5.5  Mass points: a geometric interpretation of weights

Definition 5.4 · Balance point of two masses

Place positive masses α\alpha at AA and β\beta at BB. The balance point PP of the segment ABAB is the point between its endpoints satisfying the lever relation αAP=βPB.\alpha\,AP=\beta\,PB.

Thus AP:PB=β:αAP:PB=\beta:\alpha, and the section formula gives p=αa+βbα+β.\boxed{p=\frac{\alpha a+\beta b}{\alpha+\beta}.} The larger mass pulls the balance point closer to its endpoint. For mathematical use, no physical apparatus is needed: the formula is simply a statement about division ratios on a segment.

The combined mass at the balance point is α+β\alpha+\beta. If a third mass γ\gamma is placed at CC, combine the first two at PP and balance this combined mass with the third. The resulting point has coordinate g=(α+β)p+γcα+β+γ=(α+β)αa+βbα+β+γcα+β+γ=αa+βb+γcα+β+γ.\begin{aligned} g&=\frac{(\alpha+\beta)p+\gamma c}{\alpha+\beta+\gamma}\\ &=\frac{(\alpha+\beta)\dfrac{\alpha a+\beta b}{\alpha+\beta}+\gamma c} {\alpha+\beta+\gamma}\\ &=\boxed{\frac{\alpha a+\beta b+\gamma c}{\alpha+\beta+\gamma}.} \end{aligned} If we combine B,CB,C first instead, the same numerator and denominator appear. Thus the final balance point does not depend on the order of combining masses. This algebraic fact is why mass points can be used consistently across several cevians.

Example 5.3 · Find a third cevian by balancing masses

In triangle ABCABC, let DD lie on ABAB and EE on BCBC with AD=3,DB=5,BE=3,EC=8.AD=3,\qquad DB=5,\qquad BE=3,\qquad EC=8. Let G=AE∩CDG=AE\cap CD, and let BGBG meet ACAC at FF. Find AF:FCAF:FC and FG:GBFG:GB.

Step 1: balance at DD. To obtain AD:DB=3:5AD:DB=3:5, put mass 55 at AA and mass 33 at BB. Indeed, 5⋅3=3⋅55\cdot3=3\cdot5. The combined mass at DD is 5+3=85+3=8.

Step 2: balance at EE. Retain mass 33 at BB. If the mass at CC is mm, the balance relation is 3⋅BE=m⋅EC,3⋅3=8m,m=98.3\cdot BE=m\cdot EC,\qquad 3\cdot3=8m,\qquad m=\frac98. The mass at EE is 3+9/8=33/83+9/8=33/8.

Combining A,BA,B first places their balance point at DD, so the same total balance point lies on CDCD. It is therefore the intersection GG. This justifies using the same masses on a third cevian; it is not an extra assumption about the drawing.

Step 4: balance at FF. Combine the masses at A,CA,C. Their total is 5+98=498.5+\frac98=\frac{49}{8}. Their balance point is on ACAC, with AF:FC=98:5=9:40.AF:FC=\frac98:5=\boxed{9:40}. If fractions are inconvenient, multiply all masses by 88. The masses become 40,24,940,24,9 at A,B,CA,B,C, with 4949 at FF and total mass 7373. Neither balance points nor division ratios change. In complex coordinates, f=40a+9c49,g=40a+24b+9c73.f=\frac{40a+9c}{49},\qquad \boxed{g=\frac{40a+24b+9c}{73}.} Then g=24b+49f73,g=\frac{24b+49f}{73}, which is exactly the segment formula on BFBF. The geometry and the weighted algebra give the same calculation.

5.6  Barycentric coordinates belong to a triangle

Cartesian coordinates locate a point using two perpendicular axes. Barycentric coordinates locate it using three vertices of a fixed nondegenerate triangle.

Definition 5.5 · Barycentric coordinates

Let A,B,CA,B,C be noncollinear points with complex coordinates a,b,ca,b,c. The normalised barycentric coordinates of a point PP form a triple of real numbers (λ,μ,ν)(\lambda,\mu,\nu) satisfying p=λa+μb+νc,λ+μ+ν=1.\boxed{p=\lambda a+\mu b+\nu c,\qquad\lambda+\mu+\nu=1.} Proportional weights are written (α:β:γ)(\alpha:\beta:\gamma). If their sum is nonzero, divide each by that sum to obtain the normalised triple.

The numbers a,b,ca,b,c are complex coordinates of vertices. The numbers λ,μ,ν\lambda,\mu,\nu are real weights attached to those vertices. They have different jobs. Barycentric coordinates also depend on the reference triangle: the same point generally has a different triple for a different triangle.

For example, AA has coordinates (1,0,0)(1,0,0), BB has (0,1,0)(0,1,0), and CC has (0,0,1)(0,0,1). The midpoint of BCBC has (0,1/2,1/2)(0,1/2,1/2). The centroid has (1/3,1/3,1/3)(1/3,1/3,1/3), also written (1:1:1)(1:1:1).

5.6.1  Why the sum of the weights must be one

Translate all points by tt. The new vertex coordinates are a+t,b+t,c+ta+t,b+t,c+t, and the weighted expression becomes λ(a+t)+μ(b+t)+ν(c+t)=λa+μb+νc+(λ+μ+ν)t=p+t.\begin{aligned} \lambda(a+t)+\mu(b+t)+\nu(c+t) &=\lambda a+\mu b+\nu c+(\lambda+\mu+\nu)t\\ &=p+t. \end{aligned} Thus the represented point translates by the same displacement exactly because the weights sum to 11. The condition makes a weighted combination of positions independent of the chosen origin. If the sum were, say, 22, the expression would move by 2t2t instead.

5.7  Constructing the weights by two segment divisions

Let PP lie inside triangle ABCABC. Draw APAP to meet BCBC at DD. Write BD=m,DC=n,AP=d1,PD=d2.BD=m,\quad DC=n,\qquad AP=d_1,\quad PD=d_2. All four numbers are positive. First divide BCBC: d=nb+mcm+n.d=\frac{nb+mc}{m+n}. Next divide ADAD in the ratio AP:PD=d1:d2AP:PD=d_1:d_2: p=d2a+d1dd1+d2.p=\frac{d_2a+d_1d}{d_1+d_2}. Substitute the first expression into the second and distribute: p=d2ad1+d2+d1d1+d2(nb+mcm+n)=d2d1+d2a+d1n(d1+d2)(m+n)b+d1m(d1+d2)(m+n)c.\begin{aligned} p&=\frac{d_2a}{d_1+d_2} +\frac{d_1}{d_1+d_2}\left(\frac{nb+mc}{m+n}\right)\\ &=\frac{d_2}{d_1+d_2}a +\frac{d_1n}{(d_1+d_2)(m+n)}b +\frac{d_1m}{(d_1+d_2)(m+n)}c. \end{aligned} Thus the weights are λ=d2d1+d2,μ=d1n(d1+d2)(m+n),ν=d1m(d1+d2)(m+n).\boxed{\lambda=\frac{d_2}{d_1+d_2},\quad \mu=\frac{d_1n}{(d_1+d_2)(m+n)},\quad \nu=\frac{d_1m}{(d_1+d_2)(m+n)}.} They are positive, and their sum is λ+μ+ν=d2d1+d2+d1(n+m)(d1+d2)(m+n)=d2+d1d1+d2=1.\begin{aligned} \lambda+\mu+\nu &=\frac{d_2}{d_1+d_2} +\frac{d_1(n+m)}{(d_1+d_2)(m+n)}\\ &=\frac{d_2+d_1}{d_1+d_2}=1. \end{aligned} This proves existence for interior points by ordinary segment geometry, with no assumption about vector spaces.

5.7.1  Why the representation is unique

Suppose the same point had two normalised triples. Subtract the two expressions and use the fact that both sums of weights are 11: (μ−μ′)(b−a)+(ν−ν′)(c−a)=0.(\mu-\mu')(b-a)+(\nu-\nu')(c-a)=0. If ν−ν′≠0\nu-\nu'\ne0, then c−ab−a=−μ−μ′ν−ν′,\frac{c-a}{b-a}=-\frac{\mu-\mu'}{\nu-\nu'}, a real number. By the collinearity criterion in Section 4.3, A,B,CA,B,C would lie on a line, contrary to the choice of our reference triangle. Thus ν=ν′\nu=\nu'. The remaining equation gives μ=μ′\mu=\mu' because b−a≠0b-a\ne0. Finally λ=1−μ−ν=λ′\lambda=1-\mu-\nu=\lambda'. All three weights are unique.

5.8  The weights are opposite area fractions

For an interior point PP, the three triangles PBC,PCA,PABPBC,PCA,PAB partition ABCABC, so their areas sum to [ABC][ABC]. The normalised barycentric coordinates are λ=[PBC][ABC],μ=[PCA][ABC],ν=[PAB][ABC].\boxed{\lambda=\frac{[PBC]}{[ABC]},\qquad \mu=\frac{[PCA]}{[ABC]},\qquad \nu=\frac{[PAB]}{[ABC]}.} We now verify this geometric interpretation from the preceding construction.

Because A,P,DA,P,D are collinear, the common-side comparison gives [PBC][ABC]=PDAD=d2d1+d2=λ.\frac{[PBC]}{[ABC]}=\frac{PD}{AD}=\frac{d_2}{d_1+d_2}=\lambda. For the other two areas, use common base APAP and compare the distances of C,BC,B from its line. Their ratio is DC:DB=n:mDC:DB=n:m. Thus [PCA][PAB]=nm.\frac{[PCA]}{[PAB]}=\frac nm. Their sum is [ABC]−[PBC]=(1−λ)[ABC][ABC]-[PBC]=(1-\lambda)[ABC]. The only positive pair with ratio n:mn:m and this sum is [PCA][ABC]=(1−λ)nm+n=μ,[PAB][ABC]=(1−λ)mm+n=ν.\frac{[PCA]}{[ABC]}=(1-\lambda)\frac{n}{m+n}=\mu, \qquad \frac{[PAB]}{[ABC]}=(1-\lambda)\frac{m}{m+n}=\nu. Hence the area and section constructions give the same weights.

The unnormalised barycentric coordinates are therefore (α:β:γ)=([PBC]:[PCA]:[PAB]).(\alpha:\beta:\gamma)=([PBC]:[PCA]:[PAB]). Mass, area and weighted coordinates describe the same point. This is the geometric content behind the algebraic expression p=λa+μb+νcp=\lambda a+\mu b+\nu c.

Example 5.4 · Convert areas into an actual point

Let a=0a=0, b=6b=6 and c=4ic=4i. Suppose [PBC]:[PCA]:[PAB]=2:3:5[PBC]:[PCA]:[PAB]=2:3:5. The weights sum to 1010, so p=2a+3b+5c10=18+20i10=95+2i.p=\frac{2a+3b+5c}{10}=\frac{18+20i}{10}=\frac95+2i. Conversely, this point has precisely those area ratios, by uniqueness of the normalised weights.

5.9  Boundary points and points outside the triangle

The construction extends to the boundary. A point on BCBC has λ=0\lambda=0, with μ,ν≥0\mu,\nu\ge0 and sum 11. A vertex has two zero weights. More generally,

Theorem 5.6

A point is in the closed triangle ABCABC, including its sides and vertices, exactly when its normalised barycentric weights are all nonnegative. It is in the interior exactly when all three are positive.

Proof

The interior case and its positive weights were constructed above. On a side, the ordinary section formula gives nonnegative weights, with the opposite weight zero; the vertices were already listed.

Conversely, suppose λ,μ,ν≥0\lambda,\mu,\nu\ge0 and sum to 11. If μ+ν=0\mu+\nu=0, then λ=1\lambda=1 and the point is AA. Otherwise define d=μb+νcμ+ν.d=\frac{\mu b+\nu c}{\mu+\nu}. This point lies on BCBC, because it is a segment average with nonnegative weights. Since μ+ν=1−λ\mu+\nu=1-\lambda, p=λa+(1−λ)d,p=\lambda a+(1-\lambda)d, so PP lies on the segment ADAD, inside or on the boundary of the triangle. If all weights are positive, DD is inside BCBC and PP is inside ADAD, giving an interior point. A zero weight places the point on a side, so an interior point cannot have a zero weight.

5.9.1  Every point of the plane still has weights

For an arbitrary point, negative weights may be needed. Here is a direct existence calculation, using only two linear equations. Write p−a=x+iy,b−a=u+iv,c−a=s+it.p-a=x+iy,\quad b-a=u+iv,\quad c-a=s+it. We seek real μ,ν\mu,\nu satisfying μu+νs=x,μv+νt=y.\mu u+\nu s=x,\qquad\mu v+\nu t=y. Multiply the first equation by tt, the second by ss, and subtract to eliminate ν\nu: μ(ut−vs)=xt−ys.\mu(ut-vs)=xt-ys. Multiply the second by uu, the first by vv, and subtract to eliminate μ\mu: ν(ut−vs)=uy−vx.\nu(ut-vs)=uy-vx. The quantity ut−vsut-vs is nonzero. If it were zero, the quotient (c−a)/(b−a)(c-a)/(b-a) would have zero imaginary part and the reference vertices would be collinear. Therefore μ=xt−ysut−vs,ν=uy−vxut−vs,λ=1−μ−ν.\mu=\frac{xt-ys}{ut-vs},\qquad \nu=\frac{uy-vx}{ut-vs},\qquad\lambda=1-\mu-\nu. These give a representation for every point, and the earlier uniqueness proof still applies. An outside point must have at least one negative weight; otherwise the theorem would put it in the closed triangle.

Example 5.5 · A negative weight has a visible meaning

For a=0a=0, b=4b=4, c=3ic=3i, the point p=6p=6 has weights (−1/2,3/2,0)(-1/2,3/2,0), because 6=−12⋅0+32⋅4+0⋅3i,−12+32+0=1.6=-\frac12\cdot0+\frac32\cdot4+0\cdot3i,\qquad -\frac12+\frac32+0=1. It lies on the extension of ABAB beyond BB. The negative coefficient is no longer a positive physical mass; it is a signed algebraic weight.

5.10  Area expressed by complex multiplication

We can now connect the area comparisons to an explicit complex formula. Let u=b−a=x+iyu=b-a=x+iy and v=c−a=s+itv=c-a=s+it. Then u‾v=(x−iy)(s+it)=xs+yt+i(xt−ys).\bar u v=(x-iy)(s+it)=xs+yt+i(xt-ys). The imaginary part is xt−ysxt-ys. For nonzero u,vu,v, let θ=Arg(v/u)\theta=\operatorname{Arg}(v/u) be the principal turn from uu to vv. Then u‾v=|u||v|(cosθ+isinθ),Im(u‾v)=|u||v|sinθ.\bar u v=\left|u\right|\left|v\right|(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta),\qquad \operatorname{Im}(\bar u v)=\left|u\right|\left|v\right|\sin\theta. The perpendicular height above base ABAB is |v||sinθ|\left|v\right|\left|\sin\theta\right|, so the magnitude of the imaginary part is base times height. Its sign is positive for 0<θ<π0<\theta<\pi and negative for −π<θ<0-\pi<\theta<0. For collinear arrows it is zero.

Definition 5.7 · Signed triangle area

The signed area of the ordered triple (A,B,C)(A,B,C) is Δ(a,b,c)=12Im(b−a¯(c−a)).\Delta(a,b,c)=\frac12\operatorname{Im}\bigl(\overline{b-a}(c-a)\bigr).

Thus [ABC]=|Δ(a,b,c)|.\boxed{[ABC]=\left|\Delta(a,b,c)\right|.} The formula gives zero for a degenerate triangle as well, including when one arrow is zero. For a nondegenerate triangle, anticlockwise vertex order gives positive signed area; reversing the order changes its sign.

5.10.1  A polygon formula

Expand the signed area of one triangle and use Im(w‾)=−Im(w)\operatorname{Im}(\bar w)=-\operatorname{Im}(w): 2Δ(a,b,c)=Im(b‾c−b‾a−a‾c)=Im(a‾b+b‾c+c‾a).2\Delta(a,b,c) =\operatorname{Im}(\bar b c-\bar b a-\bar a c) =\operatorname{Im}(\bar a b+\bar b c+\bar c a). The three terms now follow the three oriented edges. For a convex anticlockwise polygon z1,…,znz_1,\ldots,z_n, draw the diagonals from z1z_1 to split it into triangles. Each internal diagonal is traversed in opposite directions in the two triangles that share it; the imaginary parts of z‾jzk\bar z_jz_k and z‾kzj\bar z_kz_j cancel. Only the boundary edges remain, giving Area=12Im(z‾1z2+z‾2z3+⋯+z‾nz1).\boxed{\text{Area}=\frac12\operatorname{Im}\left(\bar z_1z_2+\bar z_2z_3+\cdots+\bar z_nz_1\right).} In Cartesian coordinates zj=xj+iyjz_j=x_j+iy_j, this becomes Area=12∑j=1n(xjyj+1−yjxj+1),(xn+1,yn+1)=(x1,y1).\text{Area}=\frac12\sum_{j=1}^{n}(x_jy_{j+1}-y_jx_{j+1}), \qquad (x_{n+1},y_{n+1})=(x_1,y_1). For clockwise order take the absolute value. The formula is the accumulated algebra of oriented triangle areas.

5.11  Ceva's ratios in barycentric language

Let PP have positive weights (λ:μ:ν)(\lambda:\mu:\nu). The line from AA through PP meets BCBC at a point DD with weights (0:μ:ν)(0:\mu:\nu). The section formula gives BDDC=νμ.\frac{BD}{DC}=\frac{\nu}{\mu}. Cyclically, CEEA=λν,AFFB=μλ.\frac{CE}{EA}=\frac{\lambda}{\nu},\qquad \frac{AF}{FB}=\frac{\mu}{\lambda}. Their product is 11. This is the same cancellation that appeared in the area proof, now written as cancellation of weights.

In Example 5.3, the total point has weights (40:24:9)(40:24:9). The cevian from BB meets ACAC at (40:0:9)(40:0:9), so AF:FC=9:40AF:FC=9:40. Its combined weight is 4949, and balancing it against 2424 at BB gives FG:GB=24:49FG:GB=24:49. The entire calculation is contained in the three weights once their geometric meaning has been established.

5.12  An affine function takes its extreme values at vertices

Definition 5.8 · Affine function

A real function of the form f(x,y)=ux+vy+w,f(x,y)=ux+vy+w, where u,v,wu,v,w are fixed real numbers, is called affine.

Unlike a homogeneous linear expression, an affine function is allowed a nonzero constant term. We will prove the following statement without calculus.

Theorem 5.9 · Vertex extrema of an affine function

If ff is affine, then for every point PP in the closed triangle ABCABC, min{f(A),f(B),f(C)}≤f(P)≤max{f(A),f(B),f(C)}.\min\{f(A),f(B),f(C)\}\le f(P)\le\max\{f(A),f(B),f(C)\}. In particular, a maximum and a minimum over the triangle are attained at vertices.

Proof

Write the normalised barycentric coordinates of PP as λ,μ,ν\lambda,\mu,\nu. They are nonnegative and sum to 11. If the vertex coordinates are (xA,yA)(x_A,y_A), (xB,yB)(x_B,y_B) and (xC,yC)(x_C,y_C), the complex weighted expression gives the two real equations xP=λxA+μxB+νxC,yP=λyA+μyB+νyC.x_P=\lambda x_A+\mu x_B+\nu x_C, \qquad y_P=\lambda y_A+\mu y_B+\nu y_C. Substitute them into the function: f(P)=u(λxA+μxB+νxC)+v(λyA+μyB+νyC)+w=λ(uxA+vyA)+μ(uxB+vyB)+ν(uxC+vyC)+(λ+μ+ν)w=λ(uxA+vyA+w)+μ(uxB+vyB+w)+ν(uxC+vyC+w)=λf(A)+μf(B)+νf(C).\begin{aligned} f(P)&=u(\lambda x_A+\mu x_B+\nu x_C) +v(\lambda y_A+\mu y_B+\nu y_C)+w\\ &=\lambda(ux_A+vy_A)+\mu(ux_B+vy_B) +\nu(ux_C+vy_C)+(\lambda+\mu+\nu)w\\ &=\lambda(ux_A+vy_A+w)+\mu(ux_B+vy_B+w) +\nu(ux_C+vy_C+w)\\ &=\boxed{\lambda f(A)+\mu f(B)+\nu f(C).} \end{aligned} The replacement of ww by (λ+μ+ν)w(\lambda+\mu+\nu)w is the step that uses normalisation.

Let M=max{f(A),f(B),f(C)}M=\max\{f(A),f(B),f(C)\}. Since the weights are nonnegative, multiplying the three inequalities f(A)≤Mf(A)\le M, f(B)≤Mf(B)\le M, f(C)≤Mf(C)\le M by the corresponding weights preserves their direction. Add them: f(P)≤λM+μM+νM=(λ+μ+ν)M=M.f(P)\le\lambda M+\mu M+\nu M=(\lambda+\mu+\nu)M=M. The same argument with the smallest vertex value proves the lower bound. At the vertex having value MM, equality is attained.

5.12.1  Where can equality occur?

The exact difference is M−f(P)=λ(M−f(A))+μ(M−f(B))+ν(M−f(C)).M-f(P)=\lambda(M-f(A))+\mu(M-f(B))+\nu(M-f(C)). Every term is nonnegative. The sum is zero precisely when each term with a positive weight is zero. Thus, if only one vertex has the maximum value, that vertex is the only maximiser. If exactly two vertices have that value, every point of their joining side is a maximiser and no other point is. If all three values agree, the function is constant throughout the triangle.

Example 5.6 · A complete maximum calculation

On the triangle with vertices A=(0,0)A=(0,0), B=(4,0)B=(4,0) and C=(0,3)C=(0,3), find the greatest and least values of f(x,y)=2x−3y+4f(x,y)=2x-3y+4.

Solution

Evaluate only at the vertices: f(A)=4,f(B)=12,f(C)=−5.f(A)=4,\qquad f(B)=12,\qquad f(C)=-5. The theorem gives −5≤f(P)≤12-5\le f(P)\le12 for every point of the triangle. The maximum 1212 occurs only at BB, and the minimum −5-5 only at CC, because both extreme vertex values are unique.

For a direct check, the barycentric weights are μ=x/4\mu=x/4, ν=y/3\nu=y/3, λ=1−x/4−y/3\lambda=1-x/4-y/3. Inside the triangle they are nonnegative, and f(P)=4λ+12μ−5ν.f(P)=4\lambda+12\mu-5\nu. This weighted average cannot be larger than 1212 or smaller than −5-5.

💡 The geometric idea to retain

Area ratios are weights. Positive weights locate points inside a triangle, and the sum-to-one condition lets affine expressions pass through a weighted average. A geometric description of position has produced an algebraic theorem about maximum and minimum values.
