💡 Study note · How to Use the Homework

First identify the structure of the expression. Do not apply a named inequality merely because it is familiar. Check the hypotheses, choose the direction of the estimate, and write the equality case.

Part A: Exchange Arguments and Rearrangement

☠ Problem 1

The numbers −3,1,4,8-3,\ 1,\ 4,\ 8 are to be paired with −2,0,5,7.-2,\ 0,\ 5,\ 7. Find the largest and the smallest possible values of the sum of the four products. Justify why no other pairing can do better.

Show solutionHide solution

Write both lists in increasing order: −3<1<4<8,−2<0<5<7.-3<1<4<8, \qquad -2<0<5<7. By rearrangement, the same-order pairing gives the maximum: (−3)(−2)+(1)(0)+(4)(5)+(8)(7)=82.(-3)(-2)+(1)(0)+(4)(5)+(8)(7)=\boxed{82}. The opposite-order pairing gives the minimum: (−3)(7)+(1)(5)+(4)(0)+(8)(−2)=−32.(-3)(7)+(1)(5)+(4)(0)+(8)(-2)=\boxed{-32}.

Let us also justify why checking these two pairings is enough. If xi<xjx_i<x_j and yr<ysy_r<y_s, then replacing the crossed contribution xiys+xjyrx_i y_s+x_j y_r by the uncrossed contribution xiyr+xjysx_i y_r+x_j y_s changes the sum by (xj−xi)(ys−yr)>0.(x_j-x_i)(y_s-y_r)>0. Thus repeated uncrossing produces the maximum, and reversing the exchanges produces the minimum. No other pairing can lie outside these two values.

☠ Problem 2

Let x1≤x2≤⋯≤xn,y1≤y2≤⋯≤yn.x_1\le x_2\le\cdots\le x_n, \qquad y_1\le y_2\le\cdots\le y_n. Prove directly by exchanges that, for every permutation σ\sigma, ∑i=1nxiyn+1−i≤∑i=1nxiyσ(i)≤∑i=1nxiyi.\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_i y_{n+1-i} \le \sum_{i=1}^{n}x_i y_{\sigma(i)} \le \sum_{i=1}^{n}x_i y_i. Discuss carefully what equality means when some entries are repeated.

Show solutionHide solution

For an arbitrary permutation, write zi=yσ(i)z_i=y_{\sigma(i)}. Suppose that i<ji<j, but zi>zjz_i>z_j. Swapping only these two assigned values changes the sum by (xizj+xjzi)−(xizi+xjzj)=(xj−xi)(zi−zj)≥0.\begin{aligned} &(x_i z_j+x_j z_i)-(x_i z_i+x_j z_j)\\ &=(x_j-x_i)(z_i-z_j)\ge0. \end{aligned} Hence removing an inversion never decreases the product sum. After finitely many exchanges, the assigned list is increasing, and therefore ∑i=1nxiyσ(i)≤∑i=1nxiyi.\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_i y_{\sigma(i)} \le \sum_{i=1}^{n}x_i y_i.

For the minimum, whenever i<ji<j and zi<zjz_i<z_j, the same calculation shows that swapping them does not increase the sum. Repeated exchanges make the assigned list decreasing, so ∑i=1nxiyn+1−i≤∑i=1nxiyσ(i).\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_i y_{n+1-i} \le \sum_{i=1}^{n}x_i y_{\sigma(i)}.

An exchange preserves the sum exactly when xi=xjx_i=x_j or the two exchanged yy-values are equal. Therefore equal entries may be permuted within their blocks. If both lists are strictly increasing, the two extremal pairings are unique.

☠ Problem 3

Under the hypotheses of the preceding problem, prove that ∑i=1n(xi−yi)2≤∑i=1n(xi−yσ(i))2\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i-y_i)^2 \le \sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i-y_{\sigma(i)})^2 for every permutation σ\sigma. Interpret the result as a statement about matching points on the real line.

Show solutionHide solution

Expand the expression: ∑i=1n(xi−yσ(i))2=∑i=1nxi2+∑i=1nyi2−2∑i=1nxiyσ(i).\begin{aligned} \sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i-y_{\sigma(i)})^2 &=\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_i^2+\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_i^2 -2\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_i y_{\sigma(i)}. \end{aligned} The first two sums are independent of σ\sigma. Thus minimising the total squared distance is the same as maximising the product sum. Rearrangement gives ∑i=1n(xi−yi)2≤∑i=1n(xi−yσ(i))2.\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i-y_i)^2 \le \sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i-y_{\sigma(i)})^2.

In geometric language, list each set of points on the real line from left to right. Matching the first point to the first, the second to the second, and so on, minimises the total squared matching cost. Crossed matches can be uncrossed without increasing the cost. Equality has the same block-permutation description as in Problem 2.

☠ Problem 4

Prove the identity n∑i=1naibi−(∑i=1nai)(∑i=1nbi)=∑1≤i<j≤n(aj−ai)(bj−bi).n\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_i b_i -\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_i\right) \left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}b_i\right) = \sum_{1\le i<j\le n}(a_j-a_i)(b_j-b_i). Hence obtain Chebyshev's sum inequality without using cyclic shifts.

Show solutionHide solution

Expand the pairwise-difference sum: ∑1≤i<j≤n(aj−ai)(bj−bi)=∑i<j(ajbj+aibi−ajbi−aibj).\begin{aligned} &\sum_{1\le i<j\le n}(a_j-a_i)(b_j-b_i)\\ &=\sum_{i<j}\bigl(a_jb_j+a_ib_i-a_jb_i-a_ib_j\bigr). \end{aligned} Every diagonal term akbka_kb_k appears n−1n-1 times, whereas the negative terms run through all aibja_i b_j with i≠ji\ne j. Hence this equals (n−1)∑iaibi−∑i≠jaibj=n∑iaibi−(∑iai)(∑ibi).(n-1)\sum_i a_i b_i-\sum_{i\ne j}a_i b_j =n\sum_i a_i b_i-\left(\sum_i a_i\right)\left(\sum_i b_i\right). This proves the identity.

If (ai)(a_i) and (bi)(b_i) are similarly ordered, then every product (aj−ai)(bj−bi)(a_j-a_i)(b_j-b_i) is non-negative. Therefore 1n∑i=1naibi≥(1n∑i=1nai)(1n∑i=1nbi),\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_i b_i \ge \left(\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_i\right) \left(\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^{n}b_i\right), which is Chebyshev's inequality. For n>1n>1, equality holds precisely when at least one of the two sequences is constant.

Part B: Applications of Ordering

☠ Problem 5

For positive real numbers a,b,ca,b,c, prove both inequalities a3+b3+c3≥a2b+b2c+c2a,a^3+b^3+c^3\ge a^2b+b^2c+c^2a, a3+b3+c3≥a2c+b2a+c2b.a^3+b^3+c^3\ge a^2c+b^2a+c^2b.

Show solutionHide solution

The two lists a2,b2,c2anda,b,ca^2,b^2,c^2 \qquad\text{and}\qquad a,b,c have the same order because a,b,c>0a,b,c>0. Their same-order product sum is a3+b3+c3a^3+b^3+c^3, and rearrangement says that it is at least every other pairing. Applying the two cyclic pairings gives a3+b3+c3≥a2b+b2c+c2aa^3+b^3+c^3\ge a^2b+b^2c+c^2a and a3+b3+c3≥a2c+b2a+c2b.a^3+b^3+c^3\ge a^2c+b^2a+c^2b. Equality in either cyclic comparison forces a=b=ca=b=c.

☠ Problem 6

Let a1,a2,…,an>0a_1,a_2,\ldots,a_n>0, and let σ\sigma be any permutation of 1,2,…,n1,2,\ldots,n. Prove that ∑i=1nai4≥∑i=1nai3aσ(i).\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_i^4 \ge \sum_{i=1}^{n}a_i^3a_{\sigma(i)}.

Show solutionHide solution

Relabel the indices so that a1≤a2≤⋯≤ana_1\le a_2\le\cdots\le a_n. This only conjugates the permutation σ\sigma, so it does not change the assertion. Since t↦t3t\mapsto t^3 is increasing for t>0t>0, the lists a13,a23,…,an3anda1,a2,…,ana_1^3,a_2^3,\ldots,a_n^3 \quad\text{and}\quad a_1,a_2,\ldots,a_n are similarly ordered. Rearrangement gives ∑i=1nai3aσ(i)≤∑i=1nai3ai=∑i=1nai4.\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_i^3a_{\sigma(i)} \le \sum_{i=1}^{n}a_i^3a_i =\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_i^4. Equality occurs when σ\sigma moves indices only among equal aia_i's. If all aia_i's are distinct, this means that σ\sigma is the identity.

☠ Problem 7

If a1≤a2≤⋯≤an,a_1\le a_2\le\cdots\le a_n, prove that a1+2a2+⋯+nana1+a2+⋯+an≥n+12,\frac{a_1+2a_2+\cdots+na_n}{a_1+a_2+\cdots+a_n} \ge\frac{n+1}{2}, provided the denominator is positive. What is the reverse statement for a decreasing sequence?

Show solutionHide solution

The sequences 1,2,…,n1,2,\ldots,n and a1,a2,…,ana_1,a_2,\ldots,a_n are similarly ordered. Chebyshev gives n∑i=1niai≥(∑i=1ni)(∑i=1nai)=n(n+1)2∑i=1nai.n\sum_{i=1}^{n}i a_i \ge \left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}i\right) \left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_i\right) =\frac{n(n+1)}2\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_i. Because ∑ai>0\sum a_i>0, we may divide to obtain a1+2a2+⋯+nana1+a2+⋯+an≥n+12.\frac{a_1+2a_2+\cdots+na_n}{a_1+a_2+\cdots+a_n} \ge\frac{n+1}{2}. For a decreasing sequence, the two lists are oppositely ordered and the inequality reverses. For n>1n>1, equality occurs when all aia_i's are equal.

☠ Problem 8

Let x1,x2,…,xn>0x_1,x_2,\ldots,x_n>0, and let r,s>0r,s>0. Prove that n∑i=1nxir+s≥(∑i=1nxir)(∑i=1nxis).n\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_i^{r+s} \ge \left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_i^r\right) \left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_i^s\right).

Show solutionHide solution

After sorting the xix_i's, the lists (xir)(x_i^r) and (xis)(x_i^s) are both increasing because r,s>0r,s>0. Chebyshev gives n∑i=1nxirxis≥(∑i=1nxir)(∑i=1nxis).n\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_i^r x_i^s \ge \left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_i^r\right) \left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_i^s\right). Since xirxis=xir+sx_i^r x_i^s=x_i^{r+s}, this is the required result. For n>1n>1, equality holds if and only if all the xix_i's are equal.

☠ Problem 9

If 0<a≤b≤c0<a\le b\le c, prove that ab+bc+ca≥ac+cb+ba≥3.\frac ab+\frac bc+\frac ca \ge \frac ac+\frac cb+\frac ba \ge3.

Show solutionHide solution

Set x=ba≥1,y=cb≥1.x=\frac ba\ge1, \qquad y=\frac cb\ge1. Then the difference between the first two expressions is 1x+1y+xy−(1xy+y+x)=(x−1)(y−1)(xy−1)xy≥0.\begin{aligned} &\frac1x+\frac1y+xy-\left(\frac1{xy}+y+x\right)\\ &=\frac{(x-1)(y-1)(xy-1)}{xy}\ge0. \end{aligned} Thus ab+bc+ca≥ac+cb+ba.\frac ab+\frac bc+\frac ca \ge \frac ac+\frac cb+\frac ba. Equality here occurs when a=ba=b or b=cb=c.

Also, ac+cb+ba≥3ac⋅cb⋅ba3=3\frac ac+\frac cb+\frac ba \ge3\sqrt[3]{\frac ac\cdot\frac cb\cdot\frac ba}=3 by AM–GM. Equality in this second inequality requires a=b=ca=b=c. Therefore equality throughout the chain occurs exactly at a=b=ca=b=c.

☠ Problem 10

For positive real numbers a1,a2,…,ana_1,a_2,\ldots,a_n, prove that ∏i=1naiai≥(∏i=1nai)(a1+a2+⋯+an)/n.\prod_{i=1}^{n}a_i^{a_i} \ge \left(\prod_{i=1}^{n}a_i\right)^{ (a_1+a_2+\cdots+a_n)/n}.

Show solutionHide solution

Taking logarithms, the desired inequality becomes ∑i=1nailogai≥1n(∑i=1nai)(∑i=1nlogai).\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_i\log a_i \ge \frac1n\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_i\right) \left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log a_i\right). The two lists (ai)(a_i) and (logai)(\log a_i) are similarly ordered because logarithm is increasing. The displayed estimate is therefore Chebyshev's inequality. Exponentiating gives ∏i=1naiai≥(∏i=1nai)(a1+⋯+an)/n.\prod_{i=1}^{n}a_i^{a_i} \ge \left(\prod_{i=1}^{n}a_i\right)^{(a_1+\cdots+a_n)/n}. For n>1n>1, equality occurs precisely when all aia_i's are equal.

Part C: Synthesis and Challenge Problems

☠ Problem 11

Let p>1p>1, and let a1,a2,…,ana_1,a_2,\ldots,a_n be distinct positive integers. Prove that ∑k=1nakkp≥∑k=1n1kp−1.\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{a_k}{k^p} \ge \sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac1{k^{p-1}}.

Show solutionHide solution

Let b1<b2<⋯<bnb_1<b_2<\cdots<b_n be the increasing rearrangement of the distinct positive integers a1,…,ana_1,\ldots,a_n. Necessarily bk≥kb_k\ge k.

The weights 1/kp1/k^p form a strictly decreasing sequence. Rearrangement says that their smallest pairing with the increasing list (bk)(b_k) is bk/kpb_k/k^p. The original order of the aka_k's is some other pairing, so ∑k=1nakkp≥∑k=1nbkkp≥∑k=1nkkp=∑k=1n1kp−1.\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{a_k}{k^p} \ge \sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{b_k}{k^p} \ge \sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{k}{k^p} =\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac1{k^{p-1}}. Equality holds exactly when ak=ka_k=k for every kk.

☠ Problem 12

For positive real numbers a1,a2,…,ana_1,a_2,\ldots,a_n, prove the chain n∑i=1nai4≥(∑i=1nai)(∑i=1nai3)≥(∑i=1nai2)2.n\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_i^4 \ge \left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_i\right) \left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_i^3\right) \ge \left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_i^2\right)^2.

Show solutionHide solution

The lists (ai)(a_i) and (ai3)(a_i^3) are similarly ordered. Chebyshev gives n∑i=1nai4≥(∑i=1nai)(∑i=1nai3).n\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_i^4 \ge \left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_i\right) \left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_i^3\right). Next apply Cauchy–Schwarz to ai\sqrt{a_i} and ai3/2a_i^{3/2}: (∑i=1nai)(∑i=1nai3)≥(∑i=1naiai3/2)2=(∑i=1nai2)2.\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_i\right) \left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_i^3\right) \ge \left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sqrt{a_i}\,a_i^{3/2}\right)^2 =\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_i^2\right)^2. Both equalities hold precisely when a1=a2=⋯=ana_1=a_2=\cdots=a_n (apart from the trivial case n=1n=1).

☠ Problem 13

Let x1,x2,…,xn>0x_1,x_2,\ldots,x_n>0, where xn+1=x1x_{n+1}=x_1. Prove that x1x2+x2x3+⋯+xnx1≥n.\frac{x_1}{x_2}+\frac{x_2}{x_3}+\cdots+ \frac{x_n}{x_1}\ge n. Determine the equality case, and explain why this problem is naturally connected with the rearrangement proof of AM–GM in this lecture.

Show solutionHide solution

The quickest proof is AM–GM. Since the product telescopes, ∏i=1nxixi+1=1,xn+1=x1.\prod_{i=1}^{n}\frac{x_i}{x_{i+1}}=1, \qquad x_{n+1}=x_1. Therefore x1x2+x2x3+⋯+xnx1≥n1n=n.\frac{x_1}{x_2}+\frac{x_2}{x_3}+\cdots+\frac{x_n}{x_1} \ge n\sqrt[n]{1}=n. Equality requires x1x2=x2x3=⋯=xnx1=1,\frac{x_1}{x_2}=\frac{x_2}{x_3}=\cdots=\frac{x_n}{x_1}=1, so x1=x2=⋯=xnx_1=x_2=\cdots=x_n.

There is also a proof that belongs exactly to this lecture. Pair the list (xi)(x_i) with its reciprocal list (1/xi)(1/x_i). These lists are oppositely ordered, so the unshifted pairing has the minimum possible sum: ∑i=1nxi1xi=n.\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_i\frac1{x_i}=n. The cyclic pairing pairs xix_i with 1/xi+11/x_{i+1}. Rearrangement therefore gives n≤∑i=1nxixi+1.n\le\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{x_i}{x_{i+1}}. This is the same mechanism used in our rearrangement proof of AM–GM: a reciprocal pairing equal to nn is compared with a carefully chosen cyclic pairing.

💡 Summary · What Homework 2 Was Really Testing

These problems all ask us to see order before calculation:

  • use a two-pair exchange to justify an extremal pairing;

  • turn squared-distance minimisation into product-sum maximisation;

  • recognise Chebyshev when two expressions are monotone in the same variable;

  • take logarithms when the product exponents themselves form an ordered list;

  • trace equality back to the exchange step, not merely to the final displayed inequality.

As I have told you before, the name of the inequality is not the starting point. The starting point is always the structure of the two lists.
