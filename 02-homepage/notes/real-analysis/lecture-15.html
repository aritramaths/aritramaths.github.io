Discontinuities and Continuous Function Theorems

Read this lecture online, then continue to the next chapter. Figures and exercises are being adapted separately for the web edition.

What Can Go Wrong at a Point?

Motivation 15.1

A limit describes the values near a point. Continuity asks whether those nearby values agree with the value at the point. Sometimes one badly chosen value is the whole problem: changing that one value repairs the function. Sometimes the two sides are already heading to different answers. Then changing the value at the point cannot possibly make both sides agree.

We will classify the behaviour at an interior point \(c\), where inputs from both sides are available. At an endpoint of a domain we use the available one-sided limit instead; there is no requirement involving inputs outside the domain.

Definition 15.1

Removable and Jump Discontinuities

Suppose \(f\) is defined at \(c\) and near \(c\) on both sides.

  1. A discontinuity at \(c\) is removable if the finite limit \(L=\lim_{x\to c}f(x)\) exists but \(f(c)\ne L\). Replacing \(f(c)\) by \(L\) makes the function continuous there.

  2. A jump discontinuity occurs if both finite limits \(L_-=\lim_{x\to c^-}f(x)\) and \(L_+=\lim_{x\to c^+}f(x)\) exist but \(L_-\ne L_+\). Its signed jump is \(L_+-L_-\).

The removable and jump cases are commonly called discontinuities of the first kind. A jump is non-removable.

The adjective signed matters: a downward jump has negative sign. The geometric distance between the two limiting heights is \(|L_+-L_-|\). Some books call this nonnegative distance the jump size.

Definition 15.2

Discontinuities of the Second Kind

A discontinuity at an interior point is of the second kind if at least one of the one-sided limits fails to exist as a finite number. An infinite one-sided limit and a persistent oscillation are two different ways this can happen. Such a discontinuity is non-removable.

Warning 15.1

Continuity at \(c\) is a statement about a function whose domain contains \(c\). If a formula is undefined at \(c\), then \(c\) lies outside its original domain. We may ask whether it has a continuous extension to \(c\). A finite two-sided limit permits exactly one such extension; this is often described as a removable hole or removable singularity. A jump, an infinite limit, or an oscillation cannot be repaired by assigning one value. We will state explicitly when the point is missing.

Classifying Concrete Examples

Example 15.1

Two Joining Points, Two Different Outcomes

Determine the continuity and the signed jumps of \[f(x)= \begin{cases} (2x^2+3)/5,&x\le1,\\ 6-5x,&1<x<3,\\ x-3,&x\ge3. \end{cases}\]

Explanation 15.1

Away from \(1\) and \(3\), only one polynomial formula is used in a sufficiently small neighbourhood. The polynomial limit laws give its limit by substitution, which equals its value there.

At \(1\), use the formula appropriate to each side: \[\lim_{x\to1^-}f(x)=\frac{2+3}{5}=1,\qquad \lim_{x\to1^+}f(x)=6-5=1.\] The first branch also gives \(f(1)=1\). The two one-sided limits and the actual value agree, so the function is continuous at \(1\).

At \(3\), the left formula gives \(6-5\cdot3=-9\); the right formula gives \(3-3=0\). Thus \[\lim_{x\to3^-}f(x)=-9,\qquad \lim_{x\to3^+}f(x)=0,\qquad f(3)=0.\] There is a jump discontinuity at \(3\), of signed jump \(0-(-9)=9\). Assigning a different value at \(3\) would leave both one-sided limits unchanged, so it cannot repair this jump.

Example 15.2

Calculate Right Minus Left

Find the signed jump at the indicated joining point: \[p(x)= \begin{cases}-2x^2,&x\le3,\\3x,&x>3,\end{cases} \qquad q(x)=\frac{|2x-3|}{2x-3}\quad(x\ne3/2).\]

Explanation 15.2

For \(p\), the left-hand limit at \(3\) is \(-18\), while the right-hand limit is \(9\). Hence its signed jump is \(9-(-18)=27\). The value \(p(3)=-18\) does not remove the disagreement.

For \(q\), the sign of \(2x-3\) determines the whole expression: \[q(x)= \begin{cases}-1,&x<3/2,\\1,&x>3/2.\end{cases}\] Its left-hand limit is \(-1\) and its right-hand limit is \(1\), so the signed jump is \(2\). The point \(3/2\) is absent from the domain of \(q\); \(q\) is continuous at every point of that domain. Nevertheless, no assignment at \(3/2\) produces a continuous extension, because the two sides approach different heights.

A Hole That Really Can Be Filled

Example 15.3

Cancellation Does Not Put a Missing Input Back

Discuss the function \[r(x)=\frac{x^3+1}{x+1}\qquad(x\ne-1).\] Can it be extended continuously to \(-1\)?

Explanation 15.3

Factor the numerator: \[x^3+1=(x+1)(x^2-x+1).\] Thus \(r(x)=x^2-x+1\) for \(x\ne-1\), and \[\lim_{x\to-1}r(x)=(-1)^2-(-1)+1=3.\] The original function is continuous on \(\mathbb{R}\setminus\{-1\}\). Its graph is the polynomial curve with the point \((-1,3)\) missing. The only continuous extension to all of \(\mathbb{R}\) is \[R(x)= \begin{cases}(x^3+1)/(x+1),&x\ne-1,\\3,&x=-1.\end{cases}\] Indeed, the limit at \(-1\) is \(3\), and this is now \(R(-1)\). If we assigned \(R(-1)=4\) instead, \(R\) would have a removable discontinuity at \(-1\). The algebraic cancellation determines nearby values; it does not define the missing value by itself.

Oscillation and a Finite Jump Are Different

Example 15.4

A Sine and an Inverse Tangent Near a Missing Point

What happens at \(0\) for \(\sin(1/x)\) and \(\arctan(1/x)\)?

Explanation 15.4

The two positive sequences in the earlier oscillation example give sine values \(1\) and \(-1\). Thus even the right-hand limit fails to exist. Negating those inputs shows the same failure on the left. No value at \(0\) can produce continuity: in our classification, this is oscillatory second-kind behaviour.

For the inverse tangent, the limit at positive infinity from Lecture 10 and the identity \(\arctan(-t)=-\arctan t\) give \[\lim_{x\to0^-}\arctan(1/x)=-\frac{\pi}{2},\qquad \lim_{x\to0^+}\arctan(1/x)=\frac{\pi}{2}.\] These are two different finite answers. The signed jump is \(\pi/2-(-\pi/2)=\pi\), and no continuous extension to \(0\) is possible. Although \(1/x\) becomes unbounded, applying inverse tangent changes that behaviour into a finite jump. We classify the final function, rather than an intermediate expression.

An Integer-Valued Staircase

Example 15.5

The Floor Function

Let \(\lfloor x\rfloor\) be the unique integer \(n\) such that \(n\le x<n+1\). Where is the function \(E(x)=\lfloor x\rfloor\) continuous?

Explanation 15.5

If \(c\) is not an integer, let \(n=\lfloor c\rfloor\). Both \(c-n\) and \(n+1-c\) are positive. On the interval \[|x-c|<\delta,\qquad \delta=\frac12\min\{c-n,n+1-c\},\] we still have \(n<x<n+1\), so \(E(x)=n=E(c)\). The output error is exactly zero, proving continuity at \(c\).

At an integer \(n\), inputs just to the left have floor \(n-1\); inputs just to the right have floor \(n\). Consequently \[E(n-)=n-1,\qquad E(n+)=n=E(n).\] There is a jump discontinuity of signed jump \(1\) at every integer. The floor is continuous from the right at an integer, but not continuous there in the two-sided sense.

Example 15.6

Downward Jumps at the Squares

Classify the behaviour of \[s(x)=\sqrt{x}-\lfloor\sqrt{x}\rfloor,\qquad x\ge0.\]

Explanation 15.6

Between consecutive squares, \(n^2<x<(n+1)^2\) with \(n\ge0\), we have \(n<\sqrt{x}<n+1\). Hence \(s(x)=\sqrt{x}-n\) on that entire interval, and the root limit gives continuity there.

At \(x=n^2\) with \(n\ge1\), the floor on the left is \(n-1\), while the floor on the right is \(n\). Therefore \[\lim_{x\to n^2-}s(x)=n-(n-1)=1,\qquad \lim_{x\to n^2+}s(x)=n-n=0=s(n^2).\] The signed jump is \(0-1=-1\). The first-kind discontinuities thus occur at \(1,4,9,\ldots\). At \(0\) there is only the right-hand approach within the domain. For \(0\le x<1\), \(s(x)=\sqrt{x}\), so \(s(x)\to0=s(0)\) as \(x\to0^+\). Thus \(s\) is continuous at its domain endpoint \(0\).

Example 15.7

Two controls: changing a branch or changing one value

For real numbers \(s,t\), let \[h_{s,t}(x)= \begin{cases} x+1,&x<1,\\ t,&x=1,\\ 3-x+s,&x>1. \end{cases}\] Which settings of \(s\) and \(t\) make the graph continuous at \(1\)?

Explanation 15.7

Imagine covering the point directly above \(x=1\) with your finger. From the left the graph approaches height \(2\); from the right it approaches \(2+s\). Neither answer involves \(t\). If \(s\ne0\), the signed jump is \((2+s)-2=s\). Moving the single dot cannot close that gap. If \(s=0\), the two branches agree on the limiting height \(2\). Now uncover the dot: the graph is continuous exactly when \(t=2\), and the discontinuity is removable for every other \(t\). Thus we must settle the branch parameter first and the point value second.

[Figure being adapted for the online edition.]

The filled dot is at the same height in both pictures. Only the left graph can be repaired by moving that dot.

Method 15.1

First record the domain and the value at the point. Then find each available one-sided limit. A finite limit equal to the actual value gives continuity; a finite limit different from it gives a removable mismatch. Different finite one-sided answers give a jump. An infinite or oscillatory side cannot be repaired by changing one value. If the point is absent from the domain, phrase the question as one about continuous extension.

Building New Continuous Functions

Motivation 15.2

Once we know that two functions are continuous, do we have to start a new \(\varepsilon\)–\(\delta\) proof every time we add or multiply them? The limit laws suggest that we should not. Continuity says that the nearby limiting output is the actual output at the point. We can therefore combine the nearby outputs using the same arithmetic that combines the values at the point.

Theorem 15.1

Arithmetic of Continuous Functions

Let \(f,g:A\to\mathbb{R}\) be continuous at \(c\in A\), and let \(\lambda\in\mathbb{R}\). Then \(f+g\), \(f-g\), \(\lambda f\), and \(fg\) are continuous at \(c\). If \(g(c)\ne0\), the quotient \(f/g\), defined where \(g\ne0\), is also continuous at \(c\).

Show proof

Proof. Take any sequence \((x_n)\) in \(A\) with \(x_n\to c\). By the sequential criterion for continuity, \[f(x_n)\longrightarrow f(c),\qquad g(x_n)\longrightarrow g(c).\] The arithmetic laws for sequences give, for example, \[(f+g)(x_n)=f(x_n)+g(x_n)\longrightarrow f(c)+g(c)=(f+g)(c),\] and \[(fg)(x_n)=f(x_n)g(x_n)\longrightarrow f(c)g(c)=(fg)(c).\] The difference and scalar multiple work in the same way. Since the input sequence was arbitrary, the sequential criterion proves each assertion.

For the quotient, first use continuity of \(g\) with tolerance \(|g(c)|/2\). There is a relative neighbourhood of \(c\) on which \[|g(x)|\ge |g(c)|-|g(x)-g(c)|>\frac{|g(c)|}{2}>0.\] Thus the quotient is defined at all sufficiently nearby domain points. For any sequence in its domain tending to \(c\), the quotient law for sequences gives \[\frac{f(x_n)}{g(x_n)}\longrightarrow\frac{f(c)}{g(c)}.\] This is continuity of the quotient at \(c\). ◻

At an accumulation point, the same proof can be written with function limits: use \(\lim f(x)=f(c)\) and \(\lim g(x)=g(c)\) in the arithmetic laws for function limits. The sequential proof also includes isolated points of the domain.

A repeated application gives continuity of every polynomial on \(\mathbb{R}\), since constants and the identity function are continuous. A rational function is continuous on its natural domain, where its denominator is nonzero.

Warning 15.2

The quotient theorem has a hypothesis, \(g(c)\ne0\). If \(g(c)=0\), it does not decide what happens. For example, \((x^2-1)/(x-1)\) is undefined at \(1\), but it simplifies to \(x+1\) away from \(1\) and has a continuous extension there. On the other hand, \(1/(x-1)\) has no finite extension there. First identify the domain; then investigate an excluded point separately if an extension is wanted.

Absolute values, maxima, and minima

The reverse triangle inequality gives \[\bigl||f(x)|-|f(c)|\bigr|\le |f(x)-f(c)|.\] Consequently, \(|f|\) is continuous wherever \(f\) is continuous. Now use \[\max\{f,g\}=\frac{f+g+|f-g|}{2},\qquad \min\{f,g\}=\frac{f+g-|f-g|}{2}.\] The arithmetic theorem shows that the pointwise maximum and minimum of two continuous functions are continuous. The formulas concern two function values at each input; they do not yet assert that a function attains a largest value on an interval.

Example 15.8

A corner does not prevent continuity

Show that \(p(x)=\max\{x,-2x\}\) is continuous on \(\mathbb{R}\).

Explanation 15.8

Both \(x\) and \(-2x\) are continuous. Their pointwise maximum is therefore continuous by the formula above. Alternatively, \[p(x)=\begin{cases}-2x,&x\le0,\\x,&x\ge0.\end{cases}\] The two formulas approach \(0\) at \(0\), which equals \(p(0)\). A sharp corner in a graph is compatible with continuity.

Passing Through a Second Function

Motivation 15.3

Think of two machines in succession. The first changes \(x\) into \(g(x)\); the second changes that output into \(F(g(x))\). At the input \(c\), the intermediate value is \(b=g(c)\). To keep the final output near \(F(b)\), first find how close the second machine needs its input to be to \(b\). Then use continuity of the first machine to meet that requirement.

Theorem 15.2

Continuity of a Composition

Let \(g:A\to B\) and \(F:B\to\mathbb{R}\), where \(A,B\subseteq\mathbb{R}\). Suppose \(g\) is continuous at \(c\in A\) and \(F\) is continuous at \(b=g(c)\in B\). Then \(F\circ g:A\to\mathbb{R}\), defined by \((F\circ g)(x)=F(g(x))\), is continuous at \(c\).

Show proof

Proof. Prescribe \(\varepsilon>0\). Since \(F\) is continuous at \(b\), there is \(\eta>0\) such that \[t\in B,\quad |t-b|<\eta \quad\Longrightarrow\quad |F(t)-F(b)|<\varepsilon.\] Continuity of \(g\) at \(c\) now gives \(\delta>0\) such that \[x\in A,\quad |x-c|<\delta \quad\Longrightarrow\quad |g(x)-g(c)|<\eta.\] Since \(g(c)=b\) and \(g(x)\in B\), apply the first implication to \(t=g(x)\). For every such \(x\), \[|F(g(x))-F(g(c))|<\varepsilon.\] This proves continuity of the composition. Notice that \(g(x)=g(c)\) is allowed: then the final error is exactly zero. ◻

[Figure being adapted for the online edition.]

There is an equally short sequential proof. If \(x_n\to c\) with \(x_n\in A\), then \(g(x_n)\to g(c)=b\). Applying continuity of \(F\) to this intermediate sequence gives \(F(g(x_n))\to F(b)\). This is exactly the sequential criterion for the composition.

Remark 15.1

This theorem justifies passing a limit through a continuous outer function. If \(u(x)\to L>0\), then \[\lim_{x\to c}\ln u(x)=\ln L =\ln\left(\lim_{x\to c}u(x)\right).\] If \(v(x)\to B\in\mathbb{R}\), then \(e^{v(x)}\to e^B\). Thus taking logarithms and then exponentiating in Lecture 11 were applications of continuity. We check continuity of these familiar outer functions below, using the standard limits already accepted in that lecture. This does not supply a new proof of the fundamental exponential standard limit itself.

Continuity of the Familiar Building Blocks

The continuity rules save work only after we know our basic functions are continuous. Here are the checks needed for the examples that follow. All angles are in radians.

Sine and cosine. The angle identities give, for \(x\) close to \(c\), \[|\sin x-\sin c| =2\left|\cos\frac{x+c}{2}\sin\frac{x-c}{2}\right| \le |x-c|,\] using \(|\sin t|\le |t|\) near \(0\). Similarly, \[|\cos x-\cos c| =2\left|\sin\frac{x+c}{2}\sin\frac{x-c}{2}\right| \le |x-c|.\] For either function, \(\delta=\varepsilon\) together with a sufficiently small fixed radius gives an \(\varepsilon\)–\(\delta\) proof at every \(c\). The quotient rule now gives continuity of \(\tan x=\sin x/\cos x\) wherever \(\cos x\ne0\).

Exponential. The standard limit \((e^h-1)/h\to1\) implies \(e^h-1\to0\), since it equals \(h(e^h-1)/h\) for \(h\ne0\), and is zero at \(h=0\). Hence \[e^x-e^c=e^c\bigl(e^{x-c}-1\bigr)\longrightarrow0\qquad(x\to c).\] So \(e^x\) is continuous on \(\mathbb{R}\).

Logarithm. For \(c>0\), put \(u=(x-c)/c\), so \(x=c(1+u)\) and \(u\to0\). The logarithmic standard limit gives \[\ln x-\ln c=\ln(1+u)\longrightarrow0,\] because \(\ln(1+u)=u[\ln(1+u)/u]\) when \(u\ne0\), with value zero at \(u=0\). Thus \(\ln x\) is continuous on \((0,\infty)\). Since \(\log_a x=\ln x/\ln a\), the same holds for every \(a>0\), \(a\ne1\). The formula \(a^x=e^{x\ln a}\) and composition give continuity of \(a^x\) for \(a>0\).

Roots. The root-input estimates from Lecture 11 show that \(x^{1/k}\) is continuous on \([0,\infty)\) for even \(k\), and on \(\mathbb{R}\) for odd \(k\). At the endpoint \(0\) of the even-root domain, continuity is relative to that domain: only nonnegative inputs are tested.

Example 15.9

Three quotients: start by finding the domain

Determine where the following functions are continuous:

\(\displaystyle R(x)=\frac{2x^5-8x^2+11}{x^4+4x^3+8x^2+8x+4}\);

\(\displaystyle S(x)=\frac{3\sin^3x+\cos^2x+1}{4\cos x-2}\);

\(\displaystyle T(x)=\frac{x^3\cos x+x^2\sin x}{\cos(1/\sin x)}\).

Explanation 15.9

(a) The numerator and denominator are polynomials. Rather than trying to solve a quartic equation, recognise the denominator: \[x^4+4x^3+8x^2+8x+4=(x^2+2x+2)^2 =\bigl((x+1)^2+1\bigr)^2>0.\] The quotient theorem applies at every real point. Thus \(R\) is defined and continuous on all of \(\mathbb{R}\).

(b) The numerator is continuous by the arithmetic rules. The only domain restriction comes from \[4\cos x-2\ne0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\cos x\ne\frac12.\] Consequently \(S\) is continuous on \[\mathbb{R}\setminus\{\,2\pi k+\pi/3,\ 2\pi k-\pi/3:k\in\mathbb{Z}\,\}.\] Here these excluded points really cannot be filled by finite values: if \(\cos x_0=1/2\), then \(\sin x_0=\pm\sqrt3/2\), and the numerator there equals \((10\pm9\sqrt3)/8\), which is nonzero. The denominator tends to zero, so the necessary condition for a finite quotient limit rules out a finite extension.

(c) Follow the operations in their actual order: \[x\ \longmapsto\ \sin x\ \longmapsto\ \frac1{\sin x} \ \longmapsto\ \cos\!\left(\frac1{\sin x}\right).\] The reciprocal requires \(\sin x\ne0\), and the final division requires \(\cos(1/\sin x)\ne0\). Hence the natural domain is \[D=\{x\in\mathbb{R}:\sin x\ne0\ \hbox{and}\ \cos(1/\sin x)\ne0\}.\] At every point of \(D\), each intermediate operation is continuous and all divisions have nonzero denominators. Therefore \(T\) is continuous on \(D\). This argument determines continuity on the domain; it does not classify every excluded point without a further limit calculation.

Example 15.10

Three compositions and their real domains

Discuss the continuity of \[\text{(a)}\quad \cos(x^n)\quad(n\in\mathbb{N}),\qquad \text{(b)}\quad\cos(\ln x),\qquad \text{(c)}\quad\sqrt{\frac12-\cos^2x}.\]

Explanation 15.10

(a) The polynomial \(x^n\) is continuous on \(\mathbb{R}\), and cosine is continuous at every real output it produces. Their composition is continuous on \(\mathbb{R}\).

(b) The inner logarithm requires \(x>0\) and is continuous there. Cosine is continuous at every \(\ln x\). Thus the composition is continuous on \((0,\infty)\).

(c) A real square root requires \(\cos^2x\le1/2\). Equivalently, \[D=\bigcup_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\left[\frac\pi4+k\pi,\frac{3\pi}4+k\pi\right].\] The inner expression \(1/2-\cos^2x\) is continuous and takes nonnegative values on \(D\). The square-root function is continuous even at its endpoint \(0\), so the composition is continuous on all of \(D\), including its interval endpoints. We use continuity relative to \(D\) there; values outside the real domain are not inputs to the function.

What Composition Can Do to a Discontinuity

The composition theorem is a sufficient condition: two continuous functions give a continuous composition. Its converse is not true. An outer function can send different intermediate outputs to the same final output. Also, simplifying a formula can suggest an extension without changing its original domain.

Example 15.11

A reciprocal inside a rational function

Let \[u=\frac1{x-1},\qquad y=\frac1{u^2+u-2}.\] Find the natural domain and investigate its excluded points.

Explanation 15.11

The inner reciprocal excludes \(x=1\). The outer denominator factors as \((u+2)(u-1)\), so we must also exclude \(u=-2\) and \(u=1\). These correspond to \(x=1/2\) and \(x=2\). On the original domain, \[y=\frac{(x-1)^2}{1+(x-1)-2(x-1)^2} =\frac{(x-1)^2}{(2x-1)(2-x)}.\] Thus the function is continuous on \(\mathbb{R}\setminus\{1/2,1,2\}\). At \(x=1\), the simplified numerator tends to \(0\) and its denominator tends to \(1\). The limit is \(0\), so assigning \(y(1)=0\) gives a continuous extension. The original composite still did not have a value there.

At \(1/2\) and \(2\), the numerator tends to a positive number and the denominator tends to zero. These are nonremovable singularities. The factors give the signs explicitly: \[\lim_{x\to(1/2)^-}y=-\infty,\quad \lim_{x\to(1/2)^+}y=+\infty,\qquad \lim_{x\to2^-}y=+\infty,\quad \lim_{x\to2^+}y=-\infty.\]

Example 15.12

Squaring removes an inner jump

Let \[u(x)=\begin{cases}x-1,&x\ge0,\\x+1,&x<0,\end{cases} \qquad y(x)=[u(x)]^2.\] Discuss continuity of \(u\) and \(y\) at \(0\).

Explanation 15.12

The left-hand limit of \(u\) is \(1\), while its right-hand limit and value at \(0\) are \(-1\). Thus \(u\) has a jump at \(0\). After squaring, both limiting outputs become \(1\), and \(y(0)=1\). More explicitly, \[y(x)=\begin{cases}(x-1)^2,&x\ge0,\\(x+1)^2,&x<0, \end{cases} \qquad |y(x)-1|\le x^2+2|x|\longrightarrow0.\] Therefore \(y\) is continuous at \(0\), and is continuous elsewhere by the polynomial formulas. The outer function \(t\mapsto t^2\) turns the two intermediate heights \(1\) and \(-1\) into the same height.

Example 15.13

An identity gives a continuous extension

Let \(u=\tan x\) and \(y=(1-u^2)/(1+u^2)\). Determine its continuity and whether its missing inputs can be filled.

Explanation 15.13

The outer denominator \(1+u^2\) is positive for every real \(u\), but the inner tangent requires \(\cos x\ne0\). On that domain, multiply numerator and denominator by \(\cos^2x\): \[y=\frac{\cos^2x-\sin^2x}{\cos^2x+\sin^2x}=\cos2x.\] The original composite is continuous on \(\mathbb{R}\setminus\{\pi/2+k\pi:k\in\mathbb{Z}\}\). At each missing input \(c\), its limit is \(\cos2c=-1\). Defining the missing value to be \(-1\) gives the continuous function \(\cos2x\) on the entire real line. Simplification found the extension; it did not retroactively define \(\tan c\).

Can a Continuous Function Escape Every Bound?

Motivation 15.4

Continuity controls what happens near each input. Does that also control all the outputs at once? For \(f(x)=1/x\) on \((0,1]\), every input has a small neighbourhood where the graph behaves well. Yet the outputs become arbitrarily large when the inputs approach the missing endpoint \(0\).

A closed, bounded interval prevents this particular escape. We cannot travel farther and farther along the input line, and we cannot approach an endpoint that has been left out. Our sequence theorems let us turn this picture into a proof.

Definition 15.3

A bounded function

A function \(f:A\to\mathbb{R}\) is bounded on \(A\) if there is a number \(M>0\) such that \[|f(x)|\le M\qquad\hbox{for every }x\in A.\] It is unbounded on \(A\) if \[\text{for every }M>0\text{ there is }x_M\in A \text{ with }|f(x_M)|>M.\] Thus boundedness of a function means boundedness of its set of outputs \(f(A)=\{f(x):x\in A\}\).

For the reciprocal example, given any \(M>0\), choose \(x_M=1/(M+1)\). This input lies in \((0,1]\), and \(f(x_M)=M+1>M\). Hence continuity alone does not imply boundedness.

There is nevertheless a useful local fact. If \(f\) is continuous at \(c\), apply its definition with output tolerance \(1\). On a sufficiently small neighbourhood of \(c\) within its domain, \[|f(x)-f(c)|<1 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad |f(x)|<|f(c)|+1.\] The neighbourhood and the bound here may depend on \(c\). We have not yet obtained one bound for the whole domain.

Why a closed interval keeps the limit

We will use the following consequence of inequalities. If \(x_n\in[a,b]\) for every \(n\) and \(x_n\to c\), then \(c\in[a,b]\). For if \(c<a\), the positive tolerance \((a-c)/2\) would eventually give \[x_n<c+\frac{a-c}{2}=\frac{a+c}{2}<a,\] contradicting \(x_n\ge a\). If \(c>b\), the tolerance \((c-b)/2\) would eventually give \(x_n>b\), another contradiction. Thus \(a\le c\le b\).

Together with Bolzano–Weierstrass, this says that every sequence in \([a,b]\) has a subsequence whose limit still belongs to \([a,b]\). Boundedness supplies the convergent subsequence; the included endpoints keep its limit in the domain.

Theorem 15.3

Boundedness on a closed, bounded interval

Let \(a,b\in\mathbb{R}\) with \(a\le b\). If \(f:[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}\) is continuous at every point of \([a,b]\), then \(f\) is bounded on \([a,b]\). At the endpoints, continuity is understood relative to the interval.

Show proof

Proof. Suppose, to the contrary, that \(f\) is unbounded. For each positive integer \(n\), choose \(x_n\in[a,b]\) such that \[|f(x_n)|>n.\] The input sequence is bounded because \(a\le x_n\le b\). Bolzano–Weierstrass gives a subsequence \((x_{n_j})\) converging to some \(c\in\mathbb{R}\). The preceding interval argument shows that \(c\in[a,b]\).

The function is continuous at this limit point. The sequential criterion for continuity therefore gives \[f(x_{n_j})\longrightarrow f(c).\] Using tolerance \(1\) in this last sequence limit, there is an index \(J\) such that \[|f(x_{n_j})|<|f(c)|+1\qquad(j\ge J).\] But the subsequence indices are increasing positive integers, so \(n_j\ge j\). Choose \(j\ge J\) with \(j>|f(c)|+1\). Our original choice of inputs now gives the contradiction \[|f(x_{n_j})|>n_j\ge j>|f(c)|+1.\] Consequently \(f\) must be bounded. ◻

The argument used three facts in a definite order: the input sequence was bounded; its subsequence limit remained in the interval; continuity then made the output subsequence converge to a finite number.

Example 15.14

Why each assumption is present

Can we drop boundedness of the interval, inclusion of its endpoints, or continuity of the function?

Explanation 15.14

An unbounded interval. The function \(f(x)=x\) is continuous on \([0,\infty)\), but its outputs have no upper bound.

A missing endpoint. The function \(g(x)=1/x\) is continuous on \((0,1]\), but is unbounded there. The inputs \(x_n=1/n\) approach the omitted endpoint \(0\).

A discontinuous function. On the closed, bounded interval \([0,1]\), put \[h(x)= \begin{cases} 0,&x=0,\\ 1/x,&0<x\le1. \end{cases}\] Now the endpoint belongs to the domain, but the function is discontinuous there. Its outputs at \(1/n\) still equal \(n\), so it is unbounded. Each omitted hypothesis can therefore allow the conclusion to fail.

Bounds and Values That Are Actually Reached

Motivation 15.5

Suppose the outputs all lie below some ceiling. The least possible ceiling is their supremum. Must the graph actually touch it? For \(f(x)=x\) on \((0,1)\), the supremum of the outputs is \(1\), but no input gives the output \(1\). Being a least upper bound and being a largest attained value are different statements.

We will prove that a continuous function on \([a,b]\) reaches both its supremum and its infimum.

Definition 15.4

Absolute maximum and absolute minimum

Let \(f:A\to\mathbb{R}\). A point \(x_{\max}\in A\) is an absolute maximum point if \[f(x)\le f(x_{\max})\qquad(x\in A).\] The number \(f(x_{\max})\) is the absolute maximum value. Similarly, \(x_{\min}\in A\) is an absolute minimum point if \[f(x_{\min})\le f(x)\qquad(x\in A).\] The number \(f(x_{\min})\) is the absolute minimum value.

If \(A\) is nonempty and \(f(A)\) is bounded, completeness gives the numbers \(\sup f(A)\) and \(\inf f(A)\). An absolute maximum exists exactly when some input produces \(\sup f(A)\); an absolute minimum exists exactly when some input produces \(\inf f(A)\). There can be several inputs giving the same extremal value.

Example 15.15

The same formula on different intervals

For \(f(x)=1/x\), determine which absolute extrema exist on each of the following domains.

Explanation 15.15

For positive \(x\), the reciprocal decreases as \(x\) increases: if \(0<x<y\), then \(1/x>1/y\). The endpoint values and limits give the following conclusions. \[\begin{array}{c|c|c} \text{domain}&\text{absolute minimum}&\text{absolute maximum}\\ \hline (0,\infty)&\text{none}&\text{none}\\ (0,1)&\text{none}&\text{none}\\ {[1,2]}&1/2\text{ at }2&1\text{ at }1\\ {[1,\infty)}&\text{none}&1\text{ at }1\\ (1,\infty)&\text{none}&\text{none} \end{array}\] On \((0,\infty)\) and \((0,1)\) the outputs are unbounded above. On \((0,1)\) the infimum is \(1\), but \(1\) is not an output there. On \([1,\infty)\) the infimum is \(0\), which is never attained. On \((1,\infty)\) the supremum is \(1\) and the infimum is \(0\); neither belongs to the range. On \([1,2]\), both endpoint values are outputs, so both extrema exist.

Theorem 15.4

The maximum–minimum theorem

Let \(a,b\in\mathbb{R}\) with \(a\le b\). If \(f:[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}\) is continuous, then there are points \(x_{\min},x_{\max}\in[a,b]\) such that \[f(x_{\min})\le f(x)\le f(x_{\max}) \qquad\text{for every }x\in[a,b].\] Equivalently, \(f\) attains the infimum and the supremum of its range.

Show proof

Proof. By the boundedness theorem, the nonempty range \(f([a,b])\) is bounded. Let \[S=\sup f([a,b]),\qquad I=\inf f([a,b]).\]

The supremum is attained. For each positive integer \(n\), the number \(S-1/n\) is strictly below the least upper bound. It cannot itself be an upper bound. Therefore we can choose \(u_n\in[a,b]\) with \[S-\frac1n<f(u_n)\le S.\] The sequence \((u_n)\) is bounded. Bolzano–Weierstrass supplies a subsequence \((u_{n_j})\) converging to some \(x_{\max}\in[a,b]\). The limit lies in the interval by the interval argument above. Since \(f\) is continuous there, \(f(u_{n_j})\to f(x_{\max})\). On the other hand, \[0\le S-f(u_{n_j})<\frac1{n_j}\le\frac1j,\] so \(f(u_{n_j})\to S\). Uniqueness of the sequence limit gives \(f(x_{\max})=S\).

The infimum is attained. For each positive integer \(n\), the number \(I+1/n\) is strictly above the greatest lower bound. It is not a lower bound. Hence there is \(v_n\in[a,b]\) such that \[I\le f(v_n)<I+\frac1n.\] Choose a convergent subsequence \((v_{m_j})\) of this bounded input sequence; write its limit as \(x_{\min}\in[a,b]\). Continuity gives \(f(v_{m_j})\to f(x_{\min})\), while \[0\le f(v_{m_j})-I<\frac1{m_j}\le\frac1j\] gives \(f(v_{m_j})\to I\). Thus \(f(x_{\min})=I\).

Finally, the definitions of \(I\) and \(S\) give \(f(x_{\min})=I\le f(x)\le S=f(x_{\max})\) for every input in the interval. ◻

Remark 15.2

The supremum argument first chooses inputs whose outputs are almost as large as possible. It then chooses a convergent subsequence of those inputs. Continuity is the step that makes the limiting input produce the limiting output. No derivative or formula for the maximising input is needed.

The theorem guarantees extremal points; it does not say that they are unique. For \(f(x)=x^2\) on \([-1,1]\), the maximum value \(1\) occurs at both \(-1\) and \(1\), while the minimum value \(0\) occurs only at \(0\). For a constant function, every input is both a maximum point and a minimum point.

Example 15.16

Finding extrema without differentiating

Find the absolute maximum and minimum values of \[F(x)=|x-1|+|x+1|\qquad(-2\le x\le2),\] and find every point where each is attained.

Explanation 15.16

The absolute-value function is continuous, so the arithmetic laws make \(F\) continuous. The maximum–minimum theorem tells us that both extrema exist. To find them, split at the places where the signs change: \[F(x)= \begin{cases} -2x,&-2\le x\le-1,\\ 2,&-1\le x\le1,\\ 2x,&1\le x\le2. \end{cases}\] On the left piece the values decrease from \(4\) to \(2\); in the middle they equal \(2\); on the right they increase from \(2\) to \(4\). Consequently the minimum value is \(2\), attained at every \(x\in[-1,1]\), and the maximum value is \(4\), attained exactly at \(x=-2\) and \(x=2\).

Example 15.17

Bounded, but the supremum is missed

Define \(q:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}\) by \[q(x)= \begin{cases} x,&0\le x<1,\\ 0,&x=1. \end{cases}\] Is boundedness on a closed interval enough to guarantee an absolute maximum?

Explanation 15.17

The range is \([0,1)\), so the function is bounded. Its supremum is \(1\), but no input has output \(1\). Indeed, if an output \(t<1\) were the largest, the input \(y=(t+1)/2\in[0,1)\) would have \(q(y)=y>t\). Thus there is no absolute maximum.

The failure occurs at the discontinuity at \(1\): \(\lim_{x\to1^-}q(x)=1\) whereas \(q(1)=0\). There is an absolute minimum, namely \(0\), attained at both \(0\) and \(1\). The example shows why the maximum–minimum theorem asks for continuity as well as a closed, bounded input interval.

Example 15.18

Which point of the track is closest to the lamp?

A curved track follows \(y=x^2\) for \(-1\le x\le 1\). A lamp is at \(P=(0,1)\). The point directly below it is \((0,0)\). Is that the closest point of the track?

Explanation 15.18

For a track point \(Q=(x,x^2)\), the squared distance from the lamp is \[D(x)=PQ^2=x^2+(x^2-1)^2.\] Before calculating anything, we know that there is a closest point: \(D\) is continuous and \([-1,1]\) is closed and bounded, so \(D\) attains its minimum by the maximum–minimum theorem. Because distances are nonnegative, the smallest squared distance and the smallest distance occur at the same inputs. To find them, complete the square: \[D(x)=\left(x^2-\tfrac12\right)^2+\tfrac34.\] The square vanishes at \(x=\pm 1/\sqrt 2\), both on the track. There are two closest points, \((\pm 1/\sqrt2,1/2)\), each at distance \(\sqrt3/2\). The point directly below the lamp is a full unit away.

A circle centred at the lamp first meets the track at two points.

The existence argument works for any continuous track \(y=f(x)\) over \([a,b]\) and any lamp \((u,v)\): the continuous function \((x-u)^2+(f(x)-v)^2\) attains a minimum. The theorem guarantees at least one closest point even when the algebra does not tell us where it is.

Example 15.19

If every distance from zero can be halved, must zero be reached?

Let \(f:[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}\) be continuous. Suppose that for every \(x\in[a,b]\) one can find \(y\in[a,b]\) with \(|f(y)|\le \tfrac12|f(x)|\). Prove that \(f(c)=0\) for some \(c\in[a,b]\).

Explanation 15.19

The number \(|f(x)|\) measures how far the output is from zero. The point \(y\) can depend on \(x\); there need not be a single \(y\) that works for everybody.

First proof: choose the closest output. The function \(h(x)=|f(x)|\) is continuous: a change in its value is at most the corresponding change in \(f\), since \(\bigl||f(x)|-|f(t)|\bigr|\le |f(x)-f(t)|\). By the maximum–minimum theorem, \(h\) has a smallest value \(m=h(c)\) at some input \(c\in[a,b]\). Apply the hypothesis at this particular input. It gives a \(y\) with \[0\le m\le |f(y)|\le \tfrac12|f(c)|=\tfrac12m .\] A positive number cannot be at most half itself. Thus \(m=0\), and \(f(c)=0\).

Second proof: keep choosing a closer output. Start at any \(x_0\in[a,b]\). Having chosen \(x_n\), choose \(x_{n+1}\) so that \(|f(x_{n+1})|\le |f(x_n)|/2\). Repeating the bound gives \(0\le |f(x_n)|\le 2^{-n}|f(x_0)|\), so the outputs tend to zero. We do not yet know that the inputs converge. They do all lie in \([a,b]\), however. By Bolzano–Weierstrass, some subsequence \(x_{n_j}\) converges to a point \(c\). Taking limits in \(a\le x_{n_j}\le b\) shows that \(c\in[a,b]\). By sequential continuity, \(f(x_{n_j})\to f(c)\). The same outputs tend to zero, hence \(f(c)=0\).

On \((0,1]\) the function \(f(x)=x\) lets us take \(y=x/2\) every time, yet it never vanishes. Its outputs get arbitrarily close to zero, but the limiting input \(0\) is missing.
