Direct Epsilon Proofs, Estimates, and Ratios

Read this lecture online, then continue to the next chapter. Figures and exercises are being adapted separately for the web edition.

Bernoulli’s inequality before we use it

We shall soon compare powers and roots with simple fractions. Before doing so, let us prove the inequality that makes those comparisons possible. Its proof uses only the principle of induction from Lecture [lec:1].

Theorem 4.1

Bernoulli’s Inequality

For every real \(u\ge-1\) and every \(n\in\mathbb{N}\), \[(1+u)^n\ge1+nu.\]

Show proof

Proof. For \(n=1\) the two sides are equal. Suppose the inequality holds for a particular \(n\). Since \(1+u\ge0\), multiplying preserves the inequality: \[(1+u)^{n+1}\ge(1+nu)(1+u) =1+(n+1)u+nu^2\ge1+(n+1)u.\] This is exactly the assertion for \(n+1\), so induction completes the proof. ◻

For example, taking \(u=1\) gives \(2^n\ge1+n\), and taking \(u=2\) gives \(3^n\ge1+2n\). We now have a proved tool, rather than a pattern suggested by a few numerical values.

Theorem 4.2

A Quadratic Estimate for Positive Powers

For \(u\ge0\) and \(n\ge2\), \[(1+u)^n\ge1+nu+\frac{n(n-1)}2u^2 \ge1+\frac{n(n-1)}2u^2.\]

Show proof

Proof. At \(n=2\) the first inequality is equality. If it holds at \(n\), multiply by \(1+u\) and collect the terms: \[\begin{aligned} (1+u)^{n+1} &\ge1+(n+1)u+\left(n+\frac{n(n-1)}2\right)u^2 +\frac{n(n-1)}2u^3\\ &\ge1+(n+1)u+\frac{n(n+1)}2u^2. \end{aligned}\] The discarded cubic term is nonnegative. Induction proves the first inequality; discarding \(nu\ge0\) proves the second. This is the part of the finite binomial expansion that we need, with its proof supplied here. The abbreviation \(\binom n2=n(n-1)/2\) will be used for its quadratic coefficient. ◻

Remark 4.1

For \(t>0\) and a fixed real \(p>0\), we use the usual positive power \(t^p\): it increases with \(t\), obeys the exponent laws, and has inverse \(t^{1/p}\). For a fixed base \(a>1\), \(a^t\) is positive and increases with \(t\); \(\log_a t\) denotes its inverse, so \(a^{\log_a t}=t\) for \(t>0\). For \(0<a<1\), \(a^t\) decreases with \(t\). The natural logarithm \(\ln t\) is the logarithm to base \(e>1\). These algebraic and order properties are our previously familiar precalculus facts; no limit, continuity, or calculus rule for powers or logarithms is being assumed in our proofs. Likewise we use the elementary trigonometric bound \(|\sin t|,|\cos t|\le1\).

Direct \(\varepsilon\)–\(N\) Proofs

In the previous lecture, the pendulum story explained what our promise means. Today we will learn to calculate a waiting index that fulfils it. I give you a positive window radius \(\varepsilon\); your task is to tell me how far along the sequence I must go so that every later term lies inside.

Method 4.1

Start from the desired inequality \(\abs{x_n-L}<\varepsilon\). Simplify it until you obtain a condition of the form \(n>F(\varepsilon)\). Then choose an integer \[N>F(\varepsilon),\] for example \(N=\floor{F(\varepsilon)}+1\).

Example 4.1

The Sequence \(1/n\)

Prove directly that \(1/n\to0\).

Show worked solution

Let \(\varepsilon>0\). We want \[\abs{\frac1n-0}=\frac1n<\varepsilon.\] This holds whenever \(n>1/\varepsilon\). Choose \(N\in\mathbb{N}\) with \(N>1/\varepsilon\). Then \(n\ge N\) implies \[\abs{\frac1n}\le\frac1N<\varepsilon.\] Therefore \(1/n\to0\).

The same calculation proves \[1-\frac1n\to1,\] because \(\abs{(1-1/n)-1}=1/n\).

Example 4.2

A Faster Sequence

Prove that \[\frac1{n^2+1}\to0.\]

Show worked solution

For every \(n\ge1\), \[0<\frac1{n^2+1}<\frac1{n^2}\le\frac1n.\] Given \(\varepsilon>0\), choose \(N>1/\varepsilon\). If \(n\ge N\), then \[\abs{\frac1{n^2+1}-0}<\frac1n\le\frac1N<\varepsilon.\]

Example 4.3

A Rational Expression

Prove that \[\frac{3n+2}{n+1}\to3.\]

Show worked solution

The key is to subtract the proposed limit: \[\abs{\frac{3n+2}{n+1}-3} =\abs{\frac{-1}{n+1}} =\frac1{n+1}<\frac1n.\] Choose \(N>1/\varepsilon\). Then \(n\ge N\) gives the required inequality.

Example 4.4

Geometric Decay

If \(0<b<1\), prove that \(b^n\to0\).

Show worked solution

Put \(u=b^{-1}-1>0\), so \(b=1/(1+u)\). By Bernoulli’s inequality, \[0<b^n=\frac1{(1+u)^n}\le\frac1{1+nu}<\frac1{nu}.\] Given \(\varepsilon>0\), choose a natural number \(N>1/(u\varepsilon)\). For every \(n\ge N\), \(|b^n-0|<1/(nu)\le1/(Nu)<\varepsilon\). This proves the claim directly.

If we want a sharper numerical waiting index, the logarithmic order properties stated above give \[b^n<\varepsilon\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad n\log b<\log\varepsilon\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad n>\frac{\log\varepsilon}{\log b}.\] The final inequality reverses because \(\log b<0\). We choose \(N\in\mathbb{N}\) larger than this number. For instance, when \(b=0.8\) and \(\varepsilon=0.01\), \(\log(0.01)/\log(0.8)\approx20.64\), so \(N=21\) works.

More Worked Epsilon Estimates

A formula may help us guess where the terms are heading. Now ask the question from the pendulum story: for a window of radius \(\varepsilon\), where can we begin so that every later term stays inside? Keep that question beside you through the following calculations. Several different looking sequences will lead us to the same kind of estimate.

Example 4.5

A Power in the Denominator

For \(p>0\), prove directly that \(n^{-p}\to0\).

Show worked solution

Given \(\varepsilon>0\), the inequality \(n^{-p}<\varepsilon\) is equivalent to \(n>\varepsilon^{-1/p}\). Choose \(N=\lfloor\varepsilon^{-1/p}\rfloor+1\). For every \(n\ge N\) we have \(0<n^{-p}\le N^{-p}<\varepsilon\). The exponent need not be an integer; only its positivity matters. The same proof works for \(p=1/2\), which means \(1/\sqrt n\to0\).

Example 4.6

Oscillation with a Shrinking Amplitude

Find and prove the limits of \[u_n=\frac{1-(-1)^n}{n},\qquad v_n=\frac{\sin((2n-1)\pi/2)}{n},\qquad w_n=\frac{2(-1)^n}{5\sqrt[3]{n}+1}.\] For \(w_n\), find the first \(N\) such that \(|w_n|<1/10\) for every \(n\ge N\).

Show worked solution

Each limit is zero. The numerator of \(u_n\) is \(0\) or \(2\), so \(|u_n|\le2/n\). Also \(|v_n|=1/n\), while \(|w_n|=2/(5\sqrt[3]n+1)<1/\sqrt[3]n\). For arbitrary \(\varepsilon>0\) choose an integer \(N>\max\{2/\varepsilon,1/\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{-3}\}\). Then each of the three absolute values is below \(\varepsilon\) for all \(n\ge N\). This convenient common choice is usually far from the smallest possible one.

For the requested sharp value solve the original inequality: \[\frac2{5\sqrt[3]n+1}<\frac1{10} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \sqrt[3]n>\frac{19}{5} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad n>\left(\frac{19}{5}\right)^3=54.872.\] Thus \(N=55\) is the first possible index. By comparison, the simple bound \(|w_n|<n^{-1/3}\) would give the less efficient choice \(N=1001\).

Example 4.7

Large Terms with a Small Perturbation

Evaluate and justify \[\frac{3^{n+1}+\sin(n\pi/4)}{3^n}, \qquad \frac{2^n+(-1)^n}{2^n} \qquad(n\to\infty).\]

Show worked solution

Rewrite the first term as \(3+\sin(n\pi/4)/3^n\) and the second as \(1+(-1)^n/2^n\). Their errors from \(3\) and \(1\) are at most \(3^{-n}\) and \(2^{-n}\). For example \(3^n\ge n+1\) and \(2^n\ge n+1\) by induction, so either error is at most \(1/(n+1)\). Choosing \(N>1/\varepsilon\) gives a direct epsilon proof of the respective limits \(3\) and \(1\).

Example 4.8

The \(n\) th Root of \(n\) : A Direct Proof

Prove \(n^{1/n}\to1\) without a squeeze theorem.

Show worked solution

For \(n\ge2\) write \(n^{1/n}=1+t_n\) with \(t_n\ge0\). The quadratic estimate proved above gives \[n=(1+t_n)^n\ge1+\binom n2t_n^2.\] Subtracting \(1\) gives \(n-1\ge n(n-1)t_n^2/2\); since \(n\ge2\), we may divide by \(n-1>0\) to obtain \(t_n^2\le2/n\). Given \(\varepsilon>0\), choose \(N>2/\varepsilon^2\) and \(N\ge2\). Then for every \(n\ge N\), \(|n^{1/n}-1|=t_n\le\sqrt{2/n}<\varepsilon\). This is the definition of the limit. Notice the useful step: the proved quadratic estimate gives a bound made smaller than any prescribed error by choosing \(n\) large enough, whereas the linear estimate \(n\ge1+nt_n\) yields only \(t_n<1\).

Definition 4.1

Divergence to Positive or Negative Infinity

We write \(x_n\to+\infty\) if, for every real \(M\), there is \(N\in\mathbb{N}\) such that \(x_n>M\) for every \(n\ge N\). We write \(x_n\to-\infty\) if, for every real \(M\), there is \(N\in\mathbb{N}\) such that \(x_n<M\) for every \(n\ge N\). These statements describe eventual passage beyond every fixed height; they are not convergence to a real number. In particular, \(n\to+\infty\): choose \(N>M\) using the Archimedean property.

Example 4.9

Constant Roots and an Infinite Limit

For \(a>0\), prove \(a^{1/n}\to1\). Also prove that \(3^{\sqrt[3]n}\to+\infty\).

Show worked solution

If \(a>1\), write \(a^{1/n}=1+d_n\). Bernoulli’s inequality gives \(a=(1+d_n)^n\ge1+nd_n\), so \(0\le d_n\le(a-1)/n\). For a given \(\varepsilon>0\), take \(N>(a-1)/\varepsilon\); then \(|a^{1/n}-1|<\varepsilon\) for every \(n\ge N\). For \(a=1\) the error is identically zero. If \(0<a<1\), put \(b=1/a>1\) and use \[0<1-a^{1/n}=1-\frac1{b^{1/n}} \le b^{1/n}-1\le\frac{b-1}{n}.\] In this case choose \(N>(b-1)/\varepsilon\); the same direct check gives \(|a^{1/n}-1|<\varepsilon\) for all \(n\ge N\).

For the second claim, the meaning is: given \(M\in\mathbb{R}\), all sufficiently late terms exceed \(M\). If \(M\le1\), this is automatic. If \(M>1\), choose an integer \(N>(\log_3 M)^3\). For every \(n\ge N\) we have \(\sqrt[3]n>\log_3 M\) and hence \(3^{\sqrt[3]n}>M\). This is divergence to \(+\infty\), not convergence to a real number.

Building an Estimate before Writing the Proof

When you see a finished epsilon proof, the choice of \(N\) can look as though it appeared from nowhere. Let us keep the rough work on the page. We first ask what would make \(|a_n-L|\) smaller than the window radius, simplify that error, and bound it by an expression we can control. Only then do we write the proof in its final order: start with an arbitrary \(\varepsilon>0\), choose \(N\), and check every \(n\ge N\). This is how we will use the definition of convergence in the next two examples.

Example 4.10

A Rational Sequence with Two Scales

Prove directly that \(a_n=(3n^2-2n+4)/(n^2+n+1)\to3\).

Show worked solution

Subtracting the proposed limit is more useful than estimating the original fraction: \[|a_n-3|=\frac{|-5n+1|}{n^2+n+1} \le\frac{5n+1}{n^2}\le\frac6n\qquad(n\ge1).\] Given \(\varepsilon>0\), choose an integer \(N>6/\varepsilon\). For every \(n\ge N\), \(|a_n-3|\le6/n\le6/N<\varepsilon\). This proves convergence. The constant \(6\) is convenient, not optimal. An estimate need only be strong enough for the desired conclusion.

Example 4.11

Rationalisation Finds the Correct Scale

Determine the limit of \(\sqrt{n^2+3n}-n\).

Show worked solution

Both terms grow without bound, so separating their limits is invalid. Rationalisation gives \[\sqrt{n^2+3n}-n =\frac{3n}{\sqrt{n^2+3n}+n} =\frac3{\sqrt{1+3/n}+1}.\] The inequality \(0\le\sqrt{1+t}-1=t/(\sqrt{1+t}+1)\le t/2\) for \(t\ge0\) suggests that the answer should be \(3/2\). We have not yet proved a rule for limits of quotients, so let us subtract that proposed answer and control the error directly: \[0\le\frac32-\frac3{\sqrt{1+3/n}+1} =\frac{3(\sqrt{1+3/n}-1)} {2(\sqrt{1+3/n}+1)} \le\frac{9}{8n}.\] Given \(\varepsilon>0\), choose \(N>9/(8\varepsilon)\). For every \(n\ge N\), the displayed error is less than \(\varepsilon\), so the limit is \(3/2\).

Remark 4.2

In Lecture [lec:5], the quotient law will provide a shorter route after rationalisation. Our proof here uses only the definition and the displayed algebraic estimate.

Comparing a term with the next term

Motivation 4.1

Suppose someone gives us a positive sequence, but its formula looks complicated. Before trying to guess its limit, let us ask a smaller question: how much of the present term is left in the next term? The fraction \(x_{n+1}/x_n\) answers exactly that question.

If, from some point onward, each term is at most three quarters of the previous term, then the terms keep shrinking. After \(k\) more steps, at most \((3/4)^k\) of that starting term remains. Similarly, if each term is at least \(3/2\) times the previous term, the terms keep growing. We do not need an exact formula for every term to see where the sequence is going. The next theorem makes this idea precise.

Theorem 4.3

The ratio criterion for a positive sequence

Let \((x_n)\) be a sequence of real numbers with \(x_n>0\) for every \(n\in\mathbb{N}\). Suppose the finite limit \[\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{x_{n+1}}{x_n}=\lambda\] exists. Then \(\lambda\ge0\), and:

  1. if \(0\le\lambda<1\), then \(x_n\to0\);

  2. if \(\lambda>1\), then \(x_n\to+\infty\).

Show proof

Proof. Every ratio \(x_{n+1}/x_n\) is positive. Its limit therefore cannot be negative. Indeed, if \(\lambda<0\), convergence with tolerance \(-\lambda/2\) would make the ratios smaller than \(\lambda/2<0\) eventually, a contradiction.

First suppose \(0\le\lambda<1\). We want a fixed multiplier which is larger than the limiting ratio but still smaller than \(1\). Choose \[r=\frac{\lambda+1}{2}.\] Then \(0<r<1\) and \(r-\lambda=(1-\lambda)/2>0\). Apply the definition of the ratio limit with tolerance \(r-\lambda\). There is \(n_0\in\mathbb{N}\) such that, for every \(n\ge n_0\), \[\left|\frac{x_{n+1}}{x_n}-\lambda\right|<r-\lambda.\] In particular, \[0<\frac{x_{n+1}}{x_n}<r, \qquad\text{so}\qquad 0<x_{n+1}<rx_n.\] This is the useful inequality. Repeating it gives \[x_{n_0+1}<rx_{n_0},\qquad x_{n_0+2}<rx_{n_0+1}<r^2x_{n_0},\qquad\ldots\] More formally, induction on \(k\) gives \[0<x_{n_0+k}\le r^k x_{n_0}\qquad(k\ge0),\] with strict inequality on the right when \(k\ge1\).

Let us also spell out why the upper bound tends to zero. Put \(t=r^{-1}-1>0\). Then \(r^k=(1+t)^{-k}\), and Bernoulli’s inequality yields \[0<x_{n_0+k}\le\frac{x_{n_0}}{1+kt}.\] Given \(\varepsilon>0\), choose an integer \(K\ge1\) with \(K>x_{n_0}/(\varepsilon t)\). For every \(k\ge K\), \[|x_{n_0+k}-0| \le\frac{x_{n_0}}{1+kt} <\frac{x_{n_0}}{kt}<\varepsilon.\] Thus \(N=n_0+K\) is a valid cutoff in the definition of \(x_n\to0\). Notice that the argument also covers \(\lambda=0\): the same choice then gives \(r=1/2\).

Now suppose \(\lambda>1\). Choose again \(r=(\lambda+1)/2\), so \(1<r<\lambda\). Use the ratio limit with tolerance \(\lambda-r>0\). For some \(n_0\), every \(n\ge n_0\) satisfies \[\frac{x_{n+1}}{x_n}>r, \qquad\text{and hence}\qquad x_{n+1}>rx_n.\] Induction now gives \[x_{n_0+k}\ge r^k x_{n_0} \ge\bigl(1+k(r-1)\bigr)x_{n_0} \qquad(k\ge0),\] where the second inequality is again Bernoulli’s inequality.

To say that \(x_n\to+\infty\) means that the terms eventually exceed every prescribed real threshold. Given \(T>0\), choose an integer \(K\ge1\) such that \[K>\frac{T}{x_{n_0}(r-1)}.\] For every \(k\ge K\), the displayed lower bound gives \(x_{n_0+k}>k(r-1)x_{n_0}>T\). Thus every term from index \(n_0+K\) onward exceeds \(T\). Nonpositive thresholds are already exceeded because every \(x_n\) is positive. This proves \(x_n\to+\infty\). ◻

Example 4.12

An exponential denominator and a factorial denominator

Find the limits of \[x_n=\frac{n}{2^n},\qquad y_n=\frac{2^n}{n!}.\]

Show worked solution

Both sequences are positive. Instead of comparing their whole formulas, compare consecutive terms. For the first sequence, \[\frac{x_{n+1}}{x_n} =\frac{n+1}{2^{n+1}}\frac{2^n}{n} =\frac12\left(1+\frac1n\right) .\] Its distance from \(1/2\) is exactly \(1/(2n)\). Given any tolerance \(\eta>0\), choosing \(N>1/(2\eta)\) makes this distance smaller than \(\eta\) whenever \(n\ge N\). Thus the ratio tends to \(1/2<1\). The ratio criterion therefore gives \(n/2^n\to0\).

For the second sequence, the cancellation is especially helpful: \[\frac{y_{n+1}}{y_n} =\frac{2^{n+1}}{(n+1)!}\frac{n!}{2^n} =\frac{2}{n+1},\] because \((n+1)!=(n+1)n!\). This ratio has distance at most \(2/n\) from zero. Given \(\eta>0\), choose \(N>2/\eta\) to prove directly that it tends to \(0<1\). Hence \(2^n/n!\to0\) as well. The additional factor \(n+1\) in the factorial eventually outweighs the additional factor \(2\) in the numerator.

Example 4.13

A polynomial factor beside geometric decay

Let \(0<y<1\) and \(x_n=ny^{n-1}\). Show that \(x_n\to0\).

Show worked solution

The terms are positive, and \[\frac{x_{n+1}}{x_n} =\frac{(n+1)y^n}{ny^{n-1}} =y\left(1+\frac1n\right).\] The difference between this ratio and \(y\) is \(y/n\). For a tolerance \(\eta>0\), choose \(N>y/\eta\). Every \(n\ge N\) then has ratio within \(\eta\) of \(y\), so the ratio tends to \(y<1\). Apply the ratio criterion to obtain \(ny^{n-1}\to0\). Although the factor \(n\) grows, its consecutive-term multiplier \(1+1/n\) tends to \(1\). The fixed multiplier \(y<1\) determines the eventual decay.

Example 4.14

Any fixed positive power against an exponential

Let \(s>0\) and \(p>0\). Show that \[x_n=\frac{n^s}{(1+p)^n}\longrightarrow0.\]

Show worked solution

First compute \[\frac{x_{n+1}}{x_n} =\frac1{1+p}\left(1+\frac1n\right)^s.\] We need to justify that \((1+1/n)^s\to1\), including when \(s\) is not an integer. Choose an integer \(M\ge s\). Since \(1+1/n>1\), the elementary order rule for positive powers gives \[1\le\left(1+\frac1n\right)^s \le\left(1+\frac1n\right)^M.\] For the integer exponent \(M\), the finite binomial formula reads \[\left(1+\frac1n\right)^M =1+\sum_{j=1}^M\binom Mj\frac1{n^j}.\] For \(n\ge1\), every \(n^{-j}\) in this finite sum is at most \(1/n\). Also \(\sum_{j=1}^M\binom Mj=2^M-1\), by putting \(1\) in the binomial formula. Therefore \[0\le\left(1+\frac1n\right)^s-1 \le\frac{2^M-1}{n}.\] In particular this gives a direct proof that \((1+1/n)^s\to1\). More specifically, the ratio itself satisfies \[\left|\frac{x_{n+1}}{x_n}-\frac1{1+p}\right| \le\frac{2^M-1}{(1+p)n}.\] Given a tolerance \(\eta>0\), choose \(N>(2^M-1)/((1+p)\eta)\). The displayed error is then less than \(\eta\) for all \(n\ge N\). Thus the ratio tends to \(1/(1+p)<1\). The ratio criterion proves the required limit. Thus an exponential with a fixed base greater than \(1\) grows faster than every fixed positive power of \(n\).

Example 4.15

Exponential growth survives a quadratic denominator

Let \(b>1\) and \(x_n=b^n/n^2\). Show that \(x_n\to+\infty\).

Show worked solution

Again the terms are positive, and \[\frac{x_{n+1}}{x_n} =\frac{b^{n+1}}{(n+1)^2}\frac{n^2}{b^n} =b\left(\frac{n}{n+1}\right)^2.\] Rather than invoking a limit rule, subtract the proposed ratio limit \(b\): \[\left|\frac{x_{n+1}}{x_n}-b\right| =b\left(1-\frac{n^2}{(n+1)^2}\right) =\frac{b(2n+1)}{(n+1)^2} \le\frac{3b}{n}.\] For every tolerance \(\eta>0\), the choice \(N>3b/\eta\) proves directly that this ratio tends to \(b>1\). The growth part of the ratio criterion therefore gives \(b^n/n^2\to+\infty\).

Warning 4.1

The theorem deliberately leaves out \(\lambda=1\). At that value, knowing the ratio limit does not determine the limit of the sequence. Consider three positive sequences: \[x_n=n,\qquad y_n=\frac1n,\qquad z_n=2+\frac1n.\] Their consecutive-term ratios all tend to \(1\): \[\left|\frac{x_{n+1}}{x_n}-1\right|=\frac1n,\qquad \left|\frac{y_{n+1}}{y_n}-1\right|=\frac1{n+1},\] and \[\left|\frac{z_{n+1}}{z_n}-1\right| =\frac{1}{n(n+1)(2+1/n)}\le\frac1{2n}.\] Each error is at most \(1/n\), so the choice \(N>1/\eta\) proves all three ratio limits directly for any tolerance \(\eta>0\). Yet \(x_n\to+\infty\), \(y_n\to0\), and \(z_n\to2\). If the ratio limit is \(1\), we must examine the sequence by another method. Throughout this section we have studied the terms of a sequence; no infinite sum is being considered.
